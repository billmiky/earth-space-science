Part 1 · Read: The Gaia Hypothesis Engage1 pt

In the 1960s, British scientist James Lovelock worked with NASA on a big question: how could we tell if Mars has life? His idea was to look at a planet's atmosphere. Mars and Venus have air that is about 95% carbon dioxide. Their gases are "settled," like a cup of soda that went flat. Earth's air is different: it has lots of oxygen mixed with small amounts of methane. Those two gases react and destroy each other, so something must keep making them. On Earth, that something is life.

You saw this in the Coevolution 5E: photosynthesis filled the air with oxygen, rusted the oceans (banded iron formations) and built the ozone layer. Life changed Earth.

Lovelock and microbiologist Lynn Margulis went one step further in the 1970s. They proposed the Gaia Hypothesis (named after the Greek goddess of the Earth):

Gaia Hypothesis: Living things and Earth's non-living systems (air, water, rock) act together as one system that regulates itself, keeping conditions like temperature and the mix of gases in a range where life can survive.

The key idea is negative feedback: when something changes, the system pushes back toward balance, like a thermostat in a house. You already met one in your packet: the weathering thermostat. When Earth warms, rocks weather faster, which pulls CO₂ out of the air and cools Earth back down.

Many scientists pushed back. Biologists Ford Doolittle and Richard Dawkins asked: how could tiny organisms "work together" to take care of a whole planet? Natural selection acts on individuals, not planets. Living things don't plan ahead. Later, paleontologist Peter Ward proposed the opposite idea, the Medea Hypothesis: life sometimes makes Earth worse for life. His example: the oxygen made by cyanobacteria poisoned many microbes and helped trigger Earth's first big ice age.

Today most scientists accept a weak version of Gaia and reject the strong version:

Weak Gaia (widely accepted)Strong Gaia (not accepted)
Life and Earth's systems affect each other through feedback loops. Some loops help keep Earth stable. (This is coevolution!) Earth is a single living organism that controls conditions on purpose to help life.

Part 2 · Graph: Daisyworld Explore4 pts

To answer his critics, Lovelock and Andrew Watson built a simple computer model in 1983 called Daisyworld. It is an imaginary planet with only two kinds of life:

⚪ White daisies reflect sunlight (high albedo). They cool the ground around them.
⚫ Black daisies absorb sunlight (low albedo). They warm the ground around them.

Both kinds grow best at about 22 °C and can't grow below 5 °C or above 40 °C. Over time, the planet's sun gets brighter, just like our real Sun, which was about 30% dimmer when Earth formed. The daisies don't "know" anything. They just grow where they can.

Part 5 · Gaia or Not? Write an Argument Evaluate4 pts

Question: Does the history of Earth and life support the idea that life helps keep Earth livable (Gaia)?

Use the evidence bank. Every item comes from your Coevolution 5E packet or this homework. You must use at least one item that supports Gaia and at least one that challenges it.

  • E1 · Weathering thermostat: warming speeds up weathering, which removes CO₂ and cools Earth. Plant roots speed it up.
  • E2 · A dim young Sun: the Sun was about 30% dimmer when Earth formed, yet rocks show liquid water on Earth for about 4 billion years. (Scientists still debate exactly why.)
  • E3 · Oxygen in a livable range: for roughly the last 400 million years, oxygen has stayed between about 15% and 35%. Today it is 21%.
  • E4 · The Great Oxidation Event (~2.4 billion years ago): oxygen from cyanobacteria was toxic to many microbes that lived without oxygen.
  • E5 · First ice age (~2.4–2.1 billion years ago): oxygen from life destroyed methane, a greenhouse gas, and Earth froze.
  • E6 · Mass extinctions: five times, 75–90% of species died, mostly from volcanoes, climate change and an asteroid. Life recovered each time, and oxygen rose again.
  • E7 · Carboniferous coal (~360–300 million years ago): buried swamp forests pushed oxygen up to about 35%, and giant insects lived.
  • E8 · Daisyworld: in the model, daisies kept the temperature near 20–25 °C over a wide range of sun brightness, until they couldn't.
