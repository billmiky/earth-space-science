The Foundation

It Starts with a Bubble

CAPE — Convective Available Potential Energy — is simply a measure of how much energy the atmosphere has stored up to launch air upward. The physics is the same as a hot air balloon: warm air is less dense than cold air, so it rises.

The question CAPE answers: if a parcel of air near the surface is forced to rise, will the atmosphere keep accelerating it upward — or pull it back down?

CAPE is measured in joules per kilogram (J/kg) — the energy per unit mass of air. It's the area of positive buoyancy on a vertical temperature profile.

Parcel Buoyancy

The Numbers

The CAPE Scale — From Calm to Violent

0–1,000 J/kg
Weak instability

Ordinary air-mass thunderstorms possible; brief heavy rain, weak lightning

1,000–2,500 J/kg
Moderate instability

Organized multicell storms; hail possible; updrafts reaching 45–70 mph

2,500–4,000 J/kg
Strong instability

Supercell territory; large hail, damaging winds, tornadoes possible

> 4,000 J/kg
Extreme instability

Violent updrafts; significant tornado risk; updraft speeds can exceed 150 mph

⚡ Reality check: The 1997 Jarrell, TX F5 tornado formed in ~7,500 J/kg CAPE. The 2011 Joplin, MO EF5 tornado had ~4,000 J/kg with extreme low-level wind shear.[1] CAPE is necessary — but as we'll see, it's never sufficient alone.

The Paradox

CIN: The Cap That
Makes Storms Violent

CIN (Convective Inhibition) is the energy barrier that prevents rising air from breaking free. A warm, stable layer aloft — called the cap — acts like a lid on a pressure cooker.

Here's the counterintuitive part: a moderate cap makes for more violent storms, not weaker ones.[2] It allows heat and moisture to build for hours in the boundary layer without releasing — then, when a trigger finally breaks the cap, the energy erupts explosively.

●Weak CIN (0–60 J/kg): Storms fire easily but remain disorganized; energy leaks before it can accumulate
●Moderate CIN (60–200 J/kg): The sweet spot — fuel builds all day, then a front or outflow boundary breaks the seal → explosive convection
●Strong CIN (>200 J/kg): The cap holds all day; storms never fire even with massive CAPE overhead

The Forecaster's Tool

Reading a Skew-T Log-P

A Skew-T diagram is the MRI of the atmosphere — a vertical snapshot from the surface to the stratosphere. The area between two lines encodes CAPE and CIN, measured in J/kg.[3]

  • Red line = environmental temperature profile (actual atmosphere)
  • Green line = lifted parcel path (how a surface air parcel cools as it rises)
  • Yellow shading = CIN area: parcel cooler than environment — must fight gravity
  • Orange shading = CAPE area: parcel warmer than environment — free ride upward
  • 🌬️ Wind barbs on the right side show speed and direction at each altitude — the key to reading wind shear

Beyond CAPE

The Four Ingredients of a Supercell

💧

Moisture

Deep boundary layer moisture — dewpoints 60°F+. Provides the latent heat that powers the updraft.

🔥

Instability

High CAPE + moderate CIN. The fuel load and the cap that allows it to build.

💨

Wind Shear

Changing wind speed and direction with altitude. Tilts the updraft and causes rotation.

⬆️

Lift / Trigger

Cold front, dryline, outflow boundary, or orographic lift — the mechanism that breaks the cap.

🌪️ The key insight: CAPE and wind shear don't just add together — they interact. High shear in a low-CAPE environment can still produce a tornado. The 0–6 km bulk shear of 40+ knots is what separates supercells from the rest.[4]

The Bigger Picture

A Warming World Is Loading the Atmosphere

More CAPE

Warmer surface temperatures increase boundary layer heat and moisture. For every 1.1°C of warming, ~8% more water vapor can be present — directly fueling potential CAPE.

10–15 More High-CAPE Days

Since 1979, parts of the Ohio Valley have seen 10–15 additional days per year with CAPE above 1,000 J/kg in spring and summer.[5] More fuel in the atmosphere.

The Shear Wildcard

Wind shear may decrease as poles warm faster than tropics — flattening the temperature gradient that drives the jet stream. More fuel, weaker organization.[6]

⚠️ The unsettled science: The models genuinely disagree on where that balance lands. This is at the edge of current understanding in climate science.

What I Keep Thinking About

CAPE tells you how much energy could be released. What decides if it actually is?

The trigger problem

Days with 4,000+ J/kg CAPE pass without a single storm. A boundary that shifts 50 miles makes the difference between nothing and an EF4. We can barely predict the boundary.[7]

The storm mode problem

Same CAPE, same shear — and one storm becomes a long-track supercell while its neighbor 20 miles away stays disorganized. What tips one into rotation and not the other?

The climate mode problem

If CAPE increases but shear decreases in a warmer world, are we heading toward more storms or just different storms? The answer affects millions of people in Tornado Alley.

Every time I open a SPC outlook and see the tornado probability hatching, I'm looking at the intersection of three uncertain ingredients — and our best models are still sometimes very wrong about which way it breaks.
