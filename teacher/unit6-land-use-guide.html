The phenomenon in one paragraph

Every summer hot weather kills hundreds of New Yorkers, and the risk is not spread evenly: the city's Heat Vulnerability Index (HVI) puts some neighborhoods at 5 (highest risk) and others at 1. Students find out why. Using the Welikia Project they see Manhattan change from forest and wetland in 1609 to pavement and buildings. Maps of zoning, tree canopy and building energy use explain why some neighborhoods run hotter than others (the urban heat island). They build a computational model of heat and then of sustainability (adding tree species as a biodiversity indicator), trace feedback loops (heat → air conditioning → more heat; trees → cooler → less energy), test a real solution (Medellín's green corridors), scale the same story up to Amazon deforestation and its tipping point, and finally add green roofs to their model for the Performance Task.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain the urban heat island: replacing forest, wetland and soil with dark pavement and buildings means more sunlight is absorbed (low albedo), less water evaporates from plants (evapotranspiration), and buildings add waste heat.
  • Use maps to relate zoning, tree cover and energy use to how much hotter a neighborhood is than its natural baseline.
  • Build and adjust a computational model (weights for each variable), test it against observed heat, and name its limits.
  • Explain why heat vulnerability is more than temperature: access to air conditioning, income, age and health also matter.
  • Draw feedback loops and say whether a change stabilizes or destabilizes the city as a habitat.
  • Calculate a rate of change for Amazon forest loss and use it to estimate when a tipping point could be reached.
  • Evaluate a solution (green corridors, green roofs) by criteria, constraints, costs, tradeoffs and equity, using model output as evidence.

Misconceptions to listen for

"The urban heat island is the same thing as global warming." It is local extra heat from land use, and it exists even without climate change. Climate change makes heat waves stronger on top of it.
"Trees cool things only by making shade." They also cool the air through evapotranspiration, the same way sweat cools skin.
"The whole city is equally hot." Map 1 shows big differences between neighborhoods a few miles apart.
"Heat vulnerability = how hot it gets." The HVI also counts social factors like AC access and income. A very hot neighborhood can still rate lower if fewer residents are at risk.
"Most heat deaths are heat stroke." Almost all are heat-exacerbated: heat worsens heart, lung and kidney disease.
"Positive feedback means good." Positive (reinforcing) means the change grows, good or bad. Heat → AC → more heat is positive feedback.
"A model that matches the map is true." A good fit means the weights work for these neighborhoods; it doesn't prove cause or include every factor.
"Deforestation only matters because of CO₂." It also heats the land locally (like a heat island), reduces rainfall and increases smoke pollution.
"We can just put Manhattan back to 1609." The change is irreversible on human time scales. We can stabilize the system, not restore it.

Key vocabulary

Urban heat island
A city area that is hotter than the natural landscape would be, because of pavement, buildings and waste heat.
Land use · zoning
How land is used (manufacturing, commercial, residential, parks); zoning is the city's rule for it.
Tree canopy cover
The percent of ground covered by tree leaves seen from above.
Albedo
How much sunlight a surface reflects. Dark asphalt has low albedo, so it absorbs heat (Unit 3 spiral).
Evapotranspiration
Water evaporating from soil and leaves, which cools the air.
Heat Vulnerability Index (HVI)
NYC's 1–5 rating of how likely people in a neighborhood are to be harmed by heat.
Biodiversity
The variety of living things in a place. Here, measured with the number of tree species.
Sustainability
Meeting today's needs without taking away future generations' ability to meet theirs.
Computational model
An equation or program that combines variables to predict an outcome; you test it against data.
Feedback loop
A change that causes changes that come back to affect the first. Reinforcing loops grow; balancing loops stabilize.
Tipping point
A threshold past which a system shifts to a new state and does not easily come back.
Green corridor · green roof
Connected strips of trees and plants along streets and waterways · plants grown on a rooftop.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a map, diagram, flowchart or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task uses something only your class has: their own slider weights, their model's scores, their group's Scenario 4 chart, the block they live on.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, ask 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Creating Computational Models

Can we write rules that predict how much hotter each neighborhood is?

Packet pp. 73–76: Part 2, Q5–16 + Tables 3 and 5PairsUrban Heat Island Effect Computational Model BuilderHeat Index Variable Descriptors · Sustainability Indicators · Tree Count Spreadsheet

By the end of today students should know

Summarize the Problem, Q1–5packet pp. 77–78

1. What was the natural resource we observed?

Land (its forests, wetlands, soil and the organisms they supported).

2. How have human population and urbanization altered that resource?

Cleared forests, filled wetlands and buried streams; covered land with dark pavement and buildings; cut tree cover and species; added energy use and waste heat. The land now absorbs more sunlight and evaporates less water, so it's hotter.

3. Are these impacts reversible?

Not fully, not on human time scales: millions of people depend on the buildings and infrastructure, natural soils and streams are gone, and many native species are lost locally. Parts can be improved (trees, parks, green roofs), which stabilizes the system without restoring it.

4. How has this alteration impacted human health?

Hotter neighborhoods mean more heat illness and more deaths, mostly from heat worsening heart and lung disease. NYC Health now estimates about 500 premature deaths a year (packet: 350). The burden is heaviest where there is less green space and less AC access.

5. Is NYC a sustainable habitat? What goes into sustainability?

Partly. Some neighborhoods (more trees, parks) are more sustainable than others. Sustainability depends on resources like clean air, water, energy and green space; biodiversity; human health; and whether future generations can still meet their needs.

Part 3: Evaluating the Modelsix prompts, in handout order · sample responses

1. How do trees create or interrupt feedback loops that stabilize or destabilize the city as a habitat?

Trees interrupt the heat → AC → heat loop by cooling the area, so less energy is used. They start stabilizing loops: trees ↔ biodiversity (habitat, soil, pollination). Removing trees lets the heating loops run unchecked.

2. Increase green cover in each neighborhood to mirror Medellín; how does it affect heat?

Heat drops in every neighborhood by (tree weight × change in tree % ÷ 10). With the Heat Index Variable Descriptors' figure (about 0.9 °F per 10% more tree cover), +20% tree cover is roughly −2 °F. The drop per 10% is the same everywhere in this model, so low-canopy neighborhoods have the most room to gain.

3. Does this change increase or decrease sustainability? Support your answer.

Increases: the score adds 0.5 per percent of tree cover (e.g., +20% → +10 points), and more species would raise the diversity term too. Students should cite their own before/after numbers.

4. Other components that might change beyond what the model shows

Lower energy use (less AC), cleaner air, less stormwater flooding, more biodiversity, more exercise and better mental health, possibly higher rents.

5. Costs or negative impacts; what might stop the city?

Planting and maintenance costs, watering, limited space (sidewalks, pipes, subway lines), roots lifting sidewalks, leaf cleanup, pollen allergies, private land, years for trees to grow, political will.

6. How might engineering green spaces with high biodiversity reduce the costs of maintaining green corridors?

Diverse plantings support pollinators, seed spread and healthy soil (the Scenario 3 loop), so plants survive with less replanting, fertilizer and pest control; one disease or pest can't wipe out the whole corridor.

  • Biodiversity and tree cover support each other, so more biodiversity also means less heat and energy use.
  • More urbanization means less tree cover, which sets off the other loops.
  • Changing one part (green space) changes the others (temperature, sustainability, energy use).
  • Total land-use change in NYC is likely irreversible, but green space can change and stabilize the system.
  • Feedback loops can stabilize or destabilize systems. All of these also work in reverse.

Lingering questions to return to: Why don't the HVI and the models match? Would this work in NYC? How much space is needed? Other ways to add green space to cities? Places besides cities? Is it happening worldwide? (Sets up Day 7.)

Deforestation of the Amazon

How are humans changing land in other areas in unsustainable ways with negative impacts to human and ecosystem health?

Handout: Deforestation of the AmazonPairs → table groupsDomino DiscoverNASA Earth Observatory · Amazon Deforestation DataCalculatorsChapter: Elaborate

By the end of today students should know

  • The Amazon stores carbon (in trees and soil), cools the air by moving water, helps make its own rain, filters pollution and holds huge biodiversity.
  • Clearing it (mostly for cattle pasture) heats the land locally, like an urban heat island, and burning adds smoke pollution.
  • About 17.5% of the Brazilian Amazon's pre-1970 forest was gone by 2019 (Mongabay table of MapBiomas/INPE data).
  • Scientists estimate a tipping point at 20–25% loss, after which reinforcing loops could turn much of it into drier savanna.
  • A rate of change lets you estimate when the threshold could be reached; solutions must weigh local benefits against costs.

Opening prompts 1–5diagram, sustainability, NASA, rate, annotation

1. Choose two interactions on the diagram; how do these loops create an ecosystem that sustains itself?

Trees → transpire water → clouds and rain → more trees. Trees → food and shelter → animals → spread seeds and pollinate → more trees. Trees → leaf litter → rich soil that stores carbon → healthier trees.

2. How does deforestation impact the sustainability of human societies around the world?

Less carbon absorbed (more climate change), hotter and drier local climate, less rain for farms and cities, more smoke and respiratory disease, loss of foods and medicines, harm to Indigenous communities who depend on the forest.

3. NASA time-lapse, July 2000 – July 2012

Clearing starts in a fishbone pattern along new roads; cleared areas (tan, light green) spread and join; solid forest breaks into fragments. NASA notes roads pushed west-northwest across Rondônia over the 12 years.

4. Quantify the rate of change, pre-1970 to 2019. What do you notice?

718,927 km² lost ÷ 49 years (1970–2019) ≈ 14,700 km²/yr (14,672). 718,927 ÷ 4,100,000 ≈ 17.5% lost; 4,100,000 − 718,927 = 3,381,073 km² left. Notice: yearly clearing was highest in the late 1980s–2000s (21,050 in 1988; 27,772 in 2004), lower in 2010 (7,000) and 2019 (9,762), but never stopped. Some students will use 1970's 4,001,600 as the start; accept it if they say so.

5. Annotate the diagram for deforestation

Trees ↓ → rain ↓, temperature ↑, soil carbon ↓ and erosion ↑, animals and pollinators ↓, fire risk ↑, smoke pollution ↑, human health ↓.

Analyze 1–4 (tipping-point calculation)sample responses and worked math

1. How does deforestation impact human health (direct and through the system)?

Direct: smoke from burning → respiratory disease; local heat (the text: up to about 8 °F warmer) → heat stress. Indirect: less rain → crop losses and water shortages; habitat loss → loss of food, medicine and income; more climate change globally.

2. How does deforestation destabilize the system?

The loops that kept the forest wet run in reverse: fewer trees → less rain and hotter → more trees die and more fires → fewer trees. Biodiversity and soil decline, so recovery is slower.

3. Why could ~25% deforestation collapse the ecosystem?

The forest makes much of its own rain. Past a threshold, too little water is recycled, the dry season lengthens, fires spread, and the rest can't sustain itself, so it shifts toward savanna. (Lovejoy & Nobre 2018 say 20–25%.)

4. How long to reach 25% deforestation?

Step 1: 75% of 4,100,000 = 3,075,000 km² remain at 25% loss. Step 2: 3,381,073 − 3,075,000 = 306,073 km² more to lose. At the 1970–2019 average (14,672 km²/yr): ≈ 21 years (about 2040). At the 2019 rate (9,762 km²/yr): ≈ 31 years (about 2050). Extension: at the 20% end, 820,000 km² lost → about 101,000 km² more → roughly 7–10 years after 2019, i.e., about now.

Benefits, costs and solutionsample responses

Benefits of deforestation for people living there

Jobs and income from cattle ranching and beef exports; cheap land (much is unowned or government land); crops, timber, mining, roads.

Costs for people living there

Local warming (up to about 8 °F, per the WRI study cited), smoke pollution and respiratory illness, less rain, loss of forest resources. Some people get the benefits while others pay the costs, and cutting ranching would not cause a food shortage (per the text).

Solution (priorities, benefits, costs, tradeoffs)

Strong answers name stakeholders (ranchers, Indigenous communities, global climate) and a specific solution: protect Indigenous-managed land, enforce limits with satellite monitoring, pay for standing forest, raise yields on already-cleared land, restrict imports of beef from deforested land. They state a cost (lost income, enforcement) and a tradeoff (short-term jobs vs. long-term rain and health).

Answer key

Step 1: Defining the Problem and Describing the SolutionPTO p. 14

Systems, human changes, how green roofs work, stabilize or destabilize

Problem: urban heat islands raise temperatures and heat deaths, especially in neighborhoods with little green space. Systems: atmosphere (air temperature), biosphere (trees, species, people), hydrosphere (rain runoff), geosphere (land surface). Human changes: replaced forest and wetland with dark roofs and pavement; added energy use and waste heat. Green roofs: plants and soil on roofs shade the surface, cool by evapotranspiration, insulate the building (less AC), hold rainwater and give habitat. They stabilize the system by weakening the heat → AC → heat loop.

Step 2: Use a Model to Analyze the Solution, Q1–4PTO pp. 14–15

1. Rewrite the "biodiversity" equation (= the sustainability formula from Explore Q15)

Sustainability = 50 + (tree types × diversity weight) + (tree % × 0.5) ± zone bonus/penalty − 4 × energy level. Heat = tree contribution + zone contribution + energy contribution.

2. How could green roofs be incorporated?

Add a term that raises sustainability and lowers heat, e.g., Sustainability + (green roof % × weight) and Heat − (green roof % ÷ 10 × weight), or count green-roof area as extra green cover. Reason: roofs act like vegetation, cooling and adding habitat.

3. How would inputs change?

Effective green/tree cover goes up; energy use may drop a range in buildings that get roofs; plant species count may rise. Zoning stays the same.

4. Results for each neighborhood

Mott Haven–Port Morris: big flat industrial roofs, little canopy, high heat and HVI → large heat drop and sustainability gain; most health benefit. Midtown–Midtown South: lots of roof area but tall buildings mean roofs cool the street less; big energy savings, sizable sustainability gain. Prospect Lefferts Gardens–Wingate: mostly residential, moderate gain. Glen Oaks–Floral Park–New Hyde Park: already greener, small pitched-roof houses → smallest change. Exact numbers depend on each student's weights.

Step 3: Evaluating the Solution + ReflectPTO p. 15

How it solves the problem · systems · each neighborhood's sustainability

Green roofs lower roof and air temperature and building energy use (the text quotes EPA via the World Economic Forum: up to about 5 °F cooler at city scale, up to 0.7% less energy demand). They help most where roofs are many and green space is scarce. At NYC's current ~0.15% coverage the effect is tiny; it only works at scale.

Other factors · comparison with green corridors

Other: less stormwater runoff and sewer overflow, habitat for pollinators and birds, longer roof life, some air filtering. Corridors cool the street where people walk and connect habitats but need street space; roofs use unused space but help pedestrians less and sit on private buildings. Best: both.

Costs, safety, environment, feedbacks

Costs: installation, structural reinforcement, maintenance, watering. Safety: roof load, leaks, worker access. Environment: mostly positive; needs water in droughts. Feedbacks: cooler → less AC → less waste heat → cooler (stabilizing); more plants → more pollinators → healthier plants. Equity: current roofs favor higher-income areas; prioritize high-HVI neighborhoods.

Reflect: local · global

Local: cooler neighborhoods, fewer heat deaths, less flooding, more biodiversity. Global: less energy use → less fossil fuel burned → less CO₂ and climate change; a model other cities can copy.
