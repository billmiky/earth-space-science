The phenomenon in one paragraph

In Earth-Sun Dynamics, students found that orbital cycles say Earth should be slowly cooling, yet the planet is warming, and the Arctic is warming almost 4 times faster than the global average. This 5E has two investigations. In Explore/Explain 1, ice cores show CO₂ and temperature rising and falling together for 800,000 years (a correlation). A carbon-cycle model shows burning fossil fuels unbalanced the cycle after the Industrial Revolution, and the Greenhouse Effect sim supplies the mechanism. Together they show human CO₂ is a cause. In Explore/Explain 2, students feel hot asphalt, run a jar-color experiment, and graph real sea ice, Greenland ice, temperature and ocean heat data. They build the ice-albedo positive feedback loop: warming melts bright ice, exposing dark ocean and land that absorb more sunlight, which causes more warming. The Elaborate adds two greenhouse-gas feedbacks (warm oceans and thawing permafrost release CO₂). In the Evaluate, students use all of it to answer climate skeptics who say "CO₂ follows temperature."

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Read a temperature anomaly graph and state that the Arctic has warmed about 3–4× faster than the globe since 1979.
  • Use ice-core data to describe the correlation between CO₂ and temperature, and the natural CO₂ range of about 170–300 ppm over 800,000 years. Compare that with today's 420+ ppm.
  • Calculate a rate of change and show that today's CO₂ rise is roughly 100× faster than the rise at the end of the last ice age.
  • Use a quantitative carbon-cycle model to show that burning fossil fuels moves carbon from the geosphere into the other reservoirs. Matter is conserved, but the system is no longer balanced.
  • Explain the greenhouse effect as a mechanism. Sunlight is absorbed, re-emitted as infrared, and greenhouse gases send some of it back. Use that mechanism to tell causation from correlation.
  • Explain albedo and the ice-albedo positive feedback loop, and use it to explain Arctic amplification.
  • Explain how warming releases CO₂ from the ocean and permafrost, so CO₂ is both a cause and an effect of warming. That answers the "CO₂ lags temperature" argument.
  • Describe positive feedbacks as destabilizing and negative feedbacks as stabilizing (CCC 7).

Corrections to the packet and teacher guide

Explain 2, Northern Hemisphere step. The teacher guide says students "should discuss that water has a higher albedo than land, because it is 'brighter'." That's backwards. Open ocean is the darkest large surface on Earth (albedo ≈ 0.06). Snow-free land is about 0.1–0.4. Glare off water only happens at low sun angles. The correct reasoning: the Northern Hemisphere has about two-thirds of Earth's land, so it has room for big continental ice sheets and seasonal snow that can grow or melt. When they melt, they expose darker ground. The Southern Hemisphere is mostly ocean, and Antarctica stays frozen year-round. So changes in northern ice change Earth's overall albedo the most.
Explain 2 summary line says the Arctic warms at "twice the rate" of the rest of the planet. Everywhere else, the 5E says "almost 4 times." Use ~4× (Rantanen et al. 2022, 1979–2021). Older sources said "twice," which is why the number is mixed up.
The Greenhouse Effect Text labels sunlight "long-wave" and infrared "short-wave." It's the reverse: incoming sunlight is short-wave (visible/UV) and Earth's outgoing heat is long-wave (infrared). Tell students before they annotate.
Burning Fossil Fuels Text says emissions reached "204 metric tons" a year by 1850. The real figure is about 200 million metric tons of CO₂ per year (Global Carbon Project).
Explain 1 key point says the Industrial Revolution "occurred in around 1850." It ran from about 1760 to 1840, starting in Britain. 1850 is when reliable global thermometer records begin, and 1850–1900 is the standard "pre-industrial" baseline. Say "since the Industrial Revolution" and use 1850 only as the baseline year.
Class Consensus "select groups" paragraphs (Explain 1 and Explain 2) were copied from Earth-Sun Dynamics. They tell you to elevate ideas about "how Earth's varying position with respect to the Sun impacts … glacial-interglacial cycles." Instead, pick groups whose work surfaces this 5E's key points (listed on Days 5 and 8).
Carbon-cycle stock tables (Explore 1, Part 2) don't conserve matter between the two eras. Fossil fuels drop by 1,000 Gt, but the other reservoirs grow by about 3,600 Gt. Real cumulative fossil emissions since 1750 are about 500 Gt C. Use the flow tables for conservation (−10 + 5 + 2.5 + 2.5 = 0), treat the stocks as illustrative, and don't ask students to check conservation with them.
Engage prep box lists the routine as Rumors, but the procedure uses Domino Discover. Use Domino Discover.
Climate Feedbacks Rubric, Cause & Effect row mentions "ocean currents," which this 5E never investigates. Accept a causal link through ice-albedo and greenhouse-gas feedbacks.

Handle the Engage with care

The Engage video is about Indigenous Arctic communities whose food, travel and homes depend on sea ice and permafrost. Frame them as experts and observers of the change, not only as victims. Some students also bring climate skepticism or anxiety from home. Name both early: "In this class we look at the evidence and decide what it supports. You'll get to test skeptics' arguments yourselves on the last day." Remind students that "positive" feedback means amplifying, not good. Close Day 10 with what people and communities are doing about it. The next 5E covers impacts on people.

Misconceptions to listen for

"CO₂ traps sunlight" / "CO₂ blocks sunlight." Sunlight passes through. CO₂ absorbs outgoing infrared from the surface and sends some back down. In the PhET sim, the yellow lines don't change.
"The greenhouse effect is the hole in the ozone layer." They're different problems. Ozone absorbs incoming UV, and the ozone hole is not the main cause of warming.
"The greenhouse effect is bad." Without it Earth would average about −18 °C. The problem is the enhanced greenhouse effect.
"Positive feedback is good for the planet." In science, positive means amplifying (more of the same change). It destabilizes the system.
"Water is shiny, so the ocean reflects sunlight." The open ocean absorbs about 94% of sunlight. It's one of the darkest surfaces on Earth.
"Burning fuel destroys carbon" / "makes new carbon." The carbon moves from the geosphere into the atmosphere. Matter is conserved.
"CO₂ followed temperature in the past, so it can't be causing warming now." CO₂ can be both an effect and a cause, which is what a feedback is. Today, CO₂ is rising first.
"The Arctic warms faster because it gets more sunlight" or "because of the ozone hole." It gets less sunlight. It warms faster because it has ice to lose (albedo feedback).
"Melting sea ice raises sea level." Floating ice doesn't. Melting land ice (Greenland, Antarctica) does. This comes up in the next 5E.
"It was cold last week, so warming isn't real." One year (or one day) is weather noise. Climate is the long-term trend.

Key vocabulary

Temperature anomaly
How much warmer or colder than a baseline average (e.g., 1951–1980). It is not the actual temperature.
Correlation vs causation
Two things change together vs one actually makes the other change. Causation needs a mechanism and empirical evidence.
ppm
Parts per million. 421 ppm CO₂ means 421 CO₂ molecules per million molecules of air.
Carbon reservoir / flow
A place carbon is stored (atmosphere, ocean, biomass, fossil fuels) / how much moves between two reservoirs each year.
Rate of change
(final − initial) ÷ time. Here, ppm per year.
Greenhouse effect
Greenhouse gases absorb infrared from Earth's surface and re-emit some back down, keeping the surface warmer.
Anthropogenic
Caused by humans.
Albedo
The fraction of sunlight a surface reflects. 0 = reflects none, 1 = reflects all.
Feedback loop
A change causes effects that come back and change the original change. Positive = amplifies (destabilizes). Negative = dampens (stabilizes).
Arctic amplification
The Arctic warming faster than the global average, mainly through the ice-albedo feedback.
Permafrost / cryosphere
Ground frozen for 2+ years / all of Earth's frozen water and ground.

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Why 10 days? The curriculum says 7–9 days, but it has two full labs (carbon cycle + PhET, and the heat-lamp jars + graphing) and two Class Consensus Discussions. At 50 minutes, 10 days runs each one in full. 8–9-day version: skip the outdoor walk and use the video (Day 6). Assign both Summary Tasks as homework. Have students make the Day 7 graphs in the student chapter (already drawn) instead of in Google Sheets. Merge Days 7 and 8.
  • 45-minute periods: cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • Do Now / Exit Slip boxes are in the Performance Task Organizer (Classes 1–6). After Class 6, students use the back of the day's handout.
  • Absent students: every packet section maps to an anchor in the student chapter, which has real NOAA/NASA/NSIDC data, the carbon model, a greenhouse model and the card sorts.
  • Lab safety (Day 6): heat lamps get very hot. Keep them clamped, away from paper edges, and unplug them before moving. Use oven mitts.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, map, graph or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has. Examples: their own jar data, the surface they stood on, their group's card sort, an idea from Domino Discover.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

CO₂ Over Time and the Carbon Cycle

How do carbon dioxide levels in the air both impact and change as a result of Earth's systems?

Packet pp. 75–79+ (Parts 1–2)Table groupsCalculatorsDomino Discover

By the end of today students should know

  • Over 800,000 years, CO₂ stayed between about 170 and 300 ppm. Today it's over 420 ppm.
  • Today's rate of rise (~0.7 ppm/yr on average since the 1800s) is about 100× faster than the end of the last ice age (~0.005–0.009 ppm/yr).

They rise and fall together: warm interglacials have high CO₂ and ice ages have low CO₂. That's a strong positive correlation.

About 170–300 ppm (lows ~170–190 in ice ages, highs ~280–300 in warm periods).

About 10,000 years (accept 8,000–18,000 depending on reading). 90 ÷ 10,000 ≈ 0.009 ppm/yr (18,000 yr gives 0.005).

Modeling the Greenhouse Effect

What impact does atmospheric carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, have on air temperature?

Part 3 + Making sense of the CO₂ and Air Temperature InvestigationTable groupsPhET Greenhouse Effect (Waves)Domino Discover1 device per pair

By the end of today students should know

  • Sunlight (yellow) passes through the atmosphere. Clouds and bright surfaces reflect some of it back to space.
  • The ground absorbs sunlight and gives off infrared (red), which is heat.
  • Greenhouse gases absorb infrared and send some back toward Earth. More greenhouse gas means more energy stays and the temperature rises, while incoming sunlight stays the same.
  • Energy changes form (light → heat) but is not created or destroyed.

Answer key

Ice Caps Cause and Effect Cards: the loopcards + mechanisms
  1. Emissions of greenhouse gases increase → atmospheric temperature rises. Mechanism: greenhouse gases absorb outgoing infrared and send some back. The emissions started with burning fossil fuels after the Industrial Revolution.
  2. Atmospheric temperature rises → Arctic ice melts. Mechanism: warmer air and ocean water heat ice above its melting point.
  3. Arctic ice melts → less solar radiation is reflected; more is absorbed, converted to heat, and re-released into the atmosphere. Mechanism: bright, high-albedo ice is replaced by dark, low-albedo ocean and land.
  4. Less radiation reflected → atmospheric temperature rises again. That closes the loop.

"Emissions increase" is the starting push, outside the loop. The other three cards form the circle.

Revisiting the Investigative Phenomenon5 required parts
What caused a change to begin in the 1800s

The Industrial Revolution. Burning fossil fuels moved carbon from the geosphere to the atmosphere, and CO₂ rose from ~280 to 420+ ppm.

How that change affected temperatures

More CO₂ strengthened the greenhouse effect, so more infrared returns to Earth. Global temperature is up ~1.2–1.3 °C.

Further impacts on Earth's systems

Arctic sea ice is shrinking (~12–13% per decade in September), Greenland is losing ice (~270 billion t/yr), and ocean heat is rising.

The role of feedback loops

The ice-albedo positive feedback: melting exposes dark surfaces, they absorb more sunlight, the air warms, and more ice melts. It repeats, so the Arctic heats much faster than places with little ice.

Rates of change since 1850

CO₂ is rising ~100× faster than at the end of the last ice age. The Arctic is warming ~0.6–0.7 °C per decade since 1979 vs ~0.2 globally (3–4×).

Class Consensus: key points to surfaceteacher guide "Take Time for These Key Points"
  • As Arctic ice melts, more dark surfaces are exposed.
  • Darker surfaces reflect less and absorb more radiation than the ice that was there.
  • They heat up faster and heat the air above them more. Warmer air melts more ice.
  • The cycle repeats, which is why the Arctic heats faster than the rest of the globe, where less ice melts.
  • Albedo increases energy leaving Earth's system by reflecting it. Melting ice decreases energy leaving, keeping it as heat.
  • It's a positive feedback loop in both directions: less ice leads to more warming and less ice; more ice leads to more cooling and more ice.
Summary Task (after Explain 2)Q1–2 (homework)
1. How does energy from the Sun affect Earth's temperature through absorption, storage and redistribution?

Sunlight is reflected by bright surfaces (ice, snow, clouds) or absorbed by darker ones (ocean, land) and turned to heat. The ocean stores most of it (ocean heat keeps rising). Heat is redistributed as the surface warms the air. Infrared radiates upward, and greenhouse gases send part of it back. Less ice means more absorbed; more greenhouse gas means less escapes.

2. How are feedback loops related to the rates of change of temperature, ice extent and CO₂?

A positive feedback speeds up change. Rising CO₂ raises temperature, which melts ice, which lowers albedo, which raises temperature more. So the changes accelerate, and they're fastest where the feedback is strongest (the Arctic).

Greenhouse Gas Feedback Mechanisms

How do greenhouse gases and albedo interact?

Greenhouse Gas Feedback Mechanisms Parts 1–2 + 2 new cardsTriads / table groupsThink-Talk-Open ExchangeDay 8 card sorts

By the end of today students should know

  • At the end of the last ice age, Antarctic temperature rose first, then CO₂, then global temperature (Shakun et al. 2012). Skeptics use this to say CO₂ is only an effect.

Q1. When temperatures increase, does atmospheric CO₂ increase or decrease?

Increase. The ocean and thawing permafrost release CO₂.

Q2. When temperatures decrease?

Decrease. Cold oceans absorb more, and frozen ground traps carbon.

The two new cards: mechanismsadded to the Day 8 sort

The ocean releases CO₂ → atmospheric temperature rises (or → "emissions of greenhouse gases increase"; accept both). Mechanism: as water warms it holds less dissolved CO₂, so the ocean releases more than it absorbs.

Part 2: Why is temperature rising so rapidly? (summary)exemplar prediction

Human emissions started the warming. Warming melts Arctic ice (lower albedo, more absorbed sunlight), warms the ocean (releasing CO₂) and thaws permafrost (releasing CO₂ and methane). Each effect feeds back into more warming, so these are positive feedback loops that destabilize the climate. Prediction: if emissions continue, the feedbacks will keep amplifying warming: less sea ice, more Greenland melt, more ocean heat, and temperatures rising faster than emissions alone would cause, fastest in the Arctic. Cutting emissions removes the push that keeps the loops going.

Revisiting the Performance Task: Climate Feedbacks 5EPTO p. 10 · Proficient exemplar

Claim: Human activities, mainly burning fossil fuels, are increasing CO₂, and that CO₂ is the main cause of today's warming. Feedback loops are amplifying it, especially in the Arctic.

Greenhouse gas levels and sources: CO₂ is over 420 ppm, far above the 800,000-year range (~170–300 ppm). About 135 ppm is from human activity. Fossil fuels add ~10 Gt C/yr (about 37 billion t CO₂), and the isotope fingerprint confirms the source.

Mechanism: CO₂ absorbs outgoing infrared and sends some back. Sunlight hasn't changed, but more energy stays in the system (sim, text, energy budget).

Ice caps today and their effect: September Arctic sea ice is down ~40% since 1979, and Greenland is losing ~270 billion t/yr. Exposed dark ocean and land absorb more sunlight, warming the Arctic ~3–4× faster than the globe.

Albedo and feedbacks: ice-albedo, ocean CO₂ release and permafrost thaw are positive feedbacks that amplify warming.

Answer to skeptics: in the past CO₂ sometimes followed temperature because it acted as a feedback. Today CO₂ rose first, because humans put it there, and the rate (~100× faster) is beyond any natural change in the record.
