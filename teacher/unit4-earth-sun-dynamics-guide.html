Earth–Sun Dynamics

What factors have contributed to climate change in the past, and are they contributing to climate change now?

PE HS-ESS2-4 Length 8 days × 50 min (teacher materials list 5) Packet Student Materials pp. 40–71 · PT prompt p. 9 Routines Domino Discover · Class Consensus Discussion
1Engage 2Explore 3Explore → Explain 4Explain 5Explain 6Explain 7Elaborate 8Evaluate

Tap a day to open it. Each day has a minute-by-minute plan, Do Nows, conferring questions, checks for understanding, the answer key, exit slips and drawn homework. Student-facing version of everything: Unit 4: Earth-Sun Dynamics chapter.

The phenomenon in one paragraph

The amount of July sunlight reaching 65° N has gone up and down over the last 750,000+ years, and big warm periods (interglacials) show up about every 100,000 years. Students ask whether natural cycles could explain that, and whether they explain today's warming. With a flashlight and a ball they find that three slow changes in Earth's position, the shape of the orbit (eccentricity), the degree of tilt (obliquity) and the direction of tilt (precession), change mostly where and when sunlight lands, not how much Earth gets in total. Plugging real orbital data for three past shifts into their models, they predict stronger or weaker northern summers and see the predictions match the ice-core record: strong summers melt ice sheets, weak summers let them grow. A computational model (Vostok/EPICA vs. Milankovitch curves) shows a strong correlation plus a mechanism, though not a perfect match. They then rule out the Sun: the 11-year solar cycle is far too short and too small. Finally they show that since 9,000 years ago the orbit has been pushing toward cooler northern summers and the Sun has been flat since ~1960, yet temperature has risen ~1.2 °C in ~140 years. Natural cycles can't be the cause, which sets up greenhouse gases in the next 5E.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain why scientists focus on summer sunlight at 65° N: that is where Northern Hemisphere ice sheets grow, and summer melt decides whether winter snow survives.
  • Distinguish correlation (rise and fall together) from causation (a mechanism plus ruling out other causes).
  • Describe each orbital factor, its range and cycle length: eccentricity ~100,000 yr; tilt 22.1°–24.5°, ~41,000 yr; direction of tilt ~23,000 yr for NH summer to line up with the closest point (axis wobble ~26,000 yr).
  • Use a model to show that eccentricity changes the total amount of sunlight (a little), while degree and direction of tilt change its distribution (angle and concentration at 65° N).
  • Use orbital data to predict stronger or weaker NH summers, then whether ice sheets grow (glacial) or shrink (interglacial), and check the prediction against the record.
  • Compare time scales: solar cycle ~11 yr vs. glacial cycles ~100,000 yr vs. today's warming ~140 yr.
  • Argue from multiple lines of evidence that orbital factors and solar output are not causing today's warming.

Misconceptions to listen for

"Summer happens when Earth is closest to the Sun." Earth is closest in early January. Seasons come from tilt. (Revisit the optional Seasons 3E if this is widespread.)
"Tilt changes how much sunlight Earth gets in total." Tilt and its direction move sunlight around (distribution); only distance changes the total, and only by a few percent.
"Ice ages happen because winters get colder." Cold winters bring snow either way. What matters is whether summers at 65° N are warm enough to melt it.
"If two graphs line up, one causes the other." That is correlation. Causation needs a mechanism and ruling out other causes (this is why the Sun gets tested on Day 7).
"Milankovitch cycles explain the ice-age record perfectly." They set the timing, but the match is imperfect; ice, ocean and CO₂ feedbacks amplify it (next 5E).
"Sunspots make the Sun dimmer, so more sunspots = less energy." Sunspots are dark, but an active Sun with more sunspots gives off slightly more total energy.
"Earth is warming because we're coming out of the last ice age." That warming ended ~10,000 years ago; since then the orbit has slowly favored cooling.
"Orbital changes could happen fast enough to matter in 100 years." They act over thousands to hundreds of thousands of years.

Key vocabulary

Insolation
Incoming solar radiation: the sunlight energy reaching a place (W/m²).
Glacial / interglacial
Long cold periods when ice sheets spread (ice ages) / shorter warm periods like today.
Eccentricity
How oval Earth's orbit is. Changes the distance to the Sun, so the total sunlight.
Obliquity (degree of tilt)
The angle of Earth's axis, 22.1°–24.5°. More tilt = stronger summers at high latitudes.
Precession (direction of tilt)
The slow wobble that changes whether NH summer happens near the closest or farthest point of the orbit.
Perihelion / aphelion
Closest / farthest point of Earth's orbit (today ~Jan 3 / ~Jul 4).
Milankovitch cycles
The combined orbital cycles that change the seasonal and latitudinal pattern of sunlight.
Correlation vs. causation
Changing together vs. one thing making the other happen (needs a mechanism).
Sunspot / solar cycle
Dark magnetic patches on the Sun; their number rises and falls every ~11 years with solar output.
Total solar irradiance (TSI)
The Sun's energy arriving at the top of Earth's atmosphere, ~1,361 W/m².

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Why 8 days? The SY 25-26 teacher materials list 5 days (the earlier edition said 6–7, with 90 lab minutes). At 50 minutes, the three Part 1 shifts, Part 2 and the first Class Consensus Discussion of the unit each need real time.
  • Six-day version. Do the Part 1 shift 130 → 113 kya as a jigsaw (each group does one shift and teaches it on the poster), assign the Part 2 practice questions and the Summary Task as homework, and do the Solar Cycles questions in pairs while you confer.
  • Do Now / Exit Slip boxes. The Google Doc version of the packet has boxes for Classes 1–6; the printed Unit 4 Student Materials PDF has none. Use a half-sheet or notebook page, and on Days 7–8 the blank p. 72 / back of p. 71.
  • Period length. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • Device-light option. Days 2 and 5 can run from a single projector: project the factor animations and the Vostok model and click through as a class while groups record.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, timeline, flowchart or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has: their flashlight observations, their group's poster, the time window they picked on the Vostok graph, the class energy budget chart.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Radiation and Temperature During Earth's Past

How has the amount of radiation (sunlight) reaching Earth varied in the past?

Packet pp. 41–42: Radiation and Temperature During Earth's PastPairsDomino DiscoverMap of Greenland · NH Ice Coverage videoChapter: Engage

By the end of today students should know

  • Ice cores, tree rings and ocean sediments let scientists reconstruct temperature back ~800,000 years; orbital math gives past sunlight.
  • July sunlight at 65° N rises and falls in a repeating but irregular pattern; interglacials come about every 100,000 years.
  • Why 65° N in summer: it is where NH ice sheets grow, and summer melt decides whether they grow or shrink.
  • High sunlight and interglacials line up: that is a correlation. Claiming causation needs more evidence.

Minute by minute 50 min

  1. 0–5

    Do Now

    Class 1 box. Pick an option below.

  2. 5–9

    Connect to the DQB

    Point to the Driving Question Board questions about natural causes: How do we know climate change isn't happening because of natural cycles? Has the sunlight reaching Earth varied? Does the Sun's output vary?

    "Scientists have pulled temperature records out of ice cores, tree rings and ocean mud going back 800,000 years, and they've used math models of Earth's orbit to work out how much sunlight arrived over that time. Let's see what that looks like."

  3. 9–16

    Map + ice video

    Show Greenland on the map (one place ice cores come from). Play Northern Hemisphere Ice Coverage Over 400,000 Years. Ask: What do you notice about the ice over time? Listen for "it grows and shrinks in a pattern." Point out that the ice edge reached New York City; Long Island is the pile of rock it left behind.

  4. 16–20

    Why 65° N in summer?

    Ice sheets form on land at high northern latitudes (Canada, Scandinavia, Siberia). Snow falls every winter; whether it survives depends on summer melt. Scientists found a strong correlation between summer sunlight at 65° N and glacial cycles.

  5. 20–32

    See-Think-Wonder (pairs)

    Display the graph. Pairs complete both rows of the See-Think-Wonder. Confer (questions below). Tip: have them put a finger on each interglacial peak and count.

  6. 32–40

    Domino Discover

    Each pair picks its most important observation, idea and question. Go pair by pair; each builds on the last. Chart the ideas on a poster titled "How has Earth's temperature varied in the past?" First Domino Discover of the unit, so model it.

  7. 40–45

    Correlation vs. causation

    Ask: Do you know the difference between "correlation" and "causation"? Define both. Then ask: What evidence would we need to say sunlight at 65° N causes glacial cycles? (A mechanism; a model that predicts the pattern; rule out other causes.)

  8. 45–50

    Explore launch + exit slip

    Ask: What about Earth's position compared to the Sun could change? Chart ideas: distance, tilt, rotation, the Sun's own output. "Next class you'll model three of these."

Do Now options

Option A · Unit recall

In the unit launch we saw Earth's temperature has changed a lot in the past. Name one natural thing you think could cause Earth to warm or cool.

Expect: the Sun, distance from the Sun, tilt, volcanoes, ocean currents. Save them for the Explore launch.
Option B · Seasons check

True or false: it's summer in New York because Earth is closest to the Sun in July. Explain.

False. Earth is closest in early January. Summer comes from the NH tilting toward the Sun (higher Sun, longer days).
Option C · Ice

Snow falls every winter in northern Canada. What has to happen for a giant ice sheet to build up there over thousands of years?

Some of each winter's snow must survive the summer, so summers have to be cool.

  • Are there sunlight peaks where temperature doesn't jump? What might that mean?Plants the "imperfect match" idea for Day 5.
  • What could change how much sunlight reaches 65° N without the Sun changing at all?
  • Do these two graphs prove one causes the other? What else would you want to know?

Orbital Factors Investigation

How does Earth's position affect the amount of radiation reaching Earth's surface?

Packet pp. 43–48: Orbital Factors InvestigationGroups of 2–3Flashlight · 6-in sphere · markers3 factor animationsChapter: virtual flashlight

By the end of today students should know

  • Three things change slowly: direction of tilt (~23,000–26,000 yr), degree of tilt (22.1°–24.5°, ~41,000 yr), shape of orbit (~100,000 yr).
  • Changing the tilt (direction or degree) does not change the total sunlight Earth gets, but it changes how concentrated the beam is at 65° N.
  • Changing the distance changes the total sunlight (closer = more), everywhere.

Minute by minute 50 min

  1. 0–4

    Do Now

    Class 2 box. Call on 3–4 students to explain their "New York Under Ice" maps.

  2. 4–8

    Launch

    Revisit yesterday's list of things about Earth's position that could change. Listen for: distance from the Sun, tilt, rotation, Sun's output.

    "You'll model three of these and collect evidence: does each one change the total sunlight Earth gets, and does it change the sunlight at 65° N?"

  3. 8–14

    Model demo (whole class)

    Show the setup: flashlight = Sun, held level and at a fixed distance; sphere = Earth with the 65° N line marked. Point out what to look at: the size of the lit spot at 65° N (smaller, brighter = concentrated) and how much of the ball is lit (total).

  4. 14–26

    Factor 1: direction of tilt

    Watch the animation; draw how it changes; answer "how long is a full cycle?" Model NH tilted toward vs. away from the flashlight. Q1–2.

  5. 26–37

    Factor 2: degree of tilt

    Same steps: less tilt vs. more tilt toward the flashlight. Q1–2. Differentiation point: if a group is stuck, pause the class for 1 minute and have a group show how it is modeling.

  6. 37–47

    Factor 3: eccentricity

    Same steps: move the sphere closer to and farther from the flashlight without changing the tilt. Q1–2.

  7. 47–50

    Exit slip

Do Now options

Option A · Angle

Shine a flashlight straight down on a desk, then at a slant. Which spot is brighter, and why?

Straight down: the same light lands on a smaller area, so it's more concentrated.
Option B · Recall

About 23.4°. Seasons.
Option C · Predict

  • What are the two extremes of this cycle? Show me both with your model.
  • Does this change affect the total amount of radiation reaching Earth? How can you tell from the ball?
  • Does it affect every place on Earth the same way, or some places more than others?
  • How would you model Earth farther from the Sun vs. closer? What do you see change?Differentiation prompt from the teacher materials.
  • How could you measure the difference in how much flashlight reaches the ball's surface?Counting squares lit at 65° N, or tracing the spot.

Making Sense: See-Think-Wonder + Analysisp. 49 · See-Think-Wonder rows + Analysis Q1
Direction of tilt

See: tilting NH toward the light made the 65° N spot small and bright; away made it spread out. Think: the direction of tilt goes through a ~26,000-yr cycle; at one extreme NH is tilted toward the Sun when Earth is closest, at the other away. This changes angle and concentration at 65° N.

Degree of tilt

See: more tilt = more concentrated summer beam at 65° N. Think: tilt goes through a ~41,000-yr cycle between ~22.1° and 24.5°, changing the angle and concentration of sunlight at different latitudes, including 65° N.

Eccentricity

See: closer = whole sphere brighter. Think: the orbit goes through a ~100,000-yr cycle between nearly circular and more oval, changing how close Earth is at different times of year. Closer = more radiation, possibly higher temperatures.

Summary Taskpp. 63–64 · reflection 1–4 (no key) · content Q1–3
1. Orbital shape, degree and direction of tilt → energy → ice sheets

Eccentricity changes Earth's distance from the Sun, so it changes the amount of energy. Degree and direction of tilt change the distribution: how directly sunlight hits high latitudes, and whether NH summer happens when Earth is closer or farther. Toward a glacial period: very slow cooling over thousands of years.

Claim: Orbital factors and solar cycles are not causing today's warming or the melting of ice caps.

Orbit: Since 9,000 years ago the direction of tilt flipped (NH summer is now at the far point, 152.14 M km) and tilt fell from 24.25° to 23.4° and is still decreasing. Our models show weaker NH summers let ice grow, a slow move toward a glacial. That matches the slow cooling over thousands of years before the 1800s. In the near future these factors keep changing only slowly (tilt keeps decreasing for ~10,000 years), so they can't cause warming, let alone fast warming.

Sun: Output rises and falls every ~11 years by <0.1%, and since ~1960 its average has been flat or slightly lower, while temperature rose ~1 °C. They are not correlated now.

Rate: Natural 1.25° rises took ~1,700–5,000 years; today's took ~140 years. Orbital changes act over tens of thousands of years.

Cause and effect: Orbital factors are correlated with glacial-interglacial cycles and have a mechanism, so they are a likely cause of past ice ages. Today they point toward cooling, so they cannot cause current warming. Something else must be adding energy.

Developing usually means: only one factor discussed; no "near future"; no mention of amount vs. distribution; or "the graphs match" with no correlation/causation language.

Kitchen-Table Rebuttal
