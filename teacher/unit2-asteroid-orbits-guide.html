Asteroid Orbits

Will a major asteroid ever hit the Earth again? How do we know, and what can humans do to survive?

The phenomenon in one paragraph

Apophis is an asteroid about 340 m wide (the packet says 300 m), roughly three football fields. On April 13, 2029 it will pass about 32,000 km above Earth's surface. That's closer than the TV and weather satellites in geostationary orbit, and close enough to see without a telescope. Nothing that big has come so close in recorded history. How can NASA know exactly where a 340 m rock will be years from now? Students rediscover the patterns in orbits. Orbits are ellipses (Kepler's 1st law). Objects speed up near the Sun and slow down far from it, sweeping equal areas in equal times (Kepler's 2nd law). The period depends on the average distance (Kepler's 3rd law). Gravity explains all of it: Newton's F = Gm₁m₂/r². Because those patterns are so stable, orbits can be predicted precisely. Gravity can also change an orbit, which is both the worry (Earth's pull on Apophis in 2029) and the solution (a gravity tractor or a kinetic impactor to nudge a dangerous asteroid).

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Kepler's 1st law: every orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. Eccentricity 0 is a circle; planets are near 0; comets and many asteroids are much more stretched.
  • Kepler's 2nd law: an orbiting object sweeps equal areas in equal times, so it moves fastest when closest to the Sun and slowest when farthest.
  • Kepler's 3rd law: the period depends on the average distance: P² = a³ (years, AU). Farther objects take longer to orbit.
  • Newton's law of gravity: F = G·m₁m₂/r². Force grows with mass and falls off with the square of distance. Double the distance and the force drops to ¼.
  • Explain why Kepler's 2nd law works: closer to the Sun, gravity is stronger and speeds the object up; farther away, it pulls back and slows it down.
  • Explain that orbits are stable and predictable, which is how NASA predicts Apophis. They can change through gravity from other bodies (like Earth's pull during a flyby) or through collisions.
  • Argue for a way to deflect a dangerous asteroid (gravity tractor or kinetic impactor) using the math of gravity and the importance of early warning.

Corrections to the curriculum

Flyby date. The teacher materials' overview says April 23, 2029 at around 4:30 PM EST. The student packet (p. 14) says April 13, which is correct. Closest approach is about 5:46 PM EDT (21:46 UTC) on April 13, 2029.
Kepler's 2nd law model (p. 10). The packet writes r = Cw ("distance = constant × orbital velocity"). That would mean the farther the object, the faster it goes, which is the opposite of what students observe. The correct relationship (at closest and farthest points) is r × v = C, or v = C / r: smaller distance, larger speed. Have students fix it before Day 4, and use it as a "does this model match our data?" moment.
The 2030s impact framing (Elaborate, p. 16) is out of date. In 2004, a small chance of a 2029 hit or a later "keyhole" return (2036, 2068) worried scientists. Radar tracking in 2021 ruled out any impact for at least 100 years. Keep the task, since the mechanism of how Earth's gravity bends Apophis's path is real and important. Have students conclude that the risk is now known to be very low because precise tracking and these math models let NASA check.
Example sweep image (p. 11). The pictured sweeps (0.0479 yr, 0.104 AU²) come from different settings than the directions on p. 6 (e = 0.7, a = 3 AU). Students' numbers won't match the picture. That's fine; what matters is that the two values match each other.

Misconceptions to listen for

"There's no gravity in space." Gravity reaches everywhere, getting weaker with distance. It's what keeps everything in orbit.
"Orbits are circles, and planets move at a constant speed." Orbits are ellipses, and speed changes all the way around.
"An object goes faster when it's farther away because it has more distance to cover." It's the reverse (the r = Cw error in the packet). It's slowest when farthest.
"Heavier objects orbit faster." An orbiting object's mass barely matters. The Sun's mass and the distance set the speed.
"Apophis is going to hit us in 2029." It will miss by ~32,000 km. Make this clear on Day 1 to head off anxiety.
"Just blow it up." Fragments could still hit. Real plans nudge the orbit years in advance.
"A gravity tractor pulls with a cable or tractor beam." It uses only the spacecraft's own gravity: a tiny force, applied for years.
"If we know an orbit now, we know it forever." Gravity from planets (and even sunlight warming the asteroid) slowly changes orbits, so they must keep being tracked.

Key vocabulary

Orbit / trajectory
The path an object follows through space.
Ellipse
A stretched circle with two focus points. The Sun sits at one focus.
Eccentricity
How stretched an ellipse is: 0 = circle, near 1 = very stretched.
Semimajor axis (a)
Half the long axis of the ellipse; the object's average distance from the Sun.
AU (astronomical unit)
Earth's average distance from the Sun, about 150 million km.
Perihelion / aphelion
Closest point to the Sun / farthest point from the Sun.
Period (P)
Time for one full orbit.
Near-Earth object (NEO)
An asteroid or comet whose orbit brings it close to Earth's orbit.
Gravitational keyhole
A small region near Earth. If an asteroid passes through it, Earth's gravity bends its orbit onto a future collision course.
Gravity tractor / kinetic impactor
Deflect an asteroid by hovering a spacecraft near it (gravity) or by crashing into it (collision).

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Period length. Every plan assumes 50 minutes. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • Do Now / Exit Slip boxes. The packet has boxes for Classes 1–6. On Day 7, students write on the back of the performance-task handout.
  • Five- or six-day version. Combine Days 4 and 5 by giving the Apophis model (p. 14) as homework and holding a shorter consensus discussion. For five days, also fold Day 6's Read-Generate-Sort-Solve into the start of Day 7.
  • Sims need devices. Days 2–3 need one device per pair. Without them, project each sim and have pairs predict before you click.
  • Math support. Some students will be nervous about the equations. Keep the focus on direction ("if r goes up, what happens to F?") before plugging in numbers, which is also what the rubric's "Scale, Proportion and Quantity" row asks for.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, map, graph or comic, often drawn to scale with a ruler or string. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper, pencil and ruler only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has, or a number the student chooses, so no two drawings match.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Answer key

Part 1: video questionsp. 3 · check against the video you show
1a. Where do asteroids in our solar system come from?

Mostly a huge reservoir between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter, the asteroid belt, with perhaps about a billion objects. Gravity from Jupiter and Saturn nudges some of them closer to Earth.

1b. How many large asteroids are there?

Millions of objects at least 50 m wide, which is big enough to destroy a city.

1c. Will another large asteroid ever strike Earth?

Eventually, yes, it's possible. Even a small ~17 m asteroid exploded in the atmosphere and injured over a thousand people (Chelyabinsk, 2013). NASA tracks these objects closely. If one were found on a collision course, we would probably have a few years of warning to act, for example by crashing a spacecraft into it to knock it off course.

2. Questions?

Answers vary. Good ones: "Is NASA especially worried about any asteroid?" · "How do they know where it will go?" · "How do you change an asteroid's path?"

Answer key

Part 1: Motion of Asteroids and Cometspp. 5–6
1. Change eccentricity. What happens?

The orbit's shape changes. At low eccentricity it's nearly a circle. As eccentricity increases, the ellipse gets more stretched and flattened, and the Sun ends up closer to one end.

2. What shape is e = 0.0?

A circle. The distance from the Sun is the same all the way around. Most planets are close to this (Earth's e ≈ 0.017).

3. What shape is e = 0.7? Same distance from the Sun?

A long, stretched ellipse. No: the object gets very close to the Sun at one end (perihelion) and much farther at the other (aphelion).

Sweeps 1. What do you notice?

Every sweep has the same area and takes the same amount of time, but the shapes differ. Near the Sun the slice is short and fat and covers a long stretch of orbit; far away it's long and skinny and covers a short stretch.

Expected numbers (a = 3 AU, e = 0.7, sweep = 1/16 of an orbit): each sweep ≈ 0.32 yr and ≈ 1.26 AU² (total orbit ≈ 5.2 yr and ≈ 20.2 AU²). Values depend on the exact settings. The key is that the two sweeps match each other.
Sweeps 2. What does this tell you about speed near and far?

Because it covers more orbit in the same time, the object moves fastest when closest to the Sun and slowest when farthest. That's Kepler's 2nd law: equal areas in equal times.

On an elliptical orbit, the object is fastest where…

  • A. it's closest to the Sun and gravity is strongestCorrect.

Answer key

Part 2: The Force of Gravity Q1–4pp. 6–7
1. Distance constant, change the mass?

More mass on either object means more gravitational force; less mass means less. It's proportional: double one mass and the force doubles.

2. Mass constant, change the distance?

Farther apart means weaker force; closer means stronger. It falls off quickly: double the distance and the force drops to about ¼.

3. Increase the mass of the Earth or Sun?

The gravity force arrow gets longer (stronger pull). With a heavier Sun, Earth is pulled in harder, so its orbit gets tighter or more stretched unless its speed changes. Make one mass big enough and the path can curve sharply or crash.

4a. Change in gravity over the ellipse?

Gravity is strongest at the closest point to the Sun and weakest at the farthest point, changing smoothly in between.

4b. Change in velocity over the ellipse?

The velocity arrow is longest (fastest) at the closest point and shortest (slowest) at the farthest. The object speeds up as it falls inward and slows down as it climbs back out, which matches Kepler's 2nd law.

Orbits as Mathematical Models

How do we predict the motions of asteroids and planets?

Packet pp. 10–13 (Parts 1–2)Pairs / table groupsCalculatorsFix the r = Cw model first

By the end of today students should know

  • A/t = C: the area swept per unit of time is constant (Kepler's 2nd law).

Answer key

Part 1: Kepler's 2nd law verbal models + Q2pp. 10–11
A/t = C: verbal model

The area an object sweeps out, divided by the time it takes, is always the same number. Equal areas are swept in equal amounts of time, anywhere in the orbit.

r = Cw (corrected to r × v = C): verbal model

The distance from the Sun times the orbital speed stays constant, so distance and speed are inversely related. When the object is closer (small r), it moves faster (big v); when it's farther, it moves slower.

If a student writes the packet's version literally ("distance equals a constant times speed, so they go up together"), they've read the equation correctly but the equation is wrong. Praise the reading, then ask whether it matches the sim.
2. How does the right-hand model explain equal time for a longer distance near the Sun?

Near the Sun, r is small, so v must be large to keep r × v constant. Moving faster, the object covers a longer stretch of orbit in the same time. The near sweep is short but wide and the far sweep is long but narrow, so their areas come out equal: 0.104 AU² in 0.0479 years for both in the example.

Part 1: Newton's law Q3–4pp. 11–12
3. Verbal model of F = G·m₁m₂/r²

The force of gravity between two objects equals a constant times their two masses multiplied, divided by the distance between their centers squared. More mass means more force; more distance means much less force: double the distance and the force is ¼.

4. Why do objects move faster closer to their star?

When an object is closer, r is smaller, so the force of gravity is much larger (it depends on 1/r²). As the object falls inward, that stronger pull accelerates it, so it speeds up. As it moves away, gravity pulls backward on it and slows it down. So gravity is the cause of Kepler's 2nd law pattern.

Part 2: Predicting Orbital Motion Q1–4p. 13
1. How long to travel around the Sun?

Kepler's 3rd law: P² = a³. If we know the average distance (semimajor axis) in AU, we can calculate the period in years. Apophis: a ≈ 0.92 AU, so P ≈ 0.88 yr ≈ 323 days.

2. How fast is it traveling?

Kepler's 2nd law (equal areas in equal times; r × v = constant): if we know where it is in its orbit, we know whether it's fast (near the Sun) or slow (far). Newton's law tells us the force changing its speed.

3. What path does it take?

Kepler's 1st law: it follows an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. Repeated telescope and radar observations of its positions let scientists pin down the exact ellipse (its size, eccentricity and tilt).

4. What's missing? Do we know why?

Kepler's laws describe what orbits do but don't explain why. The why is gravity (Newton's law): the Sun's pull curves the path into an ellipse and changes the speed. Kepler's laws also only consider the Sun. Gravity from other objects, like Earth during a close flyby, can change an orbit, so predictions must include those pulls too.

Summary Task Q5p. 15
5. How are Kepler's laws used to predict whether Apophis will orbit near Earth?

Scientists observe Apophis's position many times to find its orbit. Kepler's 1st law tells them its path is an ellipse that crosses Earth's orbit. Kepler's 3rd law (P² = a³) gives its period, about 0.89 years, so they can count forward orbit by orbit to find when Apophis and Earth will be at the same place at the same time. Kepler's 2nd law tells them how fast it moves at each point, so they know exactly where along the ellipse it will be on a given date. Orbits follow this stable pattern, which is why NASA can predict that on April 13, 2029, Apophis will pass about 32,000 km above Earth and miss. Gravity explains all three laws, and Earth's gravity during the flyby must also be included.
