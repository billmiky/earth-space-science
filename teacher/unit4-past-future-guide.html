The Past and the Future

Have humans experienced dramatic climate change in the past? How have human populations been impacted, and how can that inform how we might be impacted in the future?

The phenomenon in one paragraph

Students already know what is warming the planet (Earth–Sun dynamics, feedbacks, CO₂). This 5E asks so what? They start in the past. As Earth left the last glacial period, Greenland's ice-core record (GISP2) shows warming, then a sudden plunge back to near-glacial cold (the Younger Dryas, about 12,900–11,700 years ago), then a very fast rebound. The Clovis culture disappears from the record at the start of that swing. A tank model shows the mechanism: fresh meltwater lowers the salt concentration of the North Atlantic, the water becomes less dense, sinking slows, and the AMOC weakens, so less tropical heat reaches the north. Then students turn to the future. Satellite data show sea level rising about 3 mm per year since 1993, and speeding up. IPCC climate models project more warming, more sea level rise and more extremes under higher-emission scenarios. Real texts and data (glacial lake floods, eroding Alaska villages, crop losses, NOAA billion-dollar disasters) show that people are already being affected, which feeds the unit Performance Task.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Describe how past climate change affected people: migrations, die-offs, land bridges exposed by low sea level, and the disappearance of the Clovis culture during the Younger Dryas.
  • Explain that melting ice sheets add fresh water to the ocean, which raises sea level (~120 m since the last glacial maximum) and lowers salt concentration and density.
  • Use a model to give a mechanism: fresh water → less dense → less sinking near Greenland → weaker AMOC → less heat carried north → northern cooling.
  • Explain why water moves so much energy (high heat capacity; heat moves from warm water to cooler air) and why warm currents give coasts milder climates.
  • Calculate a rate of change from real sea level data (≈3 mm/yr, 1993–2025) and explain why a straight-line forecast underestimates the future (the rate is accelerating).
  • Use IPCC scenarios to state that more emissions → more warming → bigger and faster changes in sea level, heat and precipitation.
  • Construct an evidence-based claim about how climate hazards will likely affect people (floods, displacement, food and water supply), and say whether the evidence shows cause or only correlation.

Corrections to the packet and teacher guide

Explore 1 brine: the teacher guide recommends 400 g/L NaCl. Table salt only dissolves to about 360 g per liter of water (≈26% by mass), and a bit less when cold, so 400 g/L leaves a pile of undissolved salt. Use 300–350 g/L or simply a saturated solution.
Explore 2 Look & Listen For: "annual change is 3.0125 mm/year," "50 years: 150.625 mm," "100 years: 301.25 mm." Using the packet's own formula, (91.6 − (−5.6)) ÷ (2025 − 1993) = 97.2 ÷ 32 = 3.04 mm/yr, so ≈152 mm in 50 years and ≈304 mm in 100. (3.0125 is 96.4 ÷ 32, the October-to-October difference divided by the wrong number of years.) Still about 6 and 12 inches.
Explore 2 monthly rate: the packet divides the 14.27 mm seasonal change by 7 months to get "2 mm/month." May to October is 5 months, which gives ≈2.9 mm/month. Accept either if students follow the packet formula, but point out the mismatch. The student chapter shows the packet's ÷ 7.
Explore 2 scenario temperatures: the guide says SSP warming of 1.8, 2.7, 3.6 and 4.4 °C "by 2080." Those are IPCC AR6 best estimates for 2081–2100, relative to 1850–1900.
Explore 2 sea level tool: the guide says to "record the projected temperature rise under each condition." Students record the projected sea level rise (m) and rate (m/yr), as the packet table asks. The guide also calls the first tool the "Sea Level Projection Tool"; the packet links the BRGM Sea Level Rise Scenarios map. Either works (NASA's IPCC AR6 Sea Level Projection Tool gives the same AR6 numbers).
Explain 1 key point: "the AMOC current clouds have slowed" → "could have slowed."
Explain 1 key point: "warm air built up in the southern hemisphere and began to move North, temperatures rose very quickly." That is a simplification. Heat did build up in the south while the AMOC was weak (the "bipolar seesaw"), but the abrupt warming at the end of the Younger Dryas is mainly explained by the AMOC restarting and carrying ocean heat north again. Teach both pieces. The Clovis card "Energy circulation of the ocean stopped" also overstates it: the ocean kept circulating, and northward heat transport in the North Atlantic was greatly reduced.
Explain 1 & Explain 2 Class Consensus: the guide says to pick explanations that build understanding of "how Earth's varying position with respect to the Sun impacts … glacial-interglacial cycles" (copied from the Earth–Sun Dynamics 5E). Here, pick models about currents redistributing energy (Explain 1) and impacts of melting ice on people (Explain 2). The Explain 1 "Integrating Three Dimensions" note about "conservation of carbon" and "sudden warming at the end" should read energy redistribution and the sudden cooling at the start of the Younger Dryas.
Explain 2 chart paper title: the guide reuses "How Changes in Ocean Currents Impacts Climate." Title it "Class Consensus: How Will Melting Ice Impact Populations?"
Evaluate materials / Performance Task Organizer p. 11: the PTO page for this 5E is headed "Revisiting the Performance Task: Greenhouse Gases 5E" (there is no Greenhouse Gases 5E in Unit 4), and the teacher guide lists it under that name plus an "unknown material" handout. It is the right page: its prompt ("projected temperature changes, other projected climate changes … how human populations will likely be impacted by climate related hazards") is this 5E's. Tell students to write "The Past and the Future" over the heading.
Blue Marble timestamp: the teacher guide says stop at 1:32; the student packet says 1:36. Either is fine; the packet's 1:36 finishes the sentence.
Rubric, "Developing" column: it says "The model is incomplete in showing the current levels of carbon dioxide …" (copied from the Greenhouse Gases rubric). Read it as: the explanation is missing one or more of the five components.
Text 3 (NRDC): "Thwaites Glacier … may very well shatter in as little as five years." The 2021 warning was about Thwaites' eastern ice shelf, the floating front. Losing the shelf speeds up the glacier; the glacier itself (≈65 cm of potential sea level rise) would take much longer to collapse.
Engage reading: "Modern humans evolved in Africa about 200,000 years ago." Fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco (Hublin et al. 2017) push this to about 300,000 years. Minor; mention only if a student asks.

Misconceptions to listen for

"Melting ice only matters because of sea level." It also freshens the ocean, which changes density and currents, and it causes glacial-lake floods, landslides and water shortages.
"Melting sea ice raises sea level the same way ice sheets do." Floating sea ice is already displacing its weight in water. Land ice (Greenland, Antarctica, glaciers) raises sea level. Sea ice loss matters for albedo and wave protection (Text 3).
"Cold water sinks, so the AMOC is just about temperature." Salt concentration matters too. That is the whole point of the tank: same cold temperature, less salt → sinks more slowly.
"If the AMOC slows, the whole Earth goes into an ice age (like the movie)." A weaker AMOC moves heat around; it doesn't remove it. The North Atlantic region cools; the Southern Hemisphere warms.
"The Younger Dryas proves climate change is natural, so today's is too." Past change had natural causes; today's has a measured human cause (last 5E). Past events show how sensitive the system is and how fast it can change.
"Sea level will rise 3 mm a year forever." The rate has roughly doubled since 1993; straight-line forecasts are too low.
"Sea level rises the same everywhere." Land sinking, currents and gravity changes make it vary. The U.S. East and Gulf Coasts (including NYC) are projected above the global average.
"Models are just guesses." They're physics-based and tested against past and present data. The range comes mostly from not knowing future emissions.
"They happened at the same time, so one caused the other." Timing is correlation. A tested mechanism plus matching evidence is what supports cause (CCC 2 focus all week).

Key vocabulary

Younger Dryas
A sudden return to near-glacial cold in the Northern Hemisphere, about 12,900–11,700 years ago, as Earth was warming out of the last ice age.
Ice core
A cylinder of ice drilled from an ice sheet. Its layers record past temperature and air. GISP2 is from the top of Greenland.
Cryosphere / hydrosphere
The frozen water on Earth (ice sheets, glaciers, sea ice) / all the liquid water (oceans, lakes, rivers).
Salt concentration (salinity)
How much salt is dissolved in water. More salt → denser water.
Density
Mass per volume. Denser water sinks below less dense water.
AMOC
Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation: warm, salty surface water flows north, cools, sinks near Greenland and returns south at depth.
Heat capacity
How much energy a material can absorb per degree of warming. Water's is very high, so oceans store huge amounts of energy.
Rate of change
How much a variable changes per unit of time (e.g. mm of sea level per year).
SSP scenario
Shared Socioeconomic Pathway: one possible future for emissions used to run climate models. Higher second number = more warming.
Glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF)
A sudden flood when a natural dam holding back glacial meltwater fails.
Mitigation / adaptation
Cutting the cause (emissions) / adjusting to changes already happening (relocation, new crops, sea walls).
Correlation vs. causation
Two things change together vs. one change produces the other through a mechanism.

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Period length. Every plan assumes 50 minutes. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and send the item marked flex home.
  • Days 6 and 7 are full. The curriculum fits two Explore/Explain cycles, an Elaborate and an Evaluate into 7 days. If you have an 8th day, split Day 7: Elaborate + Think-Talk-Open Exchange on Day 7, Evaluate + Performance Task Organizer on Day 8.
  • No tank? Run the tank as a teacher demo at the front, or have students use the virtual tank in the student chapter. Absent students can do the whole lab there.
  • Devices. Day 5 needs one device per pair. The spreadsheet needs edit rights (make a copy per student). If devices are short, project the IPCC Atlas and the sea level viewers and have pairs record together.
  • Summary Tasks (after each Class Consensus) work as exit slips or homework. Each has four discussion-reflection items and two content questions. The content questions are the ones to read closely.
  • Unit 6 parking lot. When students ask "how do we fix it?", write it on the DQB. Solutions are the focus of Unit 6.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, map, graph or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has: their own tank observations, the Clovis card order their group argued about, the region they picked in the IPCC Atlas, a street they flooded in the viewer.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Answer key

Climate and Human Populations: discussion promptsEngage · look-fors
How have humans been impacted by climate change in the past?

Shifting deserts and grasslands made populations migrate, grow and shrink. The first wave out of Africa (~120,000 yr ago) died off in a drought caused by global glacial conditions. Orbital changes and suspected volcanic activity crashed the population in Africa to as few as ~10,000 mating pairs. The second wave (~60,000 yr ago) survived in milder temperatures. Low sea level exposed land bridges (Beringia) that let people reach new continents. The Clovis culture disappeared 12,750 years ago, at the same time as major climate changes.

What changes in temperature do you see on the graph?

Around 15,000 years ago Greenland warms; around 13,000 (12,900) years ago temperature drops suddenly; around 11,500 (11,700) years ago it rises again very quickly, to Holocene levels that have stayed fairly stable since. Earlier in the record are repeated rapid swings (Dansgaard–Oeschger events).

What causes can you think of?

Warming: orbital factors plus feedbacks (CO₂, ice-albedo) from the last 5Es. The sudden drop and rebound: accept any reasonable hypothesis (ice melting, ocean changes, volcanoes, a meteor impact has been proposed). Students will test the meltwater → ocean current idea over the next three days.

Melting Ice Investigation Part 1: Sea Level Q1–2Explore 1
1. How did glacial levels change over the past 20,000 years?

About 20,000 years ago an ice sheet covered most of Canada and the northern U.S. It shrank from the south and west edges inward: the southern edge was ice-free 15,000–18,000 years ago, and the last remnants (Quebec, Baffin Island) melted about 5,000–7,000 years ago.

2. How did sea level change over the past 20,000 years?

It rose about 120 m, from roughly −120 m to today's level. It rose fastest around 14,500 years ago (Meltwater Pulse 1A, ~20 m in ~500 years) and leveled off about 6,000–7,000 years ago.

Look and listen for (sets up the lab): "Meltwater goes into the ocean and raises sea level." · "Ice sheets are fresh, so the ocean near them gets less salty." · "In middle school we learned salt and temperature drive currents, so maybe the currents change."

Melting Ice in a Tank

What impact does melting or freezing ice have on ocean currents?

Melting Ice Investigation Part 2Making Sense of the Melting Ice InvestigationTable groupsTanks + dyed waterVideo: AMOC Model (if needed)Domino Discover

By the end of today students should know

  • The AMOC carries warm surface water north; near Greenland the water is cold and salty, so it is dense and sinks, driving the loop.
  • Adding fresh water (glacial melt) lowers the salt concentration, so the cold water is less dense, sinks more slowly, and the current forms more slowly and moves more slowly.
  • Energy in the system comes from the Sun warming water near the equator (in the model, the hot water). A slower current moves less energy north.

Answer key

Melting Ice Investigation Part 2: Ocean Circulation, steps 1–5plan · brainstorm · prediction · two models · observations
1. Plan for Setting up the Global Ocean Circulation Model (before a glacier melts)

A tank of room-temperature water. Equator end: hot water dyed red. North Pole end: cold, very salty water dyed blue. Pour both at the same time and watch the loop: red spreads across the top toward the pole, blue sinks and spreads along the bottom toward the equator.

2. Brainstorm: How is glacial water different from sea water? How can we change our model to show glacial melt entering the water at the north pole?

Glacial ice is fresh water (no salt). Model melt by making the polar water less salty: mix the brine with fresh water. Keep it cold so salt is the only thing that changes (a controlled test).

3. Make a prediction (glacial water vs. cold, very salty Arctic water; vs. warm salt water from the equator)

Glacial water mixing with the cold, very salty Arctic water lowers its salt concentration and density, so it won't sink as fast. Fresh meltwater is lighter than the warm salty water arriving from the equator too, so it tends to sit on top. The overturning should slow down.

Individual response (sample proficient)

Climate change will push more people to move and will threaten food and water. As glaciers melt, glacial lakes grow behind natural dams; when one failed in Bhutan in 1994 it killed 21 people, and about 15 million people live at risk of similar floods, so Bhutan is relocating ~80 families. In Alaska, less sea ice means waves erode shorelines more than 3 feet per year, forcing villages to relocate. Food is affected too: the Bering Sea snow crab fishery crashed in 2018–2019 and Peruvian farmers have had to switch crops. On top of this, our sea level data show the ocean rising ~3 mm per year and speeding up, and IPCC models project 0.44–0.77 m more by 2100 depending on emissions. Each impact has a clear mechanism that starts with warming and melting ice, so these are causal links, not coincidences.

Summary Task content Q1–2after Class Consensus · homework
1. Some climate events are temporary, like a seasonal shift in precipitation, and damage may be reversible. How are these situations similar or different?

Similar: they also cause floods, crop losses and damage. Different: lost glaciers, eroded shorelines, a crashed fishery and rising seas are long-term and largely irreversible on human timescales; they don't bounce back next season, and they keep getting worse as warming continues.

2. How do the rate of change plus our ability to use data to predict help communities adapt and respond?

Because we can measure rates (≈3 mm/yr of sea level rise, accelerating) and model scenarios, communities can plan ahead: relocate families before a dam fails (Bhutan), build protections, change crops, or cut emissions. Faster change leaves less time to adapt, so good forecasts matter more.
