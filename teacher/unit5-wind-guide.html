The phenomenon in one paragraph

Winter Storm Jonas (Jan 22–24, 2016) buried New York City (27.5 in at Central Park) and brought damaging wind gusts, yet forecasts warned people days ahead. Students ask how strong winds form and how meteorologists knew where the storm would go. Three models at three scales give the same answer: uneven heating. In connected bottles of hot and cold water, smoke sinks over the "cool land," flows through the tube and rises over the "warm land." A gas simulation shows why: cool particles are slow, crowded and dense (more pressure at the surface); warm particles are fast, spread out and less dense; air flows from high to low pressure, and convection cells form. A spinning balloon shows the Coriolis effect (paths bend right in the NH, left in the SH). The Global Circulation Model scales this up to the Hadley, Ferrel and polar cells and wind belts that bring cold and warm air masses together and steer storms like Jonas east, then north. On NOAA weather maps students draw isobars, isotherms, dew-point lines and pressure-change lines, find the H, the L and the fronts, then find a low forming on a real Jonas map. They explain why winds spiral into lows and out of highs and why a big pressure difference over a short distance makes strong wind. Finally they switch to engineering: criteria and tradeoffs for burying power lines after Hurricane Helene, then whether wind farms make sense, which feeds the Performance Task.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain wind as air moving from high to low pressure, caused by uneven heating: warm air is less dense and rises (low pressure at the surface); cool air is denser and sinks (high pressure).
  • Connect the same pattern across three scales: bottles, molecules, and the global Hadley, Ferrel and polar cells (and back to convection in the mantle and oceans from Units 3–4).
  • Use the Coriolis effect (right in the NH, left in the SH) with the convection cells to explain the trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies, and why storms like Jonas move west to east and then north up the coast.
  • Draw and read isobars, isotherms, isodrosotherms and isallobars; read station models; locate highs, lows and fronts; find a developing low on a real map (HS-ESS2-8).
  • Explain why winds spiral counterclockwise into lows and clockwise out of highs (NH), and why a large pressure difference over a short distance means strong wind.
  • Specify and prioritize criteria, and name tradeoffs, costs and benefits, for a real solution to wind damage (HS-ETS1-1, HS-ETS1-2).

Misconceptions to listen for

"Wind is caused by Earth spinning." Spin bends moving air (Coriolis). Wind starts with pressure differences from uneven heating.
"Heat rises." Heat doesn't rise. Warmer, less dense air is pushed up as denser, cooler air sinks and slides under it.
"Warm air is lighter because its molecules are lighter." Same molecules. They move faster and spread out, so there are fewer per unit volume.
"Wind blows from low to high pressure." Air moves from high to low, toward where fewer molecules push back.
"Winds blow straight into a low." The Coriolis bend turns inflow into a counterclockwise spiral (NH).
"The Coriolis effect is a force pushing the wind." It's an apparent bend: the ground turns under air moving in a straight line. It matters for storm-sized systems, not sinks.
"The H on a map means hot, the L means low temperature." H and L are pressure. Highs are often cool and dry; lows are where warm air rises.
"The wind barb points where the wind is going." It points to where the wind comes from.
"All three cells are convection cells like the bottles." Hadley and polar cells are direct convection loops. The Ferrel cell is driven by its neighbors (the teacher guide hints at this).
"A solution is good if it works." It also has to be worth it: criteria met, tradeoffs acceptable.

Key vocabulary

Uneven heating
Sunlight and surfaces heat Earth by different amounts in different places, which starts every pressure difference.
Density
How tightly packed the particles are. Warmer air is less dense; cooler air is more dense.
Air pressure
The push of air particles on a surface. High where dense air sinks; low where less dense air rises.
Wind
Air moving horizontally from high to low pressure.
Convection cell
A loop: warm fluid rises, spreads, cools, sinks and flows back (Hadley, Ferrel, polar cells).
Coriolis effect
The apparent bending of moving air because Earth rotates: right in the NH, left in the SH.
Air mass · front
A large body of air with similar temperature and moisture · the boundary where two air masses meet.
Isobar · isotherm
Lines joining equal pressure · equal temperature. Close isobars = strong wind.
Isodrosotherm · isallobar
Lines joining equal dew point · equal 3-hour pressure change.
Station model
A coded symbol showing a station's temperature, dew point, pressure, wind, sky cover and weather.
Midlatitude cyclone
A low with warm and cold fronts and counterclockwise winds (NH); Jonas was one.
Criteria · tradeoff
What a solution must do to succeed · a cost you accept because the benefits are worth it.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a short hand-drawn model. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digital images.
  • Every task uses something only your class has: their smoke observations, their balloon, their group chart, the isobars they drew, their Rumors sticky note.
  • Every drawing carries one "I chose…" note explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check next day. During the Do Now, ask 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing aloud.

Diffusion, Convection and the Spinning Balloon

How does wind form, and why does it bend?

Handout: Wind Formation Investigation, Model 2 Tables 2–3 + Model 3 Table 4Pairs → table groupsDomino DiscoverGas Properties SimulationBalloon · 3 markers

By the end of today students should know

  • When warm and cold air meet, the cold, dense air slides under and the warm air ends up on top.
  • Dense, cool air presses harder on the surface (higher pressure); warm air that has risen leaves lower pressure at the surface. Air moves toward lower pressure.
  • A convection cell is a loop: rise over warm, spread, sink over cool, flow back along the bottom.
  • On a spinning ball, a straight line drawn north–south curves: one way in the north, the opposite way in the south.

Answer key

Part 2 Q1–2Explaining the Global Movement of Storms · Part 2
1. How is the movement between 30° and 0° and between the North Pole and 60° similar to the bottles?

Both are convection loops like the bottles. Warm air rises over the "warm land" (the equator; ~60°), cooler air sinks over the "cool land" (~30°; the pole), and surface air flows from the sinking, high-pressure side to the rising, low-pressure side, like air through the tube.

2. Why do global winds follow these patterns? (molecular level · motion and pressure · converge/diverge · heat, density, pressure)

Molecular: the equator gets more direct sunlight, so air there gains heat energy; its molecules move faster and spread apart, lowering its density. Motion and pressure: the less dense air rises, leaving low pressure at the surface; air that cools aloft gets denser and sinks near 30° and the poles, making high pressure. Converge/diverge: surface air diverges (spreads out) from the highs and converges (comes together) into the lows, where it's forced up. The equator–30° (Hadley) and pole–60° (polar) loops are convection cells; the 30°–60° (Ferrel) cell turns between them, which is why surface air there flows poleward.

1. How did Jonas's strong winds form, and how was the storm carried across the country?

Uneven heating made a cold, dense, high-pressure air mass (from Canada/the Arctic) and a warm, moist, low-pressure air mass (from the Gulf/Atlantic). Air rushed from high to low, bent by Coriolis into a counterclockwise spiral, and the big pressure difference over a short distance made strong winds. 2. Why is this a problem we need to address?

Storm winds down trees and power lines, damage buildings, shut transportation and endanger lives; storms like this repeat along predictable paths, so the damage keeps happening and costs billions (Unit Opening).

Any measurable, prioritized set, e.g.: reduces damage or outages by a stated amount; costs less than the damage it prevents; works in the places storms actually hit; few harmful side effects.

Building wind farms (especially offshore) designed to survive hurricane winds; some suggest very large arrays could weaken storms while producing clean energy.

It targets wind energy itself (taking energy out of the flow) and, long term, climate change. Clean power, fewer emissions, possible (modeled, not proven) storm weakening at huge scale. It likely meets "few side effects" better than "reduces wind damage," which has little real-world evidence.

7. Costs; reasonable tradeoffs?

High cost; storm and wave damage risk; possible local weather/wake effects; wildlife and fishing impacts; doesn't protect much during the storm. A good answer decides: worth it for energy, not (yet) as a storm-wind shield.

Uneven heating → global wind patterns · temperature, density, pressure relationship · predictable storm formation and paths · same mechanism at smaller scale makes strong storm winds · the damage storm winds cause.

The Design Cycle (all three for Proficient)

Identifies the problem · identifies and prioritizes criteria · analyzes costs and benefits to judge whether the tradeoffs are worth it.

Glow / Grow

Specific: e.g., "Glow: used the westerlies to choose a location. Grow: name a measurable criterion."

Exit slip options

Option A

Yes, No or Maybe: should NYC invest in wind farms to protect against storm winds? One reason, one tradeoff.

Option B

Write one question about precipitation or flooding for the DQB.

Homework · drawn model

Where Would You Build It?

Sketch the U.S. East Coast and nearby ocean. Draw the westerlies and a typical hurricane path and Jonas's path. Mark where you would put one wind-damage solution (wind farm, buried lines, or your own idea) and why.

  • Both storm paths with arrows
  • Your solution's location with one criterion it meets and one tradeoff
  • "I chose…" note: how your group's Yes/No/Maybe vote changed (or didn't change) your choice
