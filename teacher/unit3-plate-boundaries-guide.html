The phenomenon in one paragraph

Earthquakes, volcanoes and tsunamis are not scattered at random. On a world map they trace narrow bands. Students first see the pattern (Engage), then look for an explanation in the ocean. The sea floor has long mid-ocean ridges. Its rock, dated by radiometric dating, is youngest at the ridges and older in matching bands on both sides. A map measurement gives a spreading rate of only ~2 cm per year. Earthquakes under Iceland, which sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, are shallow. Together these point to plates moving apart, with magma filling the gap (Explain 1). But many hazards are far from ridges. There, students find mountains, volcanoes and trenches, earthquakes in slanted zones that reach hundreds of kilometers down, and continents with far older rock than the ocean floor. That evidence supports plate tectonics: crust forms at ridges and sinks back into the mantle where plates converge, which is where the biggest earthquakes, the most explosive eruptions and most tsunamis happen (Explain 2). New York sits far from a plate boundary, yet it has mountains. Old rock, uplift and erosion explain the Adirondacks (Elaborate). Students then use plate settings and death-toll data to judge which of the Key 5 regions is most at risk (Evaluate).

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Describe the global pattern of earthquakes, eruptions and tsunamis (narrow bands along ridges, the Pacific "Ring of Fire" and the Mediterranean–Himalaya belt), citing map evidence.
  • Use ocean-floor age (youngest at ridges, symmetric bands) and a rate calculation (R = d ÷ t, a few cm/yr) as evidence that new crust forms where plates move apart.
  • Contrast earthquake depth patterns: shallow under ridges and rifts; a slanted zone reaching hundreds of km under subduction zones.
  • Explain subduction: denser oceanic crust sinks under less dense crust because of gravity, so crust is recycled. That is why the ocean floor is young (under ~200 million years old) while continents keep rock up to ~4 billion years old.
  • Connect boundary types to hazards and features: divergent → ridges, rifts, shallow quakes, gentle eruptions; convergent → trenches, mountains, volcanic arcs, the largest quakes and tsunamis.
  • Compare timescales: an earthquake takes seconds and an eruption hours to years; erosion and mountain building take millions of years. Some changes can't be reversed.
  • Explain how uplift and erosion exposed billion-year-old metamorphic rock at the top of the Adirondacks.
  • Argue which Key 5 region is most at risk, using plate setting, earthquake frequency and magnitude, and human-impact data.

Corrections to the packet and teacher guide

p. 38, Step 4 text: "New York State, which sits in the middle of the North Atlantic Plate…" There is no North Atlantic Plate. The packet itself says North American Plate on p. 35. Tell students before they read.
p. 38, Step 4 text: NY "has not been subject to volcanic activity for at least 1 billion years, and the rock types… are not due to divergent or convergent plate boundaries." NY's present-day landforms come from uplift and erosion, not active boundaries. But its rocks record old plate collisions (Grenville ~1.1 billion years ago; Taconic ~450 million years ago, with a volcanic island arc). Magma also intruded ~200 million years ago as Pangaea rifted (the Palisades). Say: "No volcano has shaped NY's surface recently, but its rocks were made by ancient plate collisions."
Teacher guide, Explain 2 key points (SY 25-26 and SY 26-27, and the unit plan): volcanoes at subduction zones form "when the energy due to friction heats magma." Friction is a minor factor. The sinking plate carries water down, and the water lowers the melting point of the hot mantle rock above it (flux melting). The packet's "heat and friction" is fine as a first model. Accept it, then press: "What else is the ocean plate carrying down with it?"
Teacher guide, Explain 2 key points: at divergent boundaries, magma is "like a pressure cooker." Better: as plates pull apart, hot mantle rock rises, and the drop in pressure lets it partly melt (decompression melting). The mantle is mostly solid, not a sea of magma.
Teacher guide, Elaborate Look & Listen: tectonic plate motion happens "over tens of thousands of years." At a few cm per year, building an ocean or a mountain range takes millions to hundreds of millions of years. Students just calculated this on Day 2.
p. 52, Argument Rubric, Stability & Change row: the Proficient and Developing descriptors are identical. Use: Developing = "Argument does not include, or includes inaccurate, rates at which volcanic eruptions and tsunamis occur."
p. 50, Model Rubric, Stability & Change row refers to "reflection prompt 2." That prompt is on the separate Revisit the Performance Task handout, not in the packet. Make sure students have it.
Teacher guide (both years), Explain 2 launch calls the handout "Modeling Plate Tectonics." In the packet (p. 27) it is Modeling Natural Hazards and Surface Features. Its Summary Task (pp. 32–33) has only one content question, but the guide says to discuss "prompts 4–6." In Explore 1, "After students have completed part 1" should say Part 3 (the calculation).
p. 47, Evaluate region text: Japan is described as having "frequent deep earthquakes." It does, but its most damaging quakes (Kantō 1923, Kobe 1995, Tōhoku 2011) were shallow. Shallow quakes shake the surface hardest. Don't let students conclude that "deep = dangerous."
Missing boundary type: the packet only names divergent and convergent boundaries. Many deadly quakes are on transform faults, where plates slide past each other: the North Anatolian Fault in the Mediterranean region, the Sagaing Fault in Myanmar (M7.7, March 2025), and the San Andreas. Add the term in Explain 2. If your Key 5 death table was compiled before March 2025, it doesn't include the Myanmar quake.
Spreading-rate clarification (not an error): measuring from the ridge to the 65 Ma line on one side gives how fast one plate moves away from the ridge. The two plates separate about twice as fast. Accept 1.5–2 cm/yr as the guide says. The point is the order of magnitude: centimeters per year means tens of millions of years for an ocean basin.

Misconceptions to listen for

"Earthquakes can happen anywhere equally." The maps show narrow bands. Most earthquakes are at plate boundaries; plate interiors like NY are quiet (not zero).
"The mantle is liquid magma and the plates float on it." The mantle is solid rock that flows very slowly. Magma forms only in certain places (ridges, subduction zones, hot spots).
"Plates move fast: an earthquake is the plate jumping miles." Plates creep a few cm/yr. Stress builds up, then a fault slips meters in seconds (Tōhoku up to ~50 m).
"New crust means Earth is getting bigger." This was a real 1960s hypothesis, and the packet uses it on Day 7. Subduction destroys crust as fast as ridges make it.
"The ocean floor is the oldest rock because oceans are ancient." Ocean floor is young (under ~200 million years) because it is recycled. Continents keep rock for billions of years.
"Deeper earthquakes are more dangerous." Shallow quakes usually shake the surface hardest. Magnitude, depth, distance and buildings all matter.
"Tsunamis are giant surf waves caused by wind or tides." They are caused by sudden displacement of water, from sea-floor slip, eruptions or collapses, and landslides. They move the whole water column.
"NY's mountains must be volcanoes" / "NY is on a plate boundary." The Adirondacks are a dome of ancient rock pushed up and exposed by erosion, in the middle of a plate.
"Radiometric dating measures how deep the rock is." It measures the decay of radioactive atoms since the rock formed (PS1.C).

Key vocabulary

Tectonic plate
A large, rigid slab of Earth's crust and upper mantle that moves slowly.
Plate tectonics
The theory that Earth's surface is broken into moving plates whose interactions cause earthquakes, volcanoes and most landforms.
Mid-ocean ridge
An underwater mountain chain where plates spread apart and new ocean crust forms.
Divergent boundary
Plates move apart (ridges, rift valleys). Shallow quakes, gentle eruptions.
Convergent boundary
Plates move together (trenches, mountains, volcanic arcs). The biggest quakes and eruptions.
Subduction
A denser plate sinking beneath another into the mantle.
Transform boundary
Plates slide past each other. Shallow, sometimes very damaging quakes (not in the packet; add it).
Radiometric dating
Finding a rock's age from the steady decay of radioactive atoms in it.
Spreading rate
R = d ÷ t: how fast the sea floor moves away from a ridge (cm/yr).
Magnitude / depth
Energy released by an earthquake / how far below the surface it started.
Uplift
Land pushed upward by tectonic forces.
Constructive / destructive processes
Processes that build land (volcanism, uplift, deposition) / wear it down (weathering, erosion, subduction).

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a cross-section, map, timeline or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task uses something only your class has. Examples: the student's own measured spreading rate, the region they chose in IRIS, their group's Domino Discover idea, or the class tally chart.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Answer key

See-Think-Wonder: Global Patternsp. 4 · expect variety; look for evidence
What patterns do you notice in earthquakes, eruptions and tsunamis? Evidence?

All three form long, narrow bands that overlap: around the rim of the Pacific (Japan, Indonesia, Philippines, Alaska, west coast of the Americas, New Zealand), across the Mediterranean–Middle East–Himalaya belt, and down the middle of the Atlantic. Tsunamis occur mostly where big quakes occur near coasts or under the ocean, especially around the Pacific.

Where do the largest earthquakes occur?

Around the Pacific rim, along coasts with deep ocean trenches: Chile (M9.5, 1960), Alaska (M9.2, 1964), Sumatra (M9.1, 2004), Japan (M9.0–9.1, 2011).

Where do the most explosive volcanoes erupt?

Also around the Pacific rim and Indonesia: Tambora (1815), Krakatoa (1883), Pinatubo (1991). Iceland and Hawaii erupt often but mostly effusively (lava flows), not as the largest explosions.

Look and listen for: the patterns overlap · tsunamis seem to happen when quakes hit coasts · "maybe it's plate tectonics" · "activity near plate boundaries" · "why are there fewer volcanoes than earthquakes?" · "why here and not other places?" Keep the last two on the chart. They drive the 5E.

Answer key

Part 1: Topography of the Ocean Floorp. 5 · Q1–2
1. Mark areas of high earthquake and volcanic activity.

Marks along the center of the Atlantic (through Iceland), the eastern Pacific (East Pacific Rise), the Indian Ocean ridges, and around the Pacific's edges.

2. What patterns do you notice? Which type of ocean floor is connected to the hazards?

Activity in the open ocean follows the mid-ocean ridges (yellow-green). It forms a continuous chain winding through every ocean. Abyssal plains are mostly quiet. Activity near the continents follows the ocean edges, especially around the Pacific.

Part 2: The Age of Bedrock on the Ocean Floorp. 6 · Q1–2
1. What patterns do you notice in rock age as you move toward or away from the continents?

The rock is youngest (red) at the mid-ocean ridges and gets steadily older toward the continents. The bands are roughly symmetric (mirror images) on both sides of a ridge. The oldest ocean floor is only ~180–200 million years old.

2. What might be causing that pattern?

New rock is forming at the ridges (from magma), and older rock is being pushed or carried away on both sides, so the sea floor is spreading apart. Accept any reasoned idea at this point.

Look and listen for: "the sea floor is moving apart in the red regions, which is creating ocean ridges and new crust." Get this to consensus before Part 3.
Part 3: Calculating Seafloor Changep. 8 · Q1–4 · worked example
1. Measure from the center of the new crust (red) to the dark-yellow/light-green border (65 Ma).

On a full-size print, most pairs get about 0.5–0.6 cm. The worked example uses 0.55 cm.

2. Real distance (1 cm = 2,100 km)

0.55 cm × 2,100 km/cm = 1,155 km → × 100,000 = 115,500,000 cm (1.155 × 10⁸ cm).

3. Length of time

65 million years (6.5 × 10⁷ yr).

4. Spreading rate R = d ÷ t

115,500,000 cm ÷ 65,000,000 yr ≈ 1.8 cm/yr. Accept 1.5–2 cm/yr.

Why answers vary: where students measure along the ridge, whether they measure to the near or far edge of the band, and how the map printed. All of these support the same conclusion: a few cm per year, so new ocean crust forms very slowly, over tens of millions of years. Ask: "At 1.8 cm/yr, how long would 1 km take?" (100,000 ÷ 1.8 ≈ 55,000 years).

Iceland from Below

What is happening underneath Iceland's earthquakes?

Packet pp. 10–13 (Part 4 + Making Sense)Investigation Rubric (SY 26-27)Pairs → groupsIRIS Earthquake ViewerDomino Discover

By the end of today students should know

  • Earthquakes under Iceland are shallow (mostly within the top ~20–30 km) and spread along the ridge in a shallow trough.
  • Shallow quakes plus frequent eruptions are evidence of a shallow gap between two spreading plates where magma can rise.
  • Combining several lines of evidence (topography, age, rate, earthquake depth) makes a stronger model than any one alone.

Part 2: Explaining the Pattern of Seismic Activitypp. 28–29 · exemplar

Seismic activity is concentrated in narrow bands because those bands are plate boundaries. At mid-ocean ridges plates move apart: the age map shows the youngest rock at the ridge, we calculated ~2 cm/yr of spreading, and IRIS showed only shallow quakes under Iceland. So pulling apart cracks thin crust and lets magma rise, which explains the shallow quakes and gentle eruptions. Far from ridges, quakes line up with mountains, volcanoes and trenches (Part 2 map and ESSRT p. 12). Under Japan and the Andes, quakes form a slanted zone reaching ~600–700 km, showing a dense ocean plate sinking under another. Locked plates store stress and then slip suddenly, causing the largest earthquakes (Chile M9.5, Japan M9.0–9.1) and tsunamis when the sea floor moves. Melting above the sinking plate feeds explosive volcanoes like Krakatoa. Where continents collide, crust crumples into mountains with shallower quakes. Plate interiors like New York are far from these boundaries, so they have few earthquakes.

Take time for these key points: earthquakes occur at all boundary types · volcanoes at ocean–continent convergent boundaries (confirms Krakatoa) · mountains at convergent boundaries · ridges like the Mid-Atlantic (Iceland) at divergent oceanic boundaries · tsunamis from volcano collapse, undersea eruption, or plate slip (Japan, Krakatoa) · timescales: features slow, hazards sudden; some changes irreversible · the denser plate goes under because of gravity · still unknown: where the heat and energy come from.
Summary Taskpp. 32–33 · reflection 1–4 have no key; one content question
Describe how empirical evidence helped you identify patterns, and how those patterns supported your analysis of the evidence for plate tectonics.

Measured data (rock ages from radiometric dating, our spreading-rate calculation, earthquake locations and depths from IRIS) revealed patterns: symmetric age bands, shallow quakes at ridges, deep slanted quake zones at trenches, and old continents. Each pattern matched a prediction of plate tectonics (crust made at ridges, destroyed at subduction zones), so the theory is supported by several independent lines of evidence rather than one.

Step 2: Analyzing Rock Typespp. 36–37 · Q1–5
1. Trend between rock type and age

Metamorphic rocks are the oldest (Middle Proterozoic, over ~1 billion years: Adirondacks, Hudson Highlands). Sedimentary rocks are younger (Cambrian–Devonian ~540–360 million years; Triassic–Jurassic ~200 million years in the Newark Lowlands). The youngest deposits (Cretaceous, Long Island) are loose sediments.

2–3. Highlight rocks and processes on the infographic

Rocks: limestone, shale, sandstone, dolostone, conglomerate, gneiss, quartzite, marble, schist. Processes near them: weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction, cementation, burial, heat and pressure (metamorphism/recrystallization), uplift.

Which processes formed NY rock? Which are not involved?

Involved: deposition, compaction and cementation (sedimentary); heat and pressure (metamorphic); uplift; weathering and erosion. Not involved in NY's surface rock: volcanic eruption (extrusion), and melting followed by solidification at the surface. Nuance: the Palisades formed from magma that cooled underground ~200 million years ago.

4. How did NY's bedrock form?

More than 1 billion years ago, rock deep in the crust was heated and squeezed during an ancient mountain-building collision, making metamorphic rock. Later, shallow seas covered NY and laid down sediment that became sandstone, shale and limestone on top. Uplift and erosion then exposed the old metamorphic rock in places like the Adirondacks, while younger sedimentary layers remain around it.

5. Which rocks near the surface/higher, which deeper/lower?

Based on the infographic: sedimentary rocks form at the surface from deposited sediment, so expect them near the surface and in low places (basins, seas). Metamorphic rocks form under heat and pressure deep underground, so expect them far beneath the surface.

3. Do the rock types, ages and landforms match your predictions?

Partly. The lowlands match: they are sedimentary rock at low elevation. The Adirondacks don't match: the highest region is made of the oldest, deep-formed metamorphic rock, which should be deep underground. Something must have pushed it up and removed the rock above it. Step 4: Adirondack card order + Q1–2pp. 38–39 · oldest → today
  1. Magma deep underground formed anorthositic rocks (over 1 billion years ago)
  2. Tectonic activity lifted the rock into a massive mountain range (~1 billion years ago, Grenville)
  3. Erosion wore the Grenville mountains down (1 billion → 500 million years ago)
  4. A shallow ocean formed on top (~500 million years ago)
  5. Sedimentary rock formed in the ocean above the old mountain roots (~500 million years ago)
  6. Uplift pushed the area, covered in sedimentary rock, back up (starting ~5 million years ago)
  7. Erosion (rivers, glaciers) stripped the sedimentary cover off the top
  8. Today: ancient rock at the top of the Adirondacks, surrounded by younger rock
1. How does this model explain NY's rock ages and types?

The old metamorphic/igneous core formed deep down over a billion years ago. It was buried under ~500-million-year-old sedimentary rock, then domed up. Erosion removed the sedimentary cover from the top of the dome, so the oldest rock is exposed in the high center and younger sedimentary rock rings it at lower elevations.

2. Constructive vs. destructive processes; compare timescales

Adirondacks: constructive = magma crystallizing, tectonic uplift, deposition; destructive = weathering and erosion. They took over a billion years in total, with uplift and erosion over millions of years. Krakatoa: destructive = the 1883 explosive eruption and collapse destroyed most of the island in hours to days; constructive = new eruptions built Anak Krakatau above sea level by 1927 (decades). Both involve building and wearing down, but on timescales that differ by a factor of millions.

Who is Most at Risk? Q1–2p. 43 · brainstorm
1. What distinguishes high-activity from low-activity areas?

High: located on or near plate boundaries; mountains, volcanoes, trenches, ridges or rifts; frequent quakes; at subduction zones, deep slanted quake zones. Low: plate interiors with old, stable continental crust and worn-down mountains (like NY); few quakes, no active volcanoes.

2. What affects how earthquakes affect people?

Magnitude and depth (shallow shakes harder); distance to cities; population density; building construction and codes; coastal location (tsunami); mountains (landslides); warning systems, preparedness and emergency response; wealth and infrastructure.
