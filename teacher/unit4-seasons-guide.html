Seasons

How does the annual cycle of the Earth around the Sun create seasonal temperature variations?

PE HS-ESS1-7 (NYS) Phases 3E: Engage · Explore · Explain (the materials have no Elaborate or Evaluate) Length 6 days × 50 min (teacher materials list 3) Packet Student Materials pp. 13–39 Routines Domino Discover · RUMORS · Idea Carousel · Class Consensus Discussion
1Engage 2Explore 3Explore 4Explore 5Explain 6Explain

Tap a day to open it. Each day has a minute-by-minute plan, Do Nows, conferring questions, checks for understanding, the answer key, exit slips and drawn homework. Student-facing version of everything: Unit 4: Seasons (Optional 3E) chapter.

The phenomenon in one paragraph

New York City and Melbourne, Australia sit at about the same distance from the equator (40.7° N and 37.8° S), yet NYC's hottest month (July, ~26 °C) is Melbourne's coldest (~10 °C), and the reverse in January. Students start with a probe of six kids' ideas about seasons and trade them as "rumors." With a heat lamp and globe they find the latitude facing the lamp most directly warms most, and that switching the tilt switches which hemisphere warms. A diagram of Earth's orbit shows NYC is warmest when Earth is farthest from the Sun. A flashlight shows the same light spreads over more area at a low angle. City temperature graphs and the Seasons and Ecliptic simulator give real Sun angles: in June the noon Sun stands ~73° over NYC but ~29° over Melbourne. In the Explain they build the claim that tilt + revolution change the angle and hours of sunlight each hemisphere gets, and they rule out distance. The lesson then bridges to Earth–Sun Dynamics, where slow changes in tilt and orbit drive ice ages.

By the end of the 3E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain that sunlight heats Earth, and that light hitting at a high angle is concentrated on a smaller area, so it heats more than the same light at a low angle.
  • Describe Earth's ~23.5° tilt, which keeps pointing the same direction in space (toward Polaris) as Earth revolves around the Sun.
  • Explain that when a hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun it gets a higher noon Sun and longer days (summer); tilted away, a lower Sun and shorter days (winter).
  • Explain why NYC and Melbourne have opposite seasons, and why near-equator places like Quito have very small seasonal changes.
  • Use evidence to show seasons are not caused by Earth–Sun distance: Earth is closest in early January (NYC winter), and the hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same distance.
  • Distinguish rotation (day and night) from revolution (the year).

Corrections to the packet and teacher guide

Setup, p. 16: "Mark three inches… This will represent the natural 23.5° tilt" works only on a globe about 15 in across. On other globes, mark 0.2 × the globe's diameter from the pole (12-in globe: ~2.5 in; 16-in: ~3.3 in).
Setup, p. 16: a "1 minute pause" does not cool the globe back to room temperature, so trials 2 and 3 start warmer. Have students record the starting temperature at each spot for every trial and compare the change, or let the globe cool fully.
Equipment: the packet says to point a "temperature reader" (infrared thermometer) at each spot; the teacher materials list 3 thermometers. Either works; IR readers measure the surface and are faster.
NYC's latitude: the packet gives 40° N (p. 24) and 41° N (p. 29). NYC is 40.7° N. Using 41° in the simulator is fine.
Simulator example, p. 29: the pre-filled June 21 angle is 72.8°. At exactly 41° N the noon Sun is 72.4° (72.7° at 40.7° N). Accept anything from 72° to 73°.
Part D numbering, pp. 25–26: "2." is an instruction (look at Quito), not a question. Q1 and Q3 are the "what surprises you" questions.
Summary Task, p. 39: the packet titles it "What Causes Seasons on Earth?" (same as the Explain guide). It is the handout the teacher materials call the Summary Task.
Teacher materials, Explore (pp. 40–41): two handouts are printed as "Unknown material with identifier ess.u4.l2.explore.h3 / h4." They are What causes seasons? Part 2: Using Computational and Visual Models (pp. 24–32) and Making Sense of … Part 2 (p. 33).

Misconceptions to listen for

"It's summer when Earth is closer to the Sun." (Derek) Earth is closest in early January. If distance were the cause, both hemispheres would have summer at the same time. This is the big one; most students arrive with it.
"The Northern Hemisphere is closer to the Sun in summer." (Susan) Tilting moves NYC a few thousand km closer out of ~150 million km. What matters is the angle and day length.
"The Sun gives off more heat in summer." (Lisa) The Sun's output changes by less than 0.1% over its 11-year cycle; nothing about it is seasonal.
"Winter clouds block the heat." (Devon) Clouds affect weather, not the yearly cycle. Melbourne is cloudy and cold in July.
"One side faces the Sun and the other faces away." (Simone) That's day and night (rotation), which happens every 24 hours.
"Earth's axis tips back and forth during the year." The axis keeps pointing the same way; Earth's position in its orbit changes which hemisphere leans toward the Sun.
"The Sun is straight overhead in NYC at noon in summer." Only between 23.5° N and 23.5° S. NYC's highest noon Sun is ~73°.
"The longest day (June 21) should be the hottest." Land and oceans take weeks to warm, so July is hotter; this "lag" also makes Sept. 22 warmer than Mar. 20 in Part B.
"Places on the equator are always hot." Quito is on the equator but ~13–14 °C because it is high in the Andes. Latitude controls the size of the seasons; altitude also controls temperature.

Key vocabulary

Insolation
Incoming solar radiation: the sunlight energy reaching a surface.
Angle of insolation
How high the Sun is above the horizon. Higher angle = more concentrated light = more heating.
Axial tilt
Earth's axis is tilted ~23.5° and always points toward Polaris.
Revolution / rotation
One trip around the Sun (a year) / one spin on the axis (a day).
Solstice
~June 21 and ~Dec 21: the Sun is overhead at 23.5° N or 23.5° S; longest and shortest days.
Equinox
~March 20 and ~Sept 22: the Sun is overhead at the equator; ~12 h of day everywhere.
Perihelion / aphelion
Earth's closest (~Jan 3) and farthest (early July) points from the Sun.
Weather / climate / season
Conditions now / long-term pattern of weather / part of the year with a repeating weather pattern.

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Why 6 days? The teacher materials list 3 days. In 50 minutes, the heat lamp needs three 5-minute trials plus setup and cooling, and the simulator data tables (12 dates across 3 cities) take a full period.
  • Three-day version. Day 1: probe + RUMORS (skip the weather/climate launch), then Part A as a demo at the front with one globe while groups do Part B. Day 2: Part C and Part D graphs, simulator as a jigsaw (each group takes one city and shares), Making Sense 2 as homework. Day 3: Q1–2 in pairs, video, Class Consensus; Q3 and the Summary Task as homework.
  • It's optional. If most students show the distance misconception on Day 1, do the full sequence; it pays off in Earth–Sun Dynamics, where the orbit and tilt come back.
  • Do Now / Exit Slip. The packet has no boxes; use a half-sheet or notebook page.
  • Period length. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • No heat lamps? Use the chapter's virtual heat lamp on a projector, and do Part C with real flashlights.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has: their Part A data, the rumor tally on the board, their simulator table, their group's poster.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Why Do We Have Seasons?

What do students already think causes seasons?

Packet pp. 14–15: Why do we have seasons?Individual → table groupsDomino Discover · RUMORSPost-itsChapter: Engage

By the end of today students should know

  • Weather is conditions right now; climate is the long-term pattern; seasons are a pattern that repeats every year.
  • People hold several competing ideas about seasons, and today's job is to name ours, not settle it.
  • Things about Earth's position that could matter: distance from the Sun, tilt, rotation.

Your goal today: find out what students think. Don't correct the probe yet; the models do that on Days 2–4.

Minute by minute 50 min

  1. 0–5

    Do Now

    Pick an option below.

  2. 5–13

    Launch: seasons, weather, climate

    Tell students these three are different but related. Table groups brainstorm how weather and climate are similar and different, then Domino Discover around the room.

    Listen for: climate is weather over a long time · weather is right now · seasons repeat every year · seasons have different weather and temperatures.

    "This unit is about climate. To understand long-term climate, we start with the pattern you already live through every year: seasons."

  3. 13–23

    Probe (individual)

    Handout p. 14: six students' ideas. Each student picks one, draws a diagram, and writes a few sentences. Circulate and note how many pick Derek or Susan (distance).

  4. 23–33

    RUMORS

    On a Post-it, each student writes the statement they're most sure about and why.

    "Your job is to share your rumor with as many people as you can in one minute. Say it out loud, listen to theirs, then swap Post-its. With the next partner, share the rumor in your hand, not your original. Ready… go!"

    Run 2–3 one-minute rounds.

  5. 33–42

    Sort the rumors

    Students stick the Post-it in their hand under one of the six names on the board. Read the tally aloud. Ask: Which ideas could both be true? Which ones disagree? Leave the board up all week.

  6. 42–47

    Investigation launch

    Ask: What about Earth's position compared to the Sun might change during the year? Chart ideas: distance, tilt, rotation.

    "Tomorrow you'll test some of these with a heat lamp and a globe."

  7. 47–50

    Exit slip

Do Now options

Option A · Weather or climate?

"It's snowing today." "NYC winters average about 1 °C." Which is weather and which is climate? How do you know?

Snowing today = weather (now). Winter average = climate (long-term pattern).
Option B · Your experience

Expect: day length (dark by ~4:30 pm in December), Sun lower in the sky, longer shadows, snow vs. heat. Save "darker earlier" for Day 4.
Option C · Unit 1 recall

Any answer; note who says yes. It's tested in Part B.

  • Why did you pick that person's idea? What have you seen or learned that makes you think so?
  • If your idea is right, what would summer look like in Australia?Plants the opposite-seasons idea without telling.
  • Which two ideas are the most alike? What's the difference between Derek's and Susan's?
  • How could we test your idea with a model?
  • Is there anyone's idea you're sure is wrong? What would convince you?

Part A Heat Lamp · Part B Orbit Diagram

How do Earth's tilt and distance from the Sun affect temperature?

Packet pp. 16–20: What causes seasons? Part 1, Parts A–BTable groupsHeat lamp · globe · 3 thermometers · stopwatch · tapeChapter: Part A

By the end of today students should know

  • The latitude that faces the lamp most directly warms the most; tilting the globe changes which latitude that is.
  • Tilting the North Pole toward the lamp warms the Northern Hemisphere; away warms the Southern.
  • NYC is warmest when Earth is farthest from the Sun (June) and coldest when it's closest (December), so distance can't be the cause.

Q1. What do you notice about the way the temperature changes at different latitudes?

The latitude facing the lamp most directly warms most. When the North Pole tilts toward the lamp, the Northern Hemisphere spot warms more; when it tilts away, the Southern spot warms more. With no tilt the equator is warmest and the two 45° spots match.

Q2. What do you think is causing those temperature changes?

The angle the light hits each spot. Where it hits straight on, the light is concentrated; where it hits at a slant, it's spread out. The tilt decides which spot gets the direct light. (Accept "angle" or "direct light" today; "spread" is formalized in Part C.)

If data are messy: thermometers in shadow, globes not cooled between trials, or a lamp aimed above the equator are the usual causes. Use it: "What would you change to make it a fair test?"

Q1. What do you notice in the data?

NYC is coldest when Earth is closest to the Sun and warmest when it's farthest. The distance changes only about 5 million km out of ~150 million (about 3%). September is warmer than March even though the distances are similar.

Q2. Can you reach any conclusions about the factors that cause seasons?

Distance from the Sun can't be the cause; if it were, NYC would be warmest in December/January. Something else, like the tilt (Part A), must matter more.

Part C Spread of Light · Making Sense of Part 1

How does the spread of sunlight affect how much a surface heats up?

Packet pp. 21–23: Part C + Making Sense of Part 1Table groupsFlashlight · globe · grid paperDomino DiscoverChapter: How Spread Out Is the Light?

By the end of today students should know

  • The flashlight puts out the same light every time; only the spread changes.
  • Light hitting straight on (high angle) makes a small, bright spot; light hitting at a slant (low angle) spreads over more area and is dimmer.
  • More spread → less heating per area; less spread → more heating. This matches Part A.

Q1. Do you think the amount of light coming out of the flashlight changed?

No. Same flashlight, same distance, same brightness.

Q2. Where was the light most concentrated (least spread out)? Why?

At the equator, where the beam hits the surface straight on (90°). (If the globe is tilted, the spot shifts toward the hemisphere tilted toward the light.)

Q3. Where was the light least concentrated (most spread out)? Why?

Near the poles. The surface curves away, so the beam hits at a low angle and stretches over a bigger area.

Q4. Relationship between spread of light and temperature (cite Part A).

As spread increases, temperature decreases; as spread decreases, temperature increases. Evidence: in Part A with no tilt, the equator (least spread) warmed most and 45° N / 45° S (more spread) warmed less. Same light over more area = less energy per area.

Q1. What surprises you most about the data?

The seasons are flipped: NYC's summer is Melbourne's winter, even though both are about the same distance from the equator and the same distance from the Sun on any given day. Answer key

Question 3: general explanation + diagramp. 37 · exemplar and checklist
Exemplar

Seasons happen because Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5° and keeps pointing the same direction in space as Earth revolves around the Sun. For half the year the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun. Sunlight there arrives at a high angle, so the insolation is focused on a smaller area, and days are longer. More energy per area for more hours raises the temperature: summer. At the same time the Southern Hemisphere leans away, gets spread-out light and short days, and has winter. Six months later Earth is on the other side of the Sun and it's reversed. Evidence: in Part A the spot facing the lamp most directly warmed most; in Part C slanted light spread over more area; the simulator showed NYC's noon Sun at ~72° in June but ~26° in December, while Melbourne's was the reverse, and the city graphs show their seasons are opposite.

Required: how we know it's not distance

(1) Earth is closest to the Sun (~147 M km) in early January, during NYC's winter, and farthest (~152 M km) in early July, during NYC's summer (Part B). (2) If distance caused seasons, both hemispheres would have summer at the same time, but NYC and Melbourne are opposite. (3) The distance changes only ~3%, too little to explain NYC's ~25 °C swing.

Checklist for the poster: ☐ revolution ☐ tilt (same direction) ☐ angle ☐ insolation ☐ focus/spread ☐ temperature ☐ two Earth positions in the diagram ☐ data from at least two models ☐ distance ruled out with evidence.

5. How did patterns of temperature and sunlight help you explain how Earth's motion and people's location create seasons?

Exemplar: The temperature graphs showed a pattern: NYC is warm in June–August and Melbourne is warm in December–February, while Quito barely changes. The sunlight pattern from the simulator matched it: each city is warmest when its noon Sun is highest. Because Earth revolves with its axis tilted the same way, the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun in June and away in December. So where you live (north, south or near the equator) decides when your summer happens and how strong your seasons are. Matching the two patterns showed that sunlight angle causes the temperature change, not distance, since Earth is closest in January.
