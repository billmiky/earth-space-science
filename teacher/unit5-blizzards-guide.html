The phenomenon in one paragraph

On January 22–24, 2016, Winter Storm Jonas dropped 27.5 inches of snow on Central Park, the most of any storm since records began in 1869, with gusts of 48 mph at LaGuardia and JFK (NWS New York). To explain a blizzard students need to explain two things: the wind and the snow. Wind first: bottles of hot and cold water, a gas simulator and a diffusion simulator show that uneven heating makes cool air dense and sinking (high pressure) and warm air spread out and rising (low pressure), and air flows from high to low. Snow next: a fronts simulator and three cup experiments show that where warm, moist air meets cold, dry air, the warm air rises, cools and condenses into clouds and precipitation, and it falls as snow when the air is at or below 32 °F. Students then read the weather like meteorologists, drawing isolines and finding the low, the fronts and Jonas itself on real maps. Finally they use historical storm data and a global climate model to argue whether winter storms where they live will become more frequent or more intense as Earth warms.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain at the molecular scale why heated air becomes less dense and rises and cooled air becomes denser and sinks.
  • Connect density to surface pressure (cool, sinking air = high; warm, rising air = low) and explain wind as air moving from high to low pressure.
  • Identify the independent (temperature difference / uneven heating) and dependent (wind; amount of precipitation) variables in a causal claim.
  • Explain how clouds and precipitation form at a front: warm, moist air rises, cools, and condenses; it falls as snow at or below 32 °F.
  • Explain where and when blizzards happen: mid- and high latitudes in winter, where warm, moist and cold, dry air masses meet, and why cold places with no moisture get little snow.
  • Draw and read isobars, isotherms, isodrosotherms and isallobars; decode a station model; locate H, L, cold and warm fronts on a surface map.
  • Argue from multiple lines of evidence (historical data, formation model, climate model) about future winter storm frequency or intensity, and judge the precision of the models.

Misconceptions to listen for

"Wind is caused by Earth spinning." Earth's spin bends moving air (the Coriolis effect), but wind starts with pressure differences from uneven heating.
"Hot air rises because heat rises." Heat doesn't rise; warmer, less dense air is pushed up by denser, cooler air sinking around it.
"Warm air is lighter because its molecules are lighter." The molecules weigh the same. They move faster and spread out, so there are fewer per unit volume.
"Wind blows from low pressure to high pressure." Air moves from high to low, into the space where fewer molecules are pushing back.
"Clouds are water vapor." Water vapor is invisible gas. Clouds are tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals formed by condensation.
"It's too cold to snow." Very cold air holds very little water vapor, so cold places with no moisture source (like parts of the Arctic) get little snow. Snow needs cold and moisture and lift.
"Global warming means no more blizzards." Fewer freezing days may mean fewer snowstorms, but warmer air and oceans hold more water vapor, so the storms that do happen can drop more.
"Isobars show where the wind blows." Isobars connect equal pressure. Wind blows across them from H toward L, bending (clockwise out of highs, counterclockwise into lows in the NH).

Key vocabulary

Density
How much mass is packed into a space. Warm air: less dense. Cool air: more dense.
Air pressure
The push of air molecules on a surface. More molecules pushing = higher pressure. Measured in millibars (mb).
Wind
Air moving horizontally across Earth's surface from higher to lower pressure.
Air mass
A huge body of air with about the same temperature and moisture throughout (cP, cA, mP, mT, cT).
Front
The boundary where two air masses meet. Cold, warm, occluded, stationary.
Evaporation / condensation
Liquid → gas (absorbs energy) / gas → liquid (releases energy). Condensation makes clouds.
Dew point
The temperature air must cool to for it to be saturated. Close to the air temperature = very moist air.
Isoline
A line joining equal values: isobar (pressure), isotherm (temperature), isodrosotherm (dew point), isallobar (pressure change).
Station model
A coded symbol showing one station's weather: temperature, dew point, pressure, wind, cloud cover, present weather, visibility.
Mid-latitude cyclone
A large low-pressure system with a cold and a warm front; becomes a blizzard when it's cold enough and winds are strong.
Independent / dependent variable
The cause that drives the change / the effect that changes because of it.
Precision / accuracy
How consistent results are / how close results are to the true value.

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Why 10 days? The SY 25-26 materials allow 7–13. Explore 1 has three models, and the Elaborate map work (four isoline maps plus the surface observation map) takes most students two full periods.
  • Seven-day version. Day 1 + Day 2 combined (skip Felix; run the bottles as a demo); Days 4–5 combined (pressure containers as the Do Now, one consensus discussion); Day 8 jigsaw (each pair draws one isoline map, then teaches it); Day 9 assign the station model as homework; keep Days 6, 7 and 10.
  • Packet order. The printed packet has Evaluate Part 1 and the rubric (pp. 28–30) before the Elaborate maps (pp. 31–42), and Evaluate Part 2 after them (pp. 43–46). This guide and the chapter follow 5E order.
  • Do Now / Exit Slip boxes. The packet has none. Use a half-sheet or notebook page.
  • Period length. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • Device-light option. Days 2–3 simulators and the Day 6 fronts simulator can run from one projector while groups record. The chapter has working mirrors of every model.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, storyboard, map or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has: what the smoke did in their bottles, the drops in their cups, their group's chart-paper model, the isolines they drew.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Atmospheric Sciences: Felix Baumgartnerp. 3 · prompts not visible in the source docs; key ideas to look for
How does the atmosphere change as you go up?

Air pressure and density drop fast: ~1013 mb at sea level, ~265 mb at 10 km, ~3 mb near 39 km (U.S. Standard Atmosphere). There are fewer air molecules in each space higher up.

Why did the balloon swell?

With fewer molecules pushing on the outside, the helium inside spreads out until the pressures balance. The balloon was only partly filled at launch so it had room to expand.

Why could he fall faster than sound?

The air was so thin that there was little air resistance (drag) until he fell into denser air lower down.

Model 2: Heating and cooling air moleculespp. 6–7 · See-Think-Wonder
Cooling: molecules? density at the surface? pressure on the surface?

Molecules slow down and move closer together; more dense near the surface; more pressure on the surface. Bottles: the cold-bottle air is dense and sinks, pressing harder on the water.

Heating: molecules? density? pressure?

Molecules speed up and spread apart (and rise); less dense near the surface; less pressure on the surface. Bottles: the hot-bottle air spreads out and rises.

Teacher guide look-for: "When air molecules were warmed and spread apart and rose up there was less pressure on 'Earth's surface'; when they were cooled and came closer together there was more pressure." In a sealed box, hotter gas pushes harder on the walls; in the open atmosphere, warm air rises and spreads away, so surface pressure drops. If a student notices the box pressure going up when heated, praise it and use that distinction.

Table 1, Single Compartment (very low → very high temperature)

Motion: slow → very fast. Volume taken up: particles stay low and close together when cold, spread through more of the box when hot. Density: high → low. Pressure on the bottom: in the closed box it rises as temperature rises (particles hit harder and more often).

Table 2, Diffusion Chamber (one side hotter)

Hot side: fast, spread out, less dense. Cold side: slow, crowded near the bottom, more dense. When the divider is removed, the cold, dense particles slide underneath along the bottom and the warm particles end up on top.

Table 3, Convection Cell (zones 1–4)

1. How did we use molecular-scale mechanisms to explain wind?

Heating gives molecules energy: faster, farther apart, less dense, rises. Cooling: slower, closer, denser, sinks. Crowded molecules at the surface = high pressure; spread out = low. More molecules move from high to low: that net movement is wind.

Water vapor condenses when it cools: molecules slow and stick together. Lab: drops formed on the cooler top cup, and the most formed under the ice.

2. When does evaporation increase, and the effect on clouds?

When the water (and air) is warmer. More evaporation → more water vapor in the air → more material for clouds and precipitation. Lab: hot cup > warm > cold.

Storyboard (cold, dry meets warm, moist → precipitation)

(1) Warm, moist air mass meets cold, dry air mass at a front. (2) Cold air is denser and slides under; warm, moist air is forced up. (3) Rising air cools; molecules slow. Winter is much colder everywhere, and the northern half of the country (Midwest, Northeast, northern Plains) is at or below freezing; in summer almost nowhere is.

Those are the months the northern states' air is at or below 32 °F, so precipitation falls as snow. Evidence: the winter map/months show freezing temperatures across the north.

Moisture. Very cold air holds very little water vapor, and there is no warm, moist air mass to supply it, so little precipitation forms.

Summary Taskp. 27 · answer about precipitation
1. Molecular-scale mechanisms

Warm water molecules move fast enough to escape as vapor. When moist air rises and cools, vapor molecules slow and stick together as droplets; below 32 °F they lock into ice crystals.

2. Energy and matter at two scales

Matter: water moves from ocean/ground to air, up into clouds, back down as rain or snow. Energy: absorbed during evaporation, released during condensation. Observable: clouds, snow, fronts. Molecular: speed and spacing.

3. Variables

Example: independent = how warm and moist the air mass is (water temperature); dependent = amount of precipitation. Temperature changes evaporation and precipitation, not the reverse.

The Nov–Apr mean temperature rises in every scenario through 2100; more under higher-emissions scenarios, and the most at high latitudes. Precipitation projections generally increase in winter for northern regions.

Think: frequency and intensity

Fewer days cold enough for snow could mean fewer snowstorms in places near the freezing line (like NYC), but warmer air holds more water vapor, so the storms that do form can drop more. Accept either direction if it's reasoned from the formation model.

Evaluating Precision and Accuracypp. 43–45
1. How many models is the Atlas based on?

Read the number shown on screen for the variable and scenario chosen (the CMIP6 ensemble; often around 30 or more). Each is a different climate model from a different research group.

2. How does that number enhance precision?

When many independently built models agree, the result is consistent and unlikely to be one model's error, like several scales all reading 100 g. Where the models spread apart, the projection is less precise.

PTO: Connect to the Performance Task (Blizzards 5E)PTO pp. 9–14 · rubric p. 30
Part 1: variables

Independent: global (or local) temperature. Dependent: winter storm frequency (Task 1) or intensity (Task 2).

Part 2: evidence + model

At least two sources: NCA storm graphs (historical), IPCC Atlas (climate model), plus class models (cups: warmer water → more evaporation; fronts). The model shows energy and matter flow (evaporation, rising, condensation, wind H → L) and the molecular mechanism.

Part 3: sample proficient claim (Task 2)

"Winter storms in NYC will likely become more intense as temperatures rise. Mid-latitude cold-season storm intensity rose from well below average in the 1950s to well above in the 2000s (NCA 2014), and the IPCC Atlas projects warmer, wetter winters for Eastern North America. Because warmer water evaporates more and warmer air holds more vapor, more water is available to condense when moist air rises at a front. Counterclaim: fewer days will be cold enough for snow, so some storms will fall as rain; and the models disagree more about precipitation than temperature, which limits precision."

Teacher guide tip: Task 2 (intensity) is usually more accessible because the evidence lines up. Frequency evidence points in different directions; a strong Task 1 answer weighs it and may conclude we can't be sure.
