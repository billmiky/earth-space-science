Mining

How can we evaluate and compare solutions that try to mitigate negative health outcomes for people and organisms that result from mining coal?

The phenomenon in one paragraph

Maps show that one part of the U.S., central Appalachia (eastern Kentucky, West Virginia), has some of the highest lung cancer rates and a cluster of imperiled species. Students ask what is going on there. They "mine" chocolate chips (ore) out of a cookie (land) on a grid with trees, deer habitat, topsoil, streams and vistas, then build a simple computational model: a dollar benefit from the ore and an environmental impact score from the damaged squares. The model shows why the costs are easy to ignore: the benefits are counted in dollars and the damage is not. Readings on mountaintop removal (MTR) coal mining then define the problem: blasting off mountaintops is cheaper and safer for miners, but it buries streams, cuts forests and is linked to higher rates of cancer, heart and lung disease nearby. An optional NYS lab identifies minerals with luster, hardness, streak and cleavage; a reading shows how New York lowers mining's costs with permits, reclamation plans and security deposits. Students finish by weighing pairs of solutions (renewables vs. mine reclamation; stronger regulation vs. reusing mining waste) on cost, safety and reliability, and arguing for one.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain that we get coal, metals and minerals by mining, and that all resource extraction has economic, social, environmental and geopolitical costs, risks and benefits (ESS3.A).
  • Use a computational model (their benefit and impact equations) to quantify the costs and benefits of a mine, and explain what the model leaves out.
  • Describe mountaintop removal and how it destabilizes the hydrosphere (buried and polluted streams), biosphere (forest and species loss) and human health.
  • Identify minerals using physical and chemical properties (luster, hardness, streak, cleavage, reaction with acid) and a flowchart (ESS2.B, NYS).
  • Explain how regulations and technology (permits, reclamation plans, security deposits) change the cost-benefit balance, using New York as the example.
  • Evaluate competing solutions on cost, safety and reliability, name tradeoffs, and argue for one using evidence (HS-ESS3-2, SEP 7).

Corrections to the packet and teacher guide

Explore 1, Analysis Q1: "2,000 kWh… about 1 month of electricity for one household." EIA puts the average U.S. home at roughly 900 kWh a month, so 2,000 kWh is about 2 months. The dollar value (~$350) is in the right range.
Explore 1, Analysis Q2c: the Environmental Impact equation adds counts of lost squares to a percent of unrecovered land. Have students add percents for all six terms (score out of 600) so the units match. Keys use percents.
Teacher Guide for Mining Lab, sample Table 2: the Vista row adds to 8, not 9, and the Tree row uses half-squares (0.5). By the packet's rule, 1.5 clean topsoil × 3 = 4.5 trees, not 0.5. Tell students to round down to whole squares and check that each row adds to the total.
Engage (teacher note to add): smoking is the leading cause of lung cancer (CDC), and smoking rates differ a lot by state; have students check state rates in CDC data before blaming mining alone. The maps show correlation. Hendryx's studies found higher rates near MTR after adjusting for smoking, which is the stronger evidence. Make students raise this before Explain 1.
Explore 2, Hardness step 4: "If the glass leaves a mark but the penny did not…" should be "but the nail did not." Better: try to scratch the glass with the mineral; if the mineral scratches glass, hardness is greater than 5.5.
Explore 2 (teacher guide): reference-table page numbers ("pages 9 and 10," "16–17") depend on the edition. Check them against the ESS reference tables your students actually have.
Explain 2 text: fracking "uses water or liquid CO₂." It is mostly water, sand and chemicals pumped at high pressure. Most induced earthquakes come from injecting wastewater underground (USGS), not the fracturing itself.
SY 26-27 Unit Plan storyline: "mining practices in New York City." The reading is about New York State (garnet in the Adirondacks, salt, Long Island sand).
Elaborate Solution A: "the United States produces 14%." USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 estimates U.S. mine output at about 45,000 of 390,000 tons REO in 2024, about 12%. China is by far the largest producer; check the USGS world-production table before quoting other countries' shares.
Elaborate Solution B: "90% of mined sites… have simply been abandoned." NRDC's point is that ~90% have no economic post-mining use; most were graded and seeded with grass, not left as open pits. Also, OSMRE puts the 2021 law's abandoned mine land money at about $11.3 billion (packet: $11.2 billion). Elaborate Solution B also has a stray "[Image source]" link mid-sentence; ignore it.
Explore 1, Part 1 introduction: "conduct a rare Earth element mining business," but the handout introduction and the Analysis questions treat the chips as coal. Tell students the chips stand for coal in this lab.
Explore 1 and Explore 2 partner rubrics: "For each of the four categories" but only three are listed (Contributions, Working with Others, Time Management).
Teacher materials, Explore 1: "have the students answer the Summary Questions." There is no Summary Questions section; it means the Analysis of the mining impact calculations and the Making Sense analysis questions.
Teacher materials, Explain 1: labels cost-benefit analysis "CCC ETS2.B" and says it "will be used in computational modeling in the next 5E." ETS2.B is a DCI; the CCC in this 5E is Influence of Science, Engineering, and Technology on Society and the Natural World. Mining is the last 5E. The routines notes also point to the "Biology Course Guide"; use the ESS Course Guide.
Unit Plan videos list: "Rock Cleavage" and "2 Minute Video on Rock Mineral Cleavage" are the same YouTube link. Only two distinct videos support Explore 2.

Misconceptions to listen for

"Mining is only for coal." Phones, wind turbines, batteries, salt, gravel and cement all come from mining.
"Reclaimed land is back to normal." The cookie can't be put back together. Reclaimed MTR sites are usually flatter grassland, not the original forest and streams.
"If it doesn't cost money, it isn't a cost." Lost streams, forests and health are real costs; markets just don't price them. That's the point of Analysis Q4–5.
"Mining near Appalachia proves mining causes the cancer." The maps show correlation. Smoking, radon and poverty matter too. Causation needs a mechanism (dust, polluted water) plus studies that control for other causes.
"Renewable energy has no mining impacts." Turbines, panels and batteries need metals and rare earth elements, and mining them pollutes too.
"Rare earth elements are rare." They are found worldwide but in low concentrations, which makes them hard and dirty to separate.
"New York doesn't have mining." NY mines salt, garnet, wollastonite, zinc, crushed stone, sand and gravel. It just has no coal.
"Regulation means no mining." Regulation changes the balance of costs and benefits; NY still mines, with fewer and more carefully planned mines.
"Hardness is how hard it is to break." Hardness is resistance to scratching. Breaking along flat planes is cleavage.

Key vocabulary

Ore
Rock with enough of a valuable resource to be worth mining (the chocolate chips).
Extraction · processing
Removing ore from the ground · separating the valuable part from waste rock.
Reclamation
Restoring mined land: regrading, replacing soil, replanting.
Mountaintop removal (MTR)
Blasting off mountaintops to reach coal seams; waste rock is pushed into valleys (valley fill).
Acid mine drainage
Acidic, metal-rich water that flows out of mines and waste piles.
Cost-benefit ratio
Comparing what a choice costs with what it gains, across economic, social, environmental and geopolitical factors.
Criteria · constraints · tradeoffs
What success requires · limits (cost, safety, reliability, aesthetics, culture, environment) · what you give up.
Stabilize / destabilize
Help a system stay in balance / push it out of balance.
Rare earth elements (REEs)
17 metals used in magnets, batteries and electronics; spread thinly in rock.
Luster · streak
How a surface reflects light (metallic/nonmetallic) · color of the powder on a streak plate.
Hardness · cleavage
Resistance to scratching (Mohs scale) · breaking along flat planes.
Security deposit (bond)
Money a mine gives the state up front; returned only after reclamation is done.

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Why 9 days? The teacher materials say 7–9. The cookie lab has two 5-minute timed rounds plus four weighings per student, then a long grid count and two equations; at 50 minutes that is two class days after the Engage. Explore 2 is marked optional but supports the NYS minerals standard.
  • Seven-day compressed version. (1) Skip Explore 2 (Day 6); on Day 7 give students the NY resource maps as a 5-minute warm-up instead. (2) Merge Days 2–3: do Part 1 and the grid in class, assign the Analysis calculations as homework, and run the Domino Discover as the next day's Do Now. (3) Do the Explain 1 Summary Task as homework. Keep Explain 1, Elaborate and Evaluate whole.
  • Eight-day version. Keep Explore 2 but merge Days 2–3 as above.
  • Matching the teacher materials (7–9 days): 9 days = this guide as written; 8 days = merge Days 2–3; 7 days = also skip Explore 2. Day 9 is full; if it runs long, move En-ROADS and the Domino Discover to the first 15 minutes of the Unit Closing, which opens En-ROADS again anyway.
  • Do Now / Exit Slip boxes. The SY 26-27 Student Materials have no Do Now or Exit Slip spaces; plan on a half-sheet or notebook page.
  • Food in the lab. Check allergies (nuts, gluten, dairy). Students eat cookies only after Part 2 step 12, with clean hands and no shared tools. The scales and grids are lab equipment.
  • Lab safety (Day 6). Goggles for the acid test; dilute HCl only; tape glass plate edges; scratch the plate flat on the table, never held in the hand.
  • Period length. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, cross-section, map or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task uses something only your class has: a student's own cookie masses, their marked-up Mining Area Grid, their triad's poster, their mineral test results.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, ask 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Cancer and Biodiversity Loss

Why are lung disease, heart disease, and cancer unequally distributed in the United States?

Handout: Cancer and Biodiversity Loss in the United States · Q1Table groupsDomino DiscoverChapter: Engage (see alignment note)

By the end of today students should know

  • Lung cancer is not evenly spread in the U.S.; one hot spot is central Appalachia (eastern Kentucky, West Virginia, nearby states).
  • That same region has many imperiled species; places with environmental disease often also have biodiversity loss.
  • A map pattern is a correlation. We need to find out what is happening there.
  • Coal is mined in two main U.S. regions (Appalachia and the West/Wyoming); we'll model what mining does to land.

Mining the Cookie

How does coal mining contribute to loss of biodiversity?

Extracting Materials from the Earth · Part 1 + Part 2 steps 1–11Mining Area GridIndependent → pairsCookies, scales

By the end of today students should know

  • Ore is hard to get out without damaging the land around it.
  • Land cannot be fully reclaimed: crumbs scatter, the shape changes.
  • Damage spreads downstream and through food chains: dirty water → less topsoil → fewer trees → fewer deer.
  • Where you put the mine and how carefully you dig change the damage.

Minute by minute 50 min

  1. 0–3

    Do Now

    Short today; pick A or B.

  2. 3–8

    Set up

    Pass out cookies, tools and containers. Students mass the whole cookie (Table 1, row 1) and sketch the side-view "landscape." Pairs: one holds containers, one mines, then switch.

  3. 8–15

    Site the mine

    Students choose where on the grid to put the cookie and the processing plant and trace both. Don't hint about water flow; that's the discovery.

  4. 15–20

    Extraction · 5-minute timer

    Tools only. Cookie stays flat. Run one class timer.

  5. 20–25

    Weigh (row 2)

    Mass processing plant (chips + crumbs together) and cookie; subtract to get stray crumbs. Remember to subtract container masses.

  6. 25–30

    Reclamation · 5-minute timer

    Clean the chips, return crumbs to the mine circle. Then weigh clean chips, leftover in plant, and cookie (row 3).

  7. 30–33

    Pause: Part 1 debrief

    "Did your land go back the way it was? What would that look like at a real mine next to a town?"

  8. 33–47

    Part 2 grid count (pairs)

    Steps 1–11: sketch post-mining topography, shade mine/plant squares, X crumb squares, X downstream water, then topsoil (clean water ÷ 2), trees (topsoil × 3), deer (smaller of trees ÷ 3 and water ÷ 3), vistas. Fill Table 2. Round down to whole squares. flex: finish counts tomorrow.

  9. 47–50

    Clean up · eat

    Wipe scales. Cookies only after the grid is assessed.

Do Now options

Option A

If you were a mining company, what would you care about most: speed, cost, or protecting the land? Why?

Any defended answer; revisit after the lab.
Option B

Water flows downhill. If a mine is near the top of a stream, what happens to everything downstream?

Pollution travels downstream and affects everything below the mine.

Conferring questions

  • How did mining change the landscape? Compare your two side-view sketches.
  • What challenges did you face trying to restore the original landscape?
  • How did your mining operation change the natural resources of the area?
  • Why did you put your mine there? Would you move it now?Mines downstream damage less water.
  • What numbers would you use to compare the costs and benefits of your mine?
  • Should the environmental impact number be positive or negative? Why?

Check for understanding

After the grid count

Two students mined the same mass of chips. Student 1's mine was in the upper-left corner; Student 2's was in the lower-right. Who probably damaged more water squares?

  • A. Student 1, because water flows from top-left to bottom-right, so everything downstream is contaminated.Correct.
  • B. Student 2, because the bottom has more water.The amount downstream of the mine matters, not where water collects.
  • C. Same damage, same ore.Location changes indirect impacts.
  • D. Neither; water is only hurt by the processing plant.The mine and plant both count, and crumbs too.

  • C. Deer ate them.Deer depend on trees in this model, not the other way.
  • D. Reclamation removed them.Reclamation returns crumbs; it doesn't remove trees.

Step 3 · argumentsample proficient response

New York reduces mining's health and ecological impacts mainly through regulation before and after mining. Companies must have an approved use and reclamation plan to get a permit, and the state holds a security deposit it keeps if a company abandons reclamation, so companies plan to restore land and even reclaim one part of a mine while working another. Appalachian coal mines blast off whole mountaintops and push waste into valleys, and most of that land is never restored to forest; enforcement there is left to states and has been uneven. NY's rules work partly: its mines are smaller and mostly for stone, salt and garnet, which pollute less than coal, but open pits still cause dust, noise and habitat loss, and Long Island sand mining has been linked to groundwater pollution. The costs are real: the number of permitted mines has dropped for years and drilling fell after the fracking ban, costing jobs and revenue. The constraint is that NY has no coal; Appalachia's remaining coal is too thin to mine by tunnel, so the same rules might end coal mining there entirely.

Summary TaskExplain 2; reflection prompts 1–4, then two "Explain what you know" prompts
Explain what you know, prompt 1: How do social and cultural contexts impact the ways solutions are evaluated to inform decisions about mining regulations?

NY chose to value public health and scenery (Adirondacks, wetlands) over some jobs and money, so it banned fracking and denies weak permits. In Appalachian coal towns, where mining has been the main job for generations, people weigh jobs and identity more heavily. Science shows the risks; communities' values decide the rules.

Explain what you know, prompt 2: In what ways does New York balance competing economic, societal, environmental, and ethical considerations to increase benefits and decrease costs?

It still allows mining (economic benefit) but requires reclamation plans and deposits (environmental), rejects plans that harm wetlands, forests or views (environmental and societal), and accepts fewer mines and less drilling (economic cost) to protect health (ethical).

Renewables or Reclamation?

How can we evaluate solutions to minimize environmental causes of death in mining and other communities?

Reducing the Impacts of Mining Q1–5 · Read-Generate-Sort-Solve · Reflect 1–2Mining Elaborate Student Texts with SolutionsGroups of 3–4Chapter: Elaborate (different solutions)

Sample for Solution A

Switching to renewables maximizes benefits because it removes the cause: if we burn less coal, less is mined, so fewer mountaintops are blasted and fewer people breathe mining dust and burning pollution. Sample for Solution B

Reclamation gives benefits right where the harm is: cleaning water, removing waste piles and replanting native forest directly protect Appalachian families and species, and the 2021 law already funds it. Renewables just move mining harm to other countries' communities. Reclamation is expensive and imperfect, but with native species and stricter rules on new mines it can restore much of what was lost.

Step 1. Define the problem and describe the solution

Systems: geosphere (mountains removed), hydrosphere (buried, acidic streams), biosphere (forest and species loss), human health (cancer, heart and lung disease). Humans altered them by blasting, valley fills and processing waste. The solution (e.g., reuse waste) works by removing waste and treating drainage, which stabilizes water and soil; regulation stabilizes by preventing damage before it happens.

Step 2 Q1. Review your calculations

Student's own benefit ($) and impact score (out of 600) from Day 3.

Step 2 Q2. Effect on the benefit equation

Regulation: slower, more careful mining → fewer tons in the time allowed (smaller "number of tons"), plus reclamation costs, so the benefit goes down. Reuse waste: crumbs and drainage become products, adding a new positive term (recovered metals, bricks), so the benefit goes up. Renewables: fewer tons mined, benefit from coal goes down. Reclamation: no change to tons; subtract reclamation cost.

Step 2 Q3. Effect on the environmental impact equation

Regulation: mines kept away from streams → fewer direct and downstream water squares, so topsoil, trees and deer survive; impact goes down. Reuse waste: fewer stray crumbs and cleaner water → lower "unrecovered land" and water loss. Reclamation: more crumbs returned and squares re-planted → lower score, but never zero.

Step 3. Evaluating the Solution

The written argument. Look for: how the solution solves the problem; how it affects the systems involved (geosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, human health); its costs, safety and environmental impacts with evidence from the texts; and what it leaves unsolved. A strong answer uses the student's own Step 2 model changes as evidence.

Reflect: locally / globally

Locally: cleaner streams, fewer illnesses, jobs gained or lost in Appalachian towns. Globally: less coal burned (less CO₂) for regulation and renewables; less dependence on imported REEs for reuse; harm may shift to other countries for renewables.
