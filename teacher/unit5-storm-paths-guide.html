The phenomenon in one paragraph

Storm maps show that winter storms start in the western and central U.S. and sweep west to east, curving northeast, and that Atlantic hurricanes form over warm tropical water, move west, then curve north and northeast. Students first find that sunlight is concentrated at the equator and spread out at the poles, so the planet is heated unevenly, and that air masses take their temperature from latitude and their moisture from the surface (cP from Canada, mT from the Gulf). Next they watch a global circulation animation and build Table 3: air rises where surface winds collide (0°, 60°: low pressure, wet) and sinks where they spread apart (30°, 90°: high pressure, dry). A spinning balloon shows why the winds bend (right in the NH, left in the SH), giving the trade winds and the westerlies. Put together, the westerlies carry cold and warm air masses together over the U.S. and steer winter storms northeast, while the trades push young hurricanes west until the westerlies pick them up. Students forecast Winter Storm Jonas (Jan 2016) on real surface maps, then read a 2021 study using the warm Pliocene as a model: the westerlies may shift about 5° poleward as Earth warms. They test what that would do to winter storms at NYC's latitude and write an argument with a counterclaim.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain uneven heating: the same sunlight is concentrated near the equator and spread out near the poles, so energy received (and temperature) drops toward the poles.
  • Classify air masses (cT, mT, cP, mP) and explain that they take their temperature from latitude and their moisture from the surface (land = dry, ocean = moist).
  • Use the global circulation model: rising air, low pressure and wet weather at 0° and 60°; sinking air, high pressure and dry weather at 30° and the poles; surface wind blows from high to low.
  • Use the balloon model to explain the Coriolis effect (right in the NH, left in the SH) and name the trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies.
  • Explain why winter storms form over the U.S. (cP meets mT under the westerlies) and why they track west → east → northeast; explain why Atlantic hurricanes move west, then recurve north and northeast.
  • Explain why lows spin counterclockwise in the NH (clockwise in the SH).
  • Evaluate a model-based claim (Pliocene analog → westerlies shift ~5° poleward), name its assumptions, and argue from evidence about winter storm frequency at NYC's latitude, including a counterclaim.

Misconceptions to listen for

"It's hotter at the equator because it's closer to the Sun." The difference in distance is tiny. It's the angle: the same beam is concentrated near the equator and spread out near the poles.
"Wind blows from low pressure to high pressure." Surface air flows from high to low (more molecules pushing toward fewer).
"Hot air rises because heat rises." Warm air rises because it is less dense than the cooler air around it, which sinks and pushes under it.
"The Coriolis effect is a force that pushes the wind." It's an apparent bend: the ground rotates under air that is moving in a straight line.
"Toilets and sinks spin the other way in the Southern Hemisphere." Coriolis matters for things hundreds of kilometers wide that last hours or more (storms, wind belts), not for a sink.
"Storms move from east to west across the U.S." In the midlatitudes the westerlies carry storms west to east (and northeast). The trades carry tropical storms west.
"Westerlies blow toward the west." Winds are named for where they come from. Westerlies blow from the west toward the east.
"Moving the storm track north means more storms everywhere." The bands to the north gain storms; bands to the south lose them.

Key vocabulary

Insolation
Incoming solar radiation reaching a place (W/m²).
Air mass
A huge body of air with about the same temperature and moisture throughout; it takes on the properties of where it forms.
cT · mT · cP · mP
Continental (land, dry) or maritime (ocean, moist) + tropical (warm) or polar (cold).
Air pressure
The push of air molecules on a surface. Sinking air → high (H); rising air → low (L).
Convection cell
A loop of rising warm air and sinking cool air (Hadley, Ferrel and polar cells).
Coriolis effect
The apparent bending of moving air (right in the NH, left in the SH) because Earth rotates beneath it.
Trade winds · westerlies · polar easterlies
Surface wind belts: from the east near the tropics, from the west in the midlatitudes, from the east near the poles.
Trajectory / storm track
The path a storm's center follows over time.
Front
The boundary where two air masses meet.
Analog model
Using a past event (like the Pliocene) as a stand-in to predict the future.

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has: the Rumors chart, a partner's balloon, their group poster, their own Jonas forecast marks, their own storm counts.

Q2. Patterns in "Energy from the Sun" vs. "Energy on Earth"?

Energy from the Sun is the same at every location; energy received is highest at the equator and decreases toward both poles (symmetrical in March).

Q3. Patterns in the spread of sunlight?

Near the equator the beam hits straight on and covers a small area (concentrated). Toward the poles it hits at a low angle and spreads over a larger area.

Q4. Connections between spread and energy received?

Yes: the more spread out the sunlight, the less energy each square meter receives. Spread is greatest at the poles, where energy received is lowest.

Why Does Air Curve? The Balloon Model

Why does air curve as it travels north and south?

Packet: Class 4 box · Explore 2 Part 2: procedure, Analysis of Observations Q1–8, Reliability of the Model Q1–3PairsBalloon + 3 permanent markersSY 26-27: Wind 5E Model 3, pp. 28–30Chapter: virtual balloon

By the end of today students should know

  • Seen from above the North Pole, Earth spins counterclockwise; from above the South Pole, clockwise.
  • A straight path drawn across a spinning surface appears to bend: to the right in the NH, to the left in the SH. This is the Coriolis effect.

Reliability of the Model Q1–3packet Explore 2 Part 2
R1. Differences between the balloon and Earth's surface?

Smaller, smooth, not a perfect sphere, no atmosphere, oceans or mountains, spun by hand at an uneven speed, can be spun either way.

R2. Does it work well enough to explain why wind bends?

Yes, for this purpose: it shows the cause (a rotating surface moving under a moving object) and gives the right bend directions for pole-to-equator motion. It can't show wind speeds or exact paths.

R3. Assumption needed to trust it for why tropical cyclones spin in different directions?

That the balloon's spin behaves like Earth's rotation (same direction, steady) and that air flowing toward a storm's center bends the same way as the pen line.

Know the model's limit: the pen starts with no sideways motion, which matches air leaving a pole (the pole's surface barely moves). Air leaving the equator is already moving east with the ground at ~1,670 km/h, faster than the ground farther north, so it drifts east (still a right-hand bend in the NH). A pen drawn from equator to pole on a balloon drifts west instead. That's why the SY 25-26 packet only draws pole → equator.

Answer key

Part 1: High and Low Pressure Bands, Q1–2packet Explain 2 Part 1 · CER tables
Q1. Why is pressure low at 0°, 60° N and 60° S?

Claim: air is rising there. Evidence: Table 3: surface winds collide and air rises at 0° and 60°. Reasoning: at the equator, strong sunlight warms the air; molecules speed up and spread apart, so the air is less dense and rises. At 60°, converging surface winds force the warmer westerly air up over colder polar air. Rising air leaves less air pressing on the surface.

Q2. Why is pressure high at 30° N/S and 90° N/S?

Claim: air is sinking there. Evidence: Table 3: surface winds move apart and air sinks. Reasoning: high up, air cools; molecules slow and crowd together, so it becomes denser and sinks. At the poles, very cold air is dense and sinks. Sinking air piles up and presses harder on the surface.

Part 2: General Direction of Wind, Q1–2packet Explain 2 Part 2 · CER tables
Q1. Why do surface winds move in the north/south directions shown?

Claim: surface air moves from high-pressure latitudes to low-pressure latitudes. Evidence: Table 3: high at 30° and 90°, low at 0° and 60°; winds blow from 30° toward 0° and 60°, and from 90° toward 60°. Reasoning: denser, high-pressure air pushes outward toward places with fewer molecules pressing down.

Q2. Why do they move in the east/west directions shown?

Claim: Earth's rotation bends them (Coriolis effect). Evidence: the animation's winds curved; the balloon line bent right in the NH and left in the SH. Reasoning: Earth's surface moves east at different speeds at different latitudes, so air moving north or south appears to bend: equator-bound air becomes the trades (from the east), pole-bound air becomes the westerlies (from the west).

Q1 (p. 35). How is movement between 30° and 0°, and between the pole and 60°, like the bottles?

Same loop: air sinks over the cooler side (30°, the pole), flows along the surface toward the warmer side (0°, 60°), rises there, and returns high up. A convection cell, just bigger.

Q2 (p. 35). Explain why global winds follow these patterns (heat, density, pressure).

The equator gets the most energy, so air molecules there move faster and spread out; the air is less dense and rises, leaving low pressure, so surface air converges there. High up it moves poleward, cools, becomes denser and sinks near 30°, making high pressure where surface air diverges, flowing back toward 0° (trades) and toward 60° (westerlies). Cold, dense polar air sinks (high) and flows toward 60°, where it meets the westerlies and the warmer air is forced up (low).

Q3 (p. 38). Global winds, Jonas's air masses, and why Jonas moved east then north

The pattern comes from uneven heating (heat → density → pressure) plus Coriolis bending. Polar air flowing south brought cold, dry cP air from Canada; the westerlies' southwesterly flow brought warm, moist mT air north from the Gulf. They met over the southern U.S., where a low formed. The westerlies carried the low east, and as it reached the coast it turned north-northeast up the Atlantic seaboard toward the Mid-Atlantic and NYC.

Buzzwords (p. 39): heat, density, pressure, Coriolis. A strong answer uses all four in a cause-and-effect chain.

Q1. Why do the Wet latitudes get lots of precipitation?

Claim: air rises there. Evidence: Table 3: collide, rising, low at 0° and 60°. Reasoning: rising air expands and cools; water vapor molecules slow down and condense into droplets that fall as rain or snow.

They show smooth, straight belts and ignore continents vs. oceans (land heats faster), mountains, seasons (belts shift north in July, south in January) and moving highs and lows. They're averages.

They wiggle and change: the jet stream meanders, storms form and travel, land and sea breezes and mountains redirect local winds.

Elaborate Part 1: Storm Origin and Trajectories, Q1–3packet Elaborate Part 1 (a teacher's key cites unit packet pp. 114–116)
Q1. Why do so many winter storms form over the U.S.?

The U.S. is in the midlatitudes between the source regions of cold, dry cP air (Canada, the Arctic) and warm, moist mT air (Gulf of Mexico, subtropical Atlantic). Cold air flows south and the southwesterly westerlies carry warm, moist air north; they meet along the polar front over the U.S. Warm air is forced up over the cold air, cools, and its water vapor condenses into snow, forming a winter storm. The westerlies keep moving air masses and fronts across the continent.

Q2. Path of blizzards over the U.S. (2016–2017 map + Global Winds)

Storm tracks run from the central U.S. eastward, then curve northeast along the East Coast. At ~30–60° N the westerlies blow from the southwest toward the northeast, so storms embedded in them are carried west → east and northeast.

Q3. Path of hurricanes in the North Atlantic and eastern U.S. (2020 map)

They form over warm, low-latitude Atlantic water (≥ ~26.5 °C). The trade winds steer them west toward the Caribbean. Near ~30° N they curve north around the subtropical high, then the westerlies carry them northeast, sometimes up or along the East Coast. (2020 had a record 30 named storms; NOAA.)
