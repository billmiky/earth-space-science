Origin of the Universe

How many solar systems and galaxies are out there? Were they formed the same way?

PE HS-ESS1-2 Length 7 days × 50 min (curriculum says 6–7) Anchor routines Rumors · Domino Discover · Class Consensus
1Engage 2Explore 3Explore 4Explain 5Elaborate 6Elaborate 7Evaluate

Tap a day to open it. Each day has a minute-by-minute plan, Do Nows, conferring questions, checks for understanding, the answer key, exit slips and drawn homework.

The phenomenon in one paragraph

There are more stars in the universe than grains of sand on Earth, and most of them probably have planets. Students ask: did all of those galaxies and solar systems form the same way ours did? They follow the evidence scientists used. Light from other galaxies shows the same hydrogen and helium absorption lines as the Sun, but shifted toward red, and the farther the galaxy, the bigger the shift. Using sound and light models of the Doppler effect, students explain the redshift: galaxies are moving away, so the universe is expanding. Running the expansion backward means the universe began small, hot and dense: the Big Bang. Because energy and matter are conserved, that early universe should have left traces we can see today. The traces are there: the cosmic microwave background (the early light, stretched into microwaves) and a universe that is still about 3/4 hydrogen and 1/4 helium. The same processes happened everywhere, so other solar systems are probably a lot like ours.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain the Doppler effect: when a source and an observer move apart, waves arrive stretched (longer wavelength, lower frequency). When they move together, waves arrive squished (shorter wavelength, higher frequency).
  • Connect wavelength to color: for light, longer wavelength means redder and shorter wavelength means bluer. So a source moving away is redshifted.
  • Read galaxy spectra: the same H and He absorption-line pattern appears in every galaxy, shifted toward red, and more distant galaxies shift more. So the universe is expanding.
  • Infer from expansion that the universe was once small, hot, dense and bright, and began about 13.8 billion years ago.
  • Use conservation of energy and matter to predict what should remain today: the early light is now stretched into microwaves (the CMB), and matter is still about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium. Fusion in stars and supernovae made the small amount of heavier elements.
  • Build a claim–evidence–reasoning explanation of the Big Bang from three lines of evidence: redshift, CMB and H/He ratio.
  • Argue that the same processes formed galaxies and solar systems everywhere, so with trillions of stars, Earth-like planets (and possibly life) are likely elsewhere.

Handle the Engage with care

Students may share ideas about creation rooted in religion or culture. The curriculum asks you to acknowledge and honor all of them during Rumors. Then frame the course's job: "In science class we'll look at the evidence scientists have gathered and build a scientific explanation. A scientific explanation isn't necessarily in conflict with religious or philosophical beliefs. Many scientists are religious and see the two as ideas that can coexist." Say this on Day 1 before the first idea is challenged. Also say it again on Day 4 when the class eliminates or changes ideas from the Engage list. There, eliminate scientific claims the evidence doesn't support (like "the universe has always been the same size"), not students' beliefs.

Misconceptions to listen for

"The Big Bang was an explosion at one spot that threw galaxies out into empty space." Space itself is expanding everywhere at once. There is no center and no edge it is expanding into.
"We're at the center, because everything moves away from us." Every galaxy sees all the others moving away. Check this with the balloon: pick any dot as "home."
"Redshift means the galaxy is red." The light is shifted toward longer wavelengths; the galaxy itself isn't red.
"The Doppler effect makes sounds louder and softer." It changes pitch (frequency). Loudness changes because of distance, which is a separate effect.
"The source is making waves faster when it moves." The source emits at a constant rate (Model 1, finger-snapping). Only the spacing the observer receives changes.
"The light from the Big Bang is gone." Energy can't be destroyed. It's still here, stretched into microwaves.
"Longer wavelength = more energy." It's the reverse: longer wavelength means lower frequency and lower energy (ESSRT p. 3 arrows).
"Galaxies and planets stretch as the universe expands." Gravity holds galaxies together. The balloon model is misleading here, because the marker galaxies grow. Name this limitation out loud.

Key vocabulary

Wavelength
Distance from one wave peak to the next.
Frequency
How many peaks pass a point each second. Higher frequency = shorter wavelength.
Doppler effect
A change in the observed wavelength and frequency when the source and observer move toward or away from each other.
Absorption spectrum
A rainbow with dark lines where specific elements absorb light. Each element's lines act like a fingerprint.
Redshift / blueshift
Spectral lines shifted to longer (red) or shorter (blue) wavelengths because of motion away from or toward us.
Big Bang theory
The universe began extremely small, hot and dense about 13.8 billion years ago, and has been expanding and cooling since.
Cosmic microwave background (CMB)
Leftover light from the early universe, stretched into microwaves, coming from every direction.
Conservation of matter and energy
In a closed system, matter and energy change form but the total stays the same.
Nuclear fusion
Light nuclei joining to form heavier ones (H → He in stars), releasing energy.
Theory
An explanation that has survived repeated testing against evidence. It is not a guess.

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Period length. Every plan assumes 50 minutes. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • Do Now / Exit Slip boxes. The packet has boxes for Classes 1–6. On Day 7, students write on the back of the performance-task handout.
  • Six-day version. Combine Days 5 and 6 by assigning Part 2 (the reading and Q1–3) as homework on Day 5 and skipping the Origins 101 video.
  • Simulators need devices. Days 2–3 need one device per pair. If devices are short, project Models 1–4 and have pairs record observations together.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, map, graph or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has. Examples: their Rumors post-it, the siren they heard, their own balloon, or a sticky note from the carousel.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Answer key

How did the universe form? brainstormp. 3 · no single right answer; look for idea + reason

Investigating Light Spectra Q1–3p. 4
1. What similarities or patterns do you notice across all the spectra?

Every spectrum has the same pattern of dark absorption lines with the same spacing, matching the hydrogen and helium laboratory reference. The galaxies and the star contain the same elements as the lab gas.

2. What is different about each spectrum?

The lines sit at different wavelengths. In the galaxies they are moved to the right, toward longer (red) wavelengths. The nearby star is barely shifted, the nearby galaxy is shifted a little (~15–20 nm), the distant galaxy more (~50–60 nm), and the very distant galaxy most (~100 nm or more; some lines move off the red end).

3. Is there a relationship between line wavelength and distance?

Yes. The farther the galaxy, the more its absorption lines are shifted toward longer, redder wavelengths.

Look and listen for: "Like stars in our galaxy, the lines match hydrogen and helium, except they're shifted right, toward red." · "More distant galaxy, bigger shift." · "The light travels through space from the galaxies." · "Something might be happening to the light on the way, and more to light that travels farther." That last idea is a reasonable early hypothesis. Keep it on the wonderings chart.

Sound Waves on the Move

What happens to sound waves when they travel from a moving source to an observer?

Packet pp. 5–8, See-Think-Wonder rows 1–2 (p. 11)PairsSiren video · Model 1 · Model 2Domino DiscoverDevices + audio

By the end of today students should know

  • Wavelength and frequency are inversely related: shorter wavelength means higher frequency.
  • For sound, higher frequency means higher pitch.
  • A moving source emits waves at a constant rate, but the waves bunch up in front of it and spread out behind it. An observer it approaches hears a higher pitch; once it passes, a lower pitch. That is the Doppler effect.
  • The energy in a wave isn't destroyed. It moves outward through the system.

Answer key

Waves intro Q1–2p. 5
1. How are frequency and wavelength related?

They are inversely related. Short wavelength means more peaks pass in the same time (high frequency). Long wavelength means fewer peaks (low frequency).

2. How does galaxy distance relate to the frequency of its light?

More distant galaxies' lines are shifted to longer wavelengths, so their light arrives at a lower frequency. The farther the galaxy, the lower the frequency.

Siren + Model 1pp. 6–7
Siren Q1. What happens to the sound?

The pitch is high as the vehicle approaches, then drops suddenly to a lower pitch as it passes and moves away. It also gets louder, then quieter.

Siren Q2. Initial ideas?

Answers vary: "the motion changes the sound," "the waves get pushed together." Accept any reasonable hypothesis.

Energy Q1. What happens to the energy in the sound wave over time?

It moves outward from the source in all directions and spreads over a larger area, so it gets weaker at any one spot. It is not destroyed; it moves through the system and eventually transfers to the surroundings (air molecules, the receiver).

Q2a. How often is a wave produced? Faster, slower or constant?

Constant. The snapping rhythm doesn't change as the source moves.

Receiver Q1. How often do waves come out of the blue dot?

At a steady, constant rate.

Receiver Q2. How does the sound at the microphone change?

As the dot moves toward the microphone, the snare clicks come faster (higher frequency). After it passes and moves away, they come slower (lower frequency).

Receiver Q3. Why?

The source moves forward between waves, so each new wave starts a little closer to the waves ahead of it. In front, the circles bunch together (shorter wavelength) and reach the microphone more often. Behind, they spread apart (longer wavelength) and reach it less often.

Model 2 + siren explanation + challengep. 8
1. What happens to the sound as frequency increases?

The pitch gets higher.

2. What happens to the wavelength as frequency increases?

The wavelength gets shorter; the peaks are closer together.

How does the frequency the observer perceives change as the source moves? (with evidence)

It increases while the source approaches and decreases once the source moves away. Evidence: in Model 1, the receiver turned green more often as the dot approached and less often after it passed, and the circles were closer together in front of the dot.

How would you explain the change in the siren's sound?

As the siren approaches, its sound waves are bunched together, so we hear a higher frequency and a higher pitch. As it moves away, the waves are spread out, so we hear a lower frequency and a lower pitch. The siren itself never changes its sound.

Challenge: what does the driver hear?

A constant pitch, with no Doppler effect. The driver moves along with the source, so there's no relative motion between them and the waves reach the driver evenly spaced.

Answer key

Model 3 · energy questions + tablepp. 9–10
1. Where does this energy come from?

From the galaxy: the stars in it make light energy through nuclear fusion.

2. Where does this energy go?

It travels outward through space in all directions as light waves. Some reaches the observer on Earth.

3. After it leaves the space in Model 3, where does it go?

It keeps traveling through space until it's absorbed by something (a planet, gas, dust, an eye or a telescope), where it turns into other forms like heat. It isn't destroyed; it moves and changes form.

Model 4 Q1–4p. 10 · note the Q4 typo
1. Color as frequency increases and wavelength decreases?

It shifts from red toward orange, yellow, green, blue and violet.

2. Why does light from galaxies moving closer appear blue in Model 3?

When the galaxy approaches, its light waves are squished together: shorter wavelength, higher frequency. Shorter wavelengths are at the blue end of the spectrum, so the light looks bluer.

3. Color as frequency decreases and wavelength increases?

It shifts toward red.

4. Why does light from galaxies moving away appear blue red in Model 3?

When the galaxy moves away, its light waves are stretched: longer wavelength, lower frequency. Longer wavelengths are at the red end of the spectrum, so the light is redshifted.

Part 4: compare predictions to observationspp. 21–23
1. Does the observed composition support or refute the prediction?

Supports it. The predicted 75% H and 25% He is very close to what we observe today, about 70–74% H and 24–28% He. The universe is still almost all hydrogen and helium, as conservation of matter predicts.

What processes account for the change and the new elements?

Nuclear fusion in stars has turned some hydrogen into helium (a little less H, a little more He) and into heavier elements like carbon and oxygen up to iron. Supernova explosions of massive stars made elements heavier than iron and spread them into space.

2. Murmur of a Bang

Students should notice that scientists predicted in the 1940s that the early universe's light should still be detectable as faint microwave radiation from every direction. The microwaves have much longer wavelengths (about 1 mm vs. about 500 nm, roughly 2,000 times longer) and lower frequency and energy. Yes, it matches: we predicted expansion would stretch the visible light to longer wavelengths, and the CMB is that stretched light, coming from every direction.

4. Do these observations support or refute that matter and energy are never created or destroyed?

Support. The hydrogen and helium from the early universe are still here in nearly the same ratio; only fusion has changed a small amount into other elements. The light energy is still here too, changed from visible light into microwaves but not destroyed.

Claim: All the galaxies and solar systems in the universe formed the same way, from a universe that began small, hot and dense about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since. Because there are so many of them, Earth-like planets, and possibly life, are probably common.

Evidence 1, redshift: galaxy spectra show the same H and He lines as the lab reference, shifted toward red, and more distant galaxies shift more. Because stretched light means a source moving away (Doppler effect, Model 3), the universe is expanding, therefore it used to be much smaller and denser.

Evidence 2, CMB: microwave radiation reaches us from every direction. A hot early universe would have been full of light, and because energy cannot be created or destroyed, that light is still here. Expansion stretched its waves from visible light to microwaves.

Evidence 3, composition: the universe is still about 74% hydrogen and 25% helium, matching the 75 : 25 predicted for the early universe. Because matter is conserved, the mix stayed nearly the same. Fusion and supernovae added a small amount of heavier elements.

Same everywhere: distant galaxies have the same elements and went through the same expansion, cooling, gravity and fusion. Therefore their stars and planets formed the same way ours did. With roughly 10²² stars, most with planets, the chances of Earth-like planets with life are very high.

Rubric quick check: 3+ evidence sources from the 5E ✓ · how waves (stretched) and matter (ratio unchanged) change or stay the same with expansion ✓ · because/therefore logic ✓ · energy and matter conserved ✓ → Proficient.
Look and listen for: the universe started as one incredibly hot, small mass, then suddenly expanded in all directions · the processes that formed our galaxy and solar system happened everywhere, so other galaxies and solar systems must share many characteristics · the universe is bigger than we can imagine · the number of chances for life is extremely high, so life elsewhere seems probable.

Homework · drawn model

Postcard from Another Solar System

On an index card or half sheet, draw the front of a postcard: a solar system in a far-away galaxy, with its star and at least 4 planets (one rocky and in the "just right" zone). On the back, write a short postcard to Earth explaining why this solar system looks so much like ours, using at least two pieces of Big Bang evidence.

  • A front drawing with a labeled star (what it's made of) and 4+ planets, one rocky planet in the habitable zone
  • The back names 2+ pieces of evidence (redshift, CMB, H/He, same spectra) and one process (gravity, fusion, supernova)
  • One sentence on whether life there is certain or probable, and why
  • "I chose…" note: one sticky note your group got in the Idea Carousel that you used to improve your thinking

Keep the best postcards for a bulletin board. They lead straight into the next 5E on solar system stability.
