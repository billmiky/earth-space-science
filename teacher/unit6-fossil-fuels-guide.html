The phenomenon in one paragraph

Around the world, millions of people die early from air pollution made by burning coal, oil and gas, and the deaths are not evenly spread. Students start with images of deaths and emissions and the particulate matter their own classroom collectors caught. They compare India, France and the United States: how much each burns, what pollutants each emits, and where each gets its energy. France makes most of its electricity with nuclear power, so they refine that solution for the U.S. using criteria, constraints and tradeoffs. Then they follow the carbon dioxide: CO₂ is not the pollutant that damages lungs, but a computational carbon cycle model shows it piling up in the atmosphere and surface ocean, and a breath-into-seawater lab shows it lowering pH. Reading about shellfish, omega-3s and red tides links ocean acidification back to human health, and groups build a concept map of the whole system. They evaluate two more solutions, coastal restoration (Elaborate) and Paris's low emission zones refined for NYC (Evaluate), and finish with the En-ROADS simulator and the Driving Question Board.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Name what burning fossil fuels releases: CO₂ plus particulate matter (incl. black carbon), nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide and carbon monoxide.
  • Explain that CO₂ at outdoor levels does not directly harm people, but the other pollutants cause respiratory and cardiovascular disease, and CO₂ harms people indirectly (warming, ocean acidification).
  • Use data to explain why France, India and the U.S. have different pollution: how much they burn and what they burn (France: nuclear electricity; India: coal; U.S.: gas, oil, coal).
  • Refine a solution for a new place using prioritized criteria, constraints (cost, safety, reliability, aesthetics, social/cultural) and tradeoffs (HS-ETS1-3).
  • Use a computational model to show how burning fossil fuels moves carbon from the geosphere to the atmosphere, surface ocean and biosphere (HS-ESS3-6).
  • Explain ocean acidification: more CO₂ → carbonic acid → lower pH → less carbonate for shells → food webs and human food at risk.
  • Say whether a solution stabilizes or destabilizes Earth's systems and evaluate it (nuclear, coastal restoration, low emission zones) (HS-ESS3-4).

Corrections to the packet and teacher guide

p. 68 (and unit plan Student Ideas): "28% of nitrous oxide emissions." It should be nitrogen oxides (NOx), the smog-forming pollutants. Nitrous oxide (N₂O) is a different gas, a greenhouse gas. The NYC DEP page the packet cites says nitrogen oxide.
p. 56: "Over 7.8 million people get 15% of their animal protein from seafood." FAO reports about 3.3 billion people get almost 20% of their animal protein from fish (SOFIA 2020). The point gets stronger, not weaker.
p. 42: "Two new power plants… the other of which is still under construction." These are two new reactors (Units 3 and 4) at Plant Vogtle, Georgia. Unit 3 started in July 2023 and Unit 4 in April 2024; both run now.
p. 42: "While all three were contained…" Chernobyl (1986) spread radioactive fallout across Europe, and Fukushima (2011) released radioactive material and forced long evacuations; only Three Mile Island's release was small. Also, Fukushima was triggered by an earthquake and tsunami, not only mismanagement (though design and safety-oversight failures mattered). The packet's main point, that deaths from nuclear power are small compared with fossil-fuel pollution, still holds.
p. 48, Analyze Q1: asks about "300 years ago," but the model starts in 1850 (~175 years). Read it as "in 1850."
pp. 46–47, Carbon Movement table: in the model the gray natural arrows (photosynthesis 120, respiration 119, dissolution 80, outgassing 78 GtC/yr) are constant. Only the red human arrows and the green absorption arrows change between 1850 and 2024. Tell students so they don't hunt for changes that aren't there.
p. 29, Q1 is garbled: read it as "How do annual CO₂ emissions compare to air pollution deaths from fossil fuels?" The PDF text of Q2 is also scrambled; it should read "While CO₂ does not directly cause health issues, what connection does CO₂ have to pollution that does cause health problems and death?"
p. 66: "by 2030, only cars with green badges… will be allowed in Paris." That was the plan when written; French low-emission-zone rules have since been politically contested and revised. Present 2030 as a goal, and check current rules before calling it settled.
Teacher guide, Explore 1 question "Why does air pollution from fossil fuels cause more deaths in the United States than in France?" The U.S. has about 5× France's population, so totals alone mislead. Push students to compare rates (per 100,000) and per capita emissions, as the packet's note on p. 31 suggests.
p. 50 (and chapter): "30% increase in acidity" is NOAA's rounded figure; the math for 8.2 → 8.1 gives ~26%. Accept either; don't mark students wrong for 26%.
Teacher guide, Evaluate: calls for the handout "Evaluating and Refining Limited Emissions Zones for a New Context," which is not in the SY 26-27 student PDF (p. 68 ends with the NYC text; p. 69 starts Land Use). Prompts are reproduced on Day 10.

Misconceptions to listen for

"CO₂ is the poison in car exhaust." At outdoor levels, CO₂ doesn't hurt your lungs. The harmful ones are PM2.5, NOx, SO₂, CO and ozone. CO₂ harms people indirectly.
"Clouds from nuclear plants are pollution." Cooling towers give off water vapor. Nuclear plants burn nothing; the problem is radioactive waste.
"My carbon footprint is the main problem." Individual choices help, but most emissions come from how a whole country makes electricity and moves people. The carbon footprint idea was popularized by BP in the early 2000s. Day 2 makes this point.
"pH 8.1 means the ocean is acidic." It's still basic (above 7). "Acidification" means moving toward acidic.
"0.1 pH is too small to matter." pH is logarithmic: 0.1 units is ~26–30% more hydrogen ions, enough to make shell-building harder.
"The ocean absorbing CO₂ solves the problem." It slows warming but makes the ocean more acidic. Moving carbon doesn't make it disappear.
"Most biosphere carbon is in trees." In the model's tooltip, soils hold ~1,750 GtC vs. ~550 GtC in plants.
"France has fewer deaths because it's smaller." Compare rates and per capita values, not totals.
"A solution has to fix everything." Air-pollution deaths depend on fuels, health, healthcare access and heat. Solutions are combined; each has tradeoffs.

Key vocabulary

Fossil fuels
Coal, oil and natural gas: carbon stored in the geosphere from ancient living things.
Emissions
Gases and particles released into the air when something burns.
Particulate matter (PM2.5)
Tiny particles that get deep into lungs and blood. Black carbon (soot) is one kind.
NOx · SO₂ · CO
Nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide, carbon monoxide: pollutants almost entirely from burning fossil fuels.
Annual vs. per capita
Whole-country total in a year vs. the average per person.
Carbon footprint
CO₂ released because of one person's (or group's) activities.
Nuclear fission
Splitting atoms to release heat that makes steam and electricity; no burning.
Criteria · constraints · tradeoffs
What success requires · limits the solution must work within · what you give up to get a benefit.
Stabilize / destabilize
Help a system stay in balance / push it out of balance.
Carbon reservoir · flux
A place carbon is stored (GtC) · the rate it moves between places (GtC/yr).
Ocean acidification
Ocean pH dropping as it absorbs CO₂, which forms carbonic acid.
Calcium carbonate
What shells and skeletons are made of; harder to build in more acidic water.
Coastal restoration
Replanting mangroves and seagrass so they store carbon and send basic water to the ocean.
Low emission zone
An area where only cleaner vehicles may drive (Paris uses Crit'Air badges).

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Why 10 days? The teacher materials say 7–9. At 50 minutes, Explain 1 (a long text-based Read-Generate-Sort-Solve plus an Idea Carousel) and the two Explore 2 investigations each need real time, and this is the first time students meet RGSS and Idea Carousel this unit.
  • Eight-day version. (1) Merge Days 2–3: assign the carbon-footprint table as homework and do Making Sense as a 10-minute pair talk. (2) Jigsaw Explain 1 (half of each triad reads France, half reads the U.S., as the teacher materials suggest) and skip the Idea Carousel: groups present posters in 60 seconds each. (3) Do the ocean acidification lab as a teacher demo with one pH meter, and move it onto Day 6 after the model. Keep Explain 2, Elaborate and Evaluate whole.
  • Do Now / Exit Slip boxes. The Google Doc versions of the packet had Class 1–6 (or 1–7) boxes on the cover. The SY 26-27 PDF text shows only "Student Name" on p. 24, so plan on a half-sheet or notebook page.
  • Period length. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • Device-light option. Run the carbon model (Day 6) and En-ROADS (Day 10) from one projector while groups record. The carbon calculator (Day 2) works on phones.
  • Lab safety (Day 7). One straw per student; blow out only, never sip. Seawater is not for drinking.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, flowchart, map or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task uses something only your class has: what your collectors caught, a student's own carbon-calculator result, their group's poster or concept map, their lab pH readings.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, ask 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Answer key

Engage: Death from Fossil Fuelspacket p. 26, Q1–2 · sample responses
1. How do you think these images are related?

They are all linked by burning fossil fuels. Cars and power plants burn coal, oil and gas and emit pollution: particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide and ozone. These pollutants cause health problems like the respiratory and cardiovascular diseases in the Unit Launch, so they cause deaths.

2. What data do we need to evaluate solutions aimed at reducing fossil fuel pollution deaths?

Why some countries have fewer pollution deaths than others (are they already using solutions?); why some places still rely on fossil fuels (social, political, cultural reasons); what the alternatives are; the costs, benefits and tradeoffs of existing solutions; pollution and death rates per person.

Look and listen for (teacher materials): "they're all linked by fossil fuels" · "cars and power plants emit pollution" · "these pollutants cause the same diseases as the Unit Launch" · "we need to know why some countries have fewer deaths" · "we need costs, benefits and tradeoffs." Students will say smog, smoke, gases; name the specific pollutants.
Explore 1 Part 1: mapspacket p. 29, Q1–2 · if reached today
1. How do annual CO₂ emissions compare to air pollution deaths from fossil fuels?

Countries with large annual CO₂ emissions (for example China, India, the U.S.) also tend to have many deaths, so the maps broadly match. The match isn't perfect: the U.S. emits a lot of CO₂ but has far fewer deaths per person than India. How much harmful pollution comes with each ton of CO₂ depends on the fuel (coal is worst) and on pollution controls.

2. While CO₂ does not directly cause health issues, what connection does CO₂ have to pollution that does?

The same burning that releases CO₂ also releases PM2.5, NOx, SO₂ and CO. So CO₂ emissions are a sign of how much fossil fuel is being burned, and therefore of how much harmful pollution is produced.

Think-Talk-Open Exchangepacket pp. 56–57
How does ocean acidification affect biodiversity and human health?

More CO₂ → lower pH → hydrogen ions bind carbonate → less calcium carbonate for shells, so corals, oysters, clams, plankton, urchins, crabs and lobsters struggle; oyster populations have collapsed as larvae die. Food webs that rely on them shrink, so fewer fish. Acidification lowers omega-3 fatty acids in seafood, raising heart disease and inflammation risk. Acidified water can boost toxic red tides that kill fish and release airborne toxins that irritate lungs and worsen asthma.

Fix the number: the reading says "7.8 million people get 15%" of their animal protein from seafood. FAO: about 3.3 billion people get almost 20%.

5. How did comparing inputs and outputs of the model in the past and present help describe how human greenhouse gases change carbon storage in the ocean and biosphere?

In 1850 combustion was 0.05 GtC/yr and nearly nothing extra was absorbed; by 2024 combustion was 9.8 GtC/yr and the ocean and land were absorbing ~2.9 and ~3.1 GtC/yr extra. The comparison shows that human CO₂ is piling up in the surface ocean (900 → ~1,081 GtC) and being taken up by plants, faster than natural processes can balance it.

6. How did the computational model support your claims about human impacts on carbon movement?

It put numbers on each reservoir and flow, so we could show which ones changed and by how much (e.g., the atmosphere gained ~320 GtC, fossil fuels lost ~500 GtC), and test what would happen with different emissions. That is stronger evidence than a description alone.

7. What role do human technologies play in stabilizing or destabilizing natural systems, and how does that affect biodiversity and human health?

Fossil-fuel technologies destabilize systems by moving geosphere carbon into the air and ocean and releasing pollutants, which harms biodiversity (shell-builders, food webs) and health. Technologies that replace or reduce burning (nuclear, renewables, cleaner vehicles) or restore carbon sinks can help stabilize them, though each has its own costs and risks.

Steps 1–4packet pp. 63–64 · strong group responses
Step 1. Use a model to analyze the solution

Atmosphere: less CO₂ (plants take it up; carbon buried in sediment). Hydrosphere: higher coastal pH, so less acidification; carbon stored in ocean chemistry for over 1,000 years. Biosphere: more habitat for fish and shellfish; more biodiversity. Human health: more stable seafood supply and nutrition, fewer red tides, less flooding and erosion.

Burning fossil fuels moved carbon from the geosphere to the atmosphere; the ocean absorbs part of it and becomes more acidic, harming shell-builders, food webs and people who rely on seafood. Step 3. Refine the solution

Tradeoffs: lower cost than engineered carbon removal, but slower and less certain; land at the coast can't be used for other things. Refinement: train and pay local communities and students to plant and monitor (citizen science); choose sites with the best survival odds; pair with flood-protection budgets so cities help fund it; test model predictions at a few pilot sites before scaling up.

Step 4. Evaluate the refinement

Solves the problem by capturing carbon and offsetting acidity while protecting coasts. Impacts systems by stabilizing ocean chemistry and increasing biodiversity. Costs fall if local communities share the work; safety is high (no hazardous materials); environmental impacts are mostly positive, but survival is not guaranteed and it does not reduce air pollution.

How does this solution impact communities globally?

Less CO₂ in the atmosphere helps everyone by slowing warming and acidification; healthier fisheries protect people worldwide who depend on seafood, especially in coastal and island nations. But one site is small, so it only works at scale and together with cutting emissions.
