The phenomenon in one paragraph

In 2005 the North Atlantic had a record season, and almost every storm formed between June 1 and November 30, the same window as 2018 and 2020. Students first map where and when tropical cyclones happen worldwide: six regions, all in the tropics of both hemispheres, each with its season in its own summer and fall. Using Columbia's IRI ocean-temperature model they see that those places and months are where the ocean surface is warmest (about 26.5 °C / 80 °F or more). They then explain the pattern at the molecular scale: warm water molecules move faster, so more evaporate; warm, moist air is less dense and rises; the vapor condenses and releases latent heat, which powers the storm. In Elaborate, Hurricane Helene shows that the deadliest part of a hurricane is often rain, and warmer air holds more water vapor. In Evaluate, students weigh real trend data (frequency is unclear, the share of Category 4–5 storms is rising) and IPCC climate-model projections, think about model assumptions and reliability, and write an argument about hurricanes where they live.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Name the six tropical cyclone regions and say that storms form over tropical oceans in both hemispheres, but almost never right on the equator.
  • Explain that each hemisphere's hurricane season falls in its own summer and fall (North Atlantic: June 1–November 30; Southern Hemisphere regions: about October–May).
  • Use sea surface temperature data to show that hurricanes form where and when the water is warmest (about 26.5 °C / 80 °F or more).
  • Model the molecular mechanism: faster molecules → more evaporation → warm moist air is less dense and rises → cooling → condensation releases latent heat → lower pressure, stronger winds.
  • Write a scientific explanation (question, claim, evidence, science concepts, reasoning) and name the independent (sea surface temperature) and dependent (hurricane occurrence) variables.
  • Explain why the Category (wind only) misses rain and flooding, and why warmer air means heavier rain (~7% more water vapor per °C).
  • Separate frequency from intensity: evidence for more intense storms is much stronger than for more storms.
  • Describe how model assumptions (emissions, growth, technology, policy) limit precision and reliability, and why the IPCC shows several scenarios.

Corrections to the packet and teacher materials

Investigative phenomenon (unit plan): "hurricanes occurred … between June and November 30, just like the 2018 and 2020." Close, not exact: NHC records Alberto forming May 25, 2018, Arthur May 16 and Bertha May 27, 2020, and 2005's Zeta on Dec 30. Say "almost all" storms form in the season.
Packet header: says "Unit 4" (the Drive file is "4.3 Student Materials"). It is the Unit 5 Hurricanes 5E.
Evaluate Part 2 intro: "reviewing some ideas about blizzard formation" should be hurricane formation.
Evaluate Part 1 title: "trends in tropical cyclone and winter storm occurrences": this part has no winter storm data. Tell students it is hurricanes only.
Evaluate Part 1 text: "The Earth warmed one degree Fahrenheit over the 20th Century" is an outdated (2001-era) figure. IPCC AR6: 1.09 °C (about 2 °F) for 2011–2020 compared with 1850–1900.
Evaluate Part 1 graph caption: says 1990–2020; the bars run 1990–2021 (Klotzbach et al. 2022).
Elaborate map: credited to "weather network"; the data are from the Fifth National Climate Assessment (2023). The 37% (Southeast) is for the heaviest 1% of all rainy days, not hurricane rain only. Accept it for Q1, but name the difference.
Elaborate text: Florence "dropped up to 30 inches." NHC and NWS list 35.93 in at Elizabethtown, NC, the state record. The packet understates it.
Reliability text: "Precision refers to the degree of accuracy and consistency." Precision is consistency (same result each time); accuracy is closeness to the true value. A scale that reads 105 g every time for a 100 g apple is precise but not accurate.
IPCC Atlas text: "warming scenarios of 1.5, 2, 3 and 4 °C by the year 2100." In the Atlas these are global warming levels, not dates; when each level is reached depends on the emissions scenario.
IPCC Atlas See-Think-Wonder: asks about sea surface temperature, but the Atlas opens on air "mean temperature." Have students switch the variable to Sea surface temperature (Ocean) or tell them they are reading air temperature.
Numbering: the variables questions, the Summary Task and the Elaborate questions are all numbered "1." (Google Docs reset). This guide uses 1–3, 1, 2, 3, 4a, 4b and 1–4, as the chapter does.
PTO (SY 25-26): the first box titled "Connect to the Performance Task: Hurricanes 5E" is really the Blizzards 5E box (its prompts are about winter storms). The real Hurricanes 5E box is the last one in the PTO.
Formation wording: "rising air spirals, creating the eye." Air rises in the eyewall (strongest winds); in the eye air sinks, skies clear and pressure is lowest. Same issue in SY 26-27 Wind 5E p. 59 ("this column is the eye").
Older teacher guides (SY 23-24): "hurricanes in the … southern hemisphere during winter." The SY 25-26 unit plan has it right: the Southern Hemisphere's season is during the Northern Hemisphere's winter, which is the Southern Hemisphere's summer.
SY 26-27 student p. 67 (Water 5E Engage): "Hurricane Helene dropped 30 inches of water on Asheville." NHC's highest total was 30.78 in at Busick (Yancey County, NC), northeast of Asheville; the Asheville area got roughly half that (the SY 25-26 NPR text: "more than 15 inches"). Same page: Jonas "30 inches of snow in New England": the photo is Washington, DC, not New England.
SY 26-27 student p. 59: hurricanes "tend to originate from the west coast of Africa." That is true of Cape Verde storms, but many form in the Caribbean or Gulf; Helene itself formed over the northwestern Caribbean (NHC).
SY 26-27 teacher guide, Water 5E Explain step 3: hand out "Explaining Lunar Patterns"; it should be Explaining Precipitation Formation. The Unit Standards page calls this a "Biology" course.

Misconceptions to listen for

"Hurricanes form right on the equator, where it's hottest." Almost none form within ~5° of the equator: Earth's spin can't start the air rotating there.
"Hurricane season is the same everywhere." Each hemisphere has its season in its own summer and fall.
"Hurricanes get their energy from the wind (or the Sun, or lightning)." The fuel is warm ocean water: evaporation stores energy in vapor, condensation releases it.
"Water has to boil to evaporate." The fastest molecules escape at any temperature; warmer water has more of them.
"Clouds are water vapor." Vapor is invisible. Clouds are tiny liquid droplets (or ice) made when vapor condenses.
"Heat rises." Warm, moist air rises because it is less dense than the air around it.
"Condensation cools the air." Condensation releases heat; that is what powers the storm.
"A Category 1 is a weak storm." Category measures wind only. Florence (Cat 1) set North Carolina's rain record.
"Global warming means many more hurricanes." The number is uncertain; stronger storms and heavier rain are what the evidence supports.
"Hurricanes don't hit New York." The 1938 hurricane made landfall on Long Island as a Category 3; Sandy (2012) flooded the subways and tunnels.

Key vocabulary

Tropical cyclone
A rotating low-pressure storm that forms over warm tropical water. Called a hurricane (Atlantic, E Pacific), typhoon (W Pacific) or cyclone (Indian Ocean, S Pacific) once winds reach 74 mph.
Sea surface temperature (SST)
Temperature of the top layer of the ocean.
Evaporation
Fast-moving molecules leave liquid water and become vapor, carrying energy with them.
Condensation
Vapor molecules slow down and join into droplets, releasing energy (latent heat).
Latent heat
Energy stored in water vapor and released when it condenses.
Density
How much mass is packed into a space. Less dense air rises.
Eye / eyewall
Calm, clear center with the lowest pressure / ring of tall clouds and strongest winds around it.
Frequency vs. intensity
How many storms vs. how strong they are.
Independent / dependent variable
The cause that changes / the effect that responds.
Saffir-Simpson scale
Categories 1–5 by sustained wind speed only.
Computational model
A computer program that uses data and math to represent a system (IRI, IPCC Atlas).
Precision · accuracy · reliability
Consistent results · close to the true value · trustworthy over time and in new conditions.
Assumption · scenario
An idea a model is built on · one possible future based on a set of assumptions.

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Why 7 days? The SY 25-26 unit plan lists 5–6 days, but the packet has 7 Do Now/Exit Slip boxes, and the IRI model and IPCC Atlas each need real device time.
  • Five-day version. Day 1: video + quick Post-it ideas (skip Rumors), assign the bar graph as homework. Day 2: When Q1–3, IRI model, See-Think-Wonder. Day 3: video, diagram, Part 1, start Part 2. Day 4: finish Part 2, variables, Elaborate Q1–4 as homework. Day 5: Evaluate Parts 1–2 as a jigsaw (half the pairs do trends, half do the Atlas), then the PTO.
  • Period length. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • Device-light option. Project the IRI model and the Atlas and click through as a class; students record. The chapter has working versions of both with the same data.
  • Where this sits. Students come in from the Paths of Severe Storms 5E (global winds, why storms travel the way they do). Use that: hurricanes ride the trade winds west, then curve north and east in the westerlies.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a map, comic, flowchart or diagram. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has: the rumor a student ended up holding, their own colored warm-water map, their group's poster, the region they picked in the Atlas.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

See: What patterns do you notice about where these storms are taking place?

Storms cluster in the tropics on both sides of the equator, in six regions (East Pacific, West Pacific, North Atlantic, North Indian, South Indian, Southwest Pacific). If asked: the South Atlantic and southeast Pacific have cooler water and strong upper winds (wind shear), so storms rarely form there.

How Many, When, and Where's the Warm Water?

What patterns do we see in global occurrence of hurricanes?

By the end of today students should know

When do hurricanes happen? Q1–3Explore

1. Which regions have hurricane season at about the same time? Which regions have similar timing?

Two groups. April/May/June to November–January: East Pacific, West Pacific, North Atlantic, North Indian. October to May: Southwestern Pacific and South Indian.

2. In terms of location, what do regions with similar seasons have in common?

They are on the same side of the equator: the first group is in the Northern Hemisphere, the second in the Southern Hemisphere.

3. In which seasons are hurricanes most common?

Summer and fall of each hemisphere (late summer/early fall peak; Atlantic peak ~Sept 10).

Where's the warm water? Map + questionExplore · IRI model steps 1–4

Map (June–August vs December–February)

Jun–Aug: warmest water (≥ ~28 °C) in a belt from about the equator to ~25–30° N: western Pacific warm pool, Caribbean/Gulf of Mexico, tropical Atlantic, Bay of Bengal/Arabian Sea, eastern Pacific off Mexico. Dec–Feb: the belt shifts south: warm water from the equator to ~20–25° S off Australia, Madagascar and the South Pacific islands; the Gulf of Mexico and North Atlantic cool.

How does the warm water during the Northern Hemisphere's summer and winter relate to the times of year that hurricanes occur most frequently in different regions?

Each region's hurricane season happens when the water there is warmest: Northern Hemisphere regions in Jun–Nov when their water is warm; Southern Hemisphere regions in Oct–May (their summer, our winter) when their water is warm.

Part 2: Scientific ExplanationExplain · sample Proficient response

Question

What causes hurricane occurrence near the equator between June 1 and November 30?

Claim

Hurricanes occur near the equator from June 1 to November 30 because that is where and when Northern Hemisphere ocean water (and the air above it) is warmest, warm enough (about 26.5 °C / 80 °F) to supply the energy hurricanes need.

Evidence (any two or more)

2005 storms formed over red (warmest) water in the tropics, June–November, and died over cooler water. The IRI model shows the warmest water near the equator in the NH in Jun–Aug and in the SH in Dec–Feb. NH regions have their seasons Apr/Jun–Nov/Jan; SH regions Oct–May, when their water is warmest.

Science concepts

Warmer water has more fast-moving molecules, so more evaporate. Warm, moist air is less dense, so it rises. Rising air cools; vapor condenses and releases latent heat, warming the air so it rises faster, lowering surface pressure and strengthening winds.

Reasoning

Because warm water causes more evaporation, it supplies more vapor to rise and condense. Therefore enough energy is released for hurricanes to form only where and when the water is warmest. When storms move north over cooler water, evaporation drops and they lose their energy.

Constructing a causal relationship: 1–3Explain · packet numbers these "1., 1., 1."

1. Dependent variable

Hurricane occurrence (frequency) near the equator between June 1 and November 30.

2. Independent variable(s)

Sea surface temperature (accept: ocean temperature; air temperature over the ocean; time of year/latitude as stand-ins for SST).

3. Relationship

As sea surface temperature increases above about 26.5 °C, hurricane occurrence increases, because warmer water causes more evaporation and more latent heat release that powers storms.

2. How have we used mechanisms at a molecular scale to figure out what causes hurricane occurrence near the equator between June 1 and Nov 30?

We followed water molecules: in warm water they move faster and more evaporate; vapor rises in less dense air, condenses and releases latent heat. That small-scale mechanism explains the big pattern: storms form only where and when water is warm enough to supply the energy.

3. Main ideas from the formation diagram, and how they helped explain location and time of year

Hurricanes need warm water (> 80 °F), rising humid air, converging winds at the surface and outflow aloft, light winds around them. Energy comes from evaporation and condensation. Since warm water is in the tropics during each hemisphere's summer and fall, that's where and when hurricanes form.

The dependent variable is the effect I'm explaining (hurricane occurrence); the independent variable is the cause that changes it (SST).

Answer key

IPCC Atlas See-Think-WonderEvaluate Part 2 · sample responses

See: What do you notice about the average sea surface temperature?

It rises in every hurricane region and season selected; more in high-emission scenarios (SSP5-8.5) and later periods, less in low ones (SSP1-2.6). The shaded band shows models disagree somewhat.

Think: What does this make you think about how frequent and intense hurricanes will be?

More energy available: storms that form can become more intense and wetter. Frequency is less certain (more warm area, but the threshold and wind shear can change too).

Wonder (sample)

Which regions change most? Will NYC's water get warm enough to keep storms strong? How reliable is the model?

Reliability of Models Based on Assumptions, Q1–2Evaluate

1. How much variability do you think there can be in the assumptions you read about? Explain.

A lot. Emissions, population and economic growth, technology, policy and land use all depend on human choices that haven't been made yet; feedbacks (permafrost, clouds) are also uncertain.

2. Why does the IPCC WGI Interactive Atlas include different warming levels (1.5, 2, 3, 4 °C)?

Because assumptions vary, a range shows how outcomes depend on our choices and how confident we can be. Each is an "if-then": if warming reaches X, then expect Y. (They are warming levels, not "by 2100" dates.) For scale: SSP1-2.6 → ~1.8 °C and SSP5-8.5 → ~4.4 °C by 2081–2100 (IPCC AR6).

PTO: Connect to the Performance Task: Hurricanes 5ETask 1 or Task 2 · Parts 1–3 · sample for NYC

Part 1: variables (both tasks)

Independent: global temperature (linked to North Atlantic sea surface temperature). Dependent: frequency (Task 1) or intensity (Task 2) of hurricanes where you live. "Where you live" is the place, not a variable.

Part 2: evidence table (examples)

Cat 4–5 storms 171 → 269 globally and 16 → 25 in the North Atlantic (Webster table) · share of Cat 4–5 rising (Klotzbach graph d) · global count flat or slightly down 1990–2021 (graph a) · North Atlantic frequency up 1980–2018 (NOAA map) · SST projected to rise where hurricanes form (IPCC Atlas) · heaviest-day rain +60% in the Northeast (NCA5 map).

Part 2: model

Must show energy and matter flow: higher global temperature → warmer ocean → faster molecules, more evaporation → more vapor rises (less dense) → condensation releases more latent heat → lower pressure, stronger winds and more rain. Include molecules at the surface and in the cloud; for NYC, note storms travel north over cooler water before arriving.

Part 3: Task 2 (intensity), Proficient sample

Claim: Hurricane intensity near NYC will likely increase as global temperatures rise. Evidence: North Atlantic Cat 4–5 storms rose from 16 to 25; the Atlas projects warmer Jun–Nov sea surface temperatures in the North Atlantic. Reasoning: because warmer water evaporates more and condensation releases more latent heat, therefore storms gain more energy and wind, and warmer air holds more vapor, so they drop more rain. Counterclaim: part of the increase may come from better satellite measurement, and the water off NYC is cooler, so storms usually weaken before arriving; still, warmer water along the path means they weaken less. Evaluation: name the scenario used, the model's assumptions (emissions, policy), and evidence that would help (local SST trends off NYC).

Part 3: Task 1 (frequency)

"Stay about the same or uncertain" is best supported (global counts flat; IPCC projects global frequency to decrease or stay the same). "Increase" is defensible for the North Atlantic (NOAA map) if the student handles the global counterclaim.

Final Argument Rubric: what Level 4 looks likeEvaluate
  • Claim/Counterclaim: both address the prompt for their location and are accurate.
  • Evidence: at least two pieces for the claim, from data or models (science ideas are not evidence).
  • Reasoning, concepts: evaporation, density, condensation/latent heat, energy flow; none missing.
  • Reasoning, logic: "because… therefore…" linking evidence to claim.
  • Evaluation: strengths and weaknesses, additional evidence needed, and how cause and effect or systems models were used.
Developing usually means: claim with only one piece of evidence; "it's getting hotter" with no molecules; no counterclaim; frequency and intensity mixed up.
