Coevolution of Earth & Life

What set of conditions allowed intelligent life to evolve from single, simple cells?

Tap a layer to open that day. Each day has a minute-by-minute plan, Do Nows, conferring questions, checks for understanding, the answer key, exit slips and drawn homework.

The phenomenon in one paragraph

After life appeared on Earth, it took about 3.5 billion years to go from single, simple cells to intelligent humans, and almost all complex life showed up in the last billion years. Students find out why. The story is oxygen. Photosynthesizing microbes slowly added oxygen to the oceans. The oxygen first rusted dissolved iron (banded iron formations), then built up in the air (red beds, ozone). That oxygen made aerobic respiration possible, which allowed complex cells, then many-celled life, then life on land. Living things changed the planet, and the changed planet changed what could live. That two-way relationship is coevolution. It explains why intelligent life took so long, and it sets up the next question: how likely is it to happen elsewhere?

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain that Earth's early atmosphere had almost no free oxygen: first hydrogen and helium, then methane, ammonia, water vapor and nitrogen by 3.8 bya. Today's air is 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and 1% other gases.
  • Identify photosynthetic microbes (stromatolites ~2.7 bya, cyanobacteria ~2.1 bya) as the source of Earth's oxygen.
  • Use banded iron formations (~2.5 bya) and red beds (~2 bya) as rock evidence that oxygen was present. Iron + oxygen → rust (iron oxide).
  • Connect rising oxygen to aerobic respiration and more complex life: eukaryotes (~2.1 bya), multicellular organisms (~600 mya), animals (~560 mya), land plants and animals (~450–425 mya).
  • Give examples of life changing Earth's systems: oxygen in the air, rust in rocks, soil formation, ozone. Give examples of Earth's systems changing life: nutrients from the Rodinia breakup, shallow equatorial seas → swamps, mass extinctions.
  • Explain why Earth's history is hard to reconstruct: the rock cycle and plate tectonics destroy old rock, microfossils are tiny, and non-living structures can look like life. Explain why the choice of isotope (half-life and dating range) matters.
  • Tell correlation (events happen at the same time) apart from causation (a mechanism links them). Write a claim–evidence–reasoning argument for coevolution.
  • Describe mass extinctions as drops in oxygen and big changes to Earth's systems that were negative (species lost) and positive (open niches, new groups such as mammals). Plant recovery brings oxygen back.

Misconceptions to listen for

"Earth always had oxygen." Free O₂ was near 0% until ~2.4 bya. Life made it.
"Evolution happens at a steady pace." Life changed slowly for billions of years, then very fast in the last billion as oxygen rose.
"Animals or trees made the first oxygen." Ocean microbes did, 2+ billion years before plants.
"Oxygen showed up in the air right after photosynthesis started." No. It reacted with dissolved iron first (banded iron formations). Only after that could it build up in the air (red beds).
"Carbon-14 dates everything old." C-14 only works from 100 to 70,000 years. 200,000-year-old human fossils need K-40 (Day 3, Q3).
"After 2 half-lives it's all gone." Half of the remaining amount decays each time: 100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5%.
"If two things happen at the same time, one caused the other." That is correlation. Causation needs a mechanism, like iron + O₂ → rust.
"Extinctions are only bad." They open niches. Mammals took over after the dinosaurs.

Key vocabulary

Coevolution
Two things evolving together, each causing change in the other: here, life and Earth's systems.
Photosynthesis
Light + water + CO₂ → glucose + oxygen.
Aerobic respiration
Using oxygen to release energy from food. It is far more efficient than doing it without oxygen.
Eukaryote / prokaryote
Complex cells with a nucleus / simple cells without one.
Banded iron formation
Ocean rock with red iron-oxide (rust) layers. The first oxygen in the rock record.
Red beds
Land sediments turned red by oxygen in the air. Evidence of atmospheric O₂.
Stromatolite
Layered structure built by mats of microbes.
Half-life
The time for half of a radioactive parent isotope to decay.
Biosignature
Any sign that life was present: fossils, chemicals, or changes life made to its environment.
Feedback
A change in one system causes a change in another, which then causes more change in the first.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, map, graph or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil only. No printed or digitally made images, which makes copy-pasting AI output useless.
  • Every task references something only your class has. Examples: their group's timeline arrows, the nail demo result, or their own Day 1 brainstorm.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud. If they can't explain it, the work wasn't theirs.

The Pace of Evolution

How has the pace of evolution changed over the past 4 billion years?

Answer key

Pace of Evolution Q1–6p. 2–3 · accept reasonable readings of the diagram (±0.3 billion years)
1. When did the Earth form?

About 4.6 billion years ago (the diagram shows ~4.5–4.6).

2. How long did it take for the first signs of life to appear?

Prokaryotes appear around 4–3.8 bya, so roughly 0.6–0.8 billion years (600–800 million years) after Earth formed.

3. After simple cells appeared, how long until complex cells (with nuclei)?

Eukaryotes appear ~2 bya, so about 2 billion years after the first simple cells.

4. After complex cells appeared, how long until cells that live clustered together?

Multicellular life (sponges/fungi) appears ~1 bya, so about 1 billion years.

5. After clustered cells, how long until animals like jellyfish and worms?

Cambrian explosion ~0.54 bya, so about 400–500 million years.

6. How much change has occurred in the last billion years?

Most of it. Sponges and fungi, corals, the Cambrian explosion, fish, insects, plants on land, amphibians, reptiles, dinosaurs, mammals, birds, flowers, primates and humans all appeared in the last billion years. The pace sped up dramatically.

Do Now options

Option A · Order it

Put these in order from oldest to newest: dinosaurs, first cells, first land plants, humans, first animals.

Answer: first cells → first animals → first land plants → dinosaurs → humans.
Option B · Spheres

What is the difference between the biosphere and the geosphere? Give one example of each.

Biosphere = all living things (e.g. algae). Geosphere = rocks, soil, minerals and the solid Earth (e.g. a red rock layer).

Its position in rock layers (deeper = older, relative dating) and radioactive (radiometric) dating of nearby rock.

Answer key

Part 1 · Analysis Q1–6pp. 4–5
1. Which events impacted other events? (arrows)

Strong arrows (★ = crosses the midline):

  • ★ Stromatolite photosynthesis (2.7 bya) → Banded iron (2.5): O₂ reacted with dissolved iron → rust layers.
  • ★ Cyanobacteria / oxygen → Red beds (2 bya): O₂ in the air weathered rock red.
  • ★ Banded iron / rising O₂ → Eukaryotic cells (2.1): aerobic respiration needs oxygen.
  • ★ Rodinia breakup (~1 bya) → Multicellular algae: erosion added nutrients to the ocean.
  • ★ Multicellular algae (oxygen burst) → BIF recurrence (750 mya): more O₂ formed more iron oxide.
  • Multicellular algae → Multicellular organisms (600) → Soft animals (560) → Ocean diversity (518): more O₂ → more energy → more complex life.
  • ★ Land plants (457) / vascular plants (420) → Preserved soil (407): roots and microbes made soil.
  • ★ Soil → more vascular plants: soil supported bigger plants.
  • Land plants → Oldest land animal (425): food and oxygen on land.
  • ★ Equatorial land and shallow water (356) → Swamp plants (300): good conditions for plant growth.
  • Swamp plants → 21-inch dragonfly (300): high O₂ allowed giant insects.
2. How do Earth's systems seem to affect life?

Earth's systems set the conditions life can live in. When Rodinia broke up (~1 bya), erosion added nutrients to the oceans, and multicellular algae became abundant. Land and shallow seas near the equator (~356 mya) made swamps where plants thrived. Once oxygen built up in the oceans and atmosphere, organisms could use aerobic respiration. That allowed eukaryotes (2.1 bya), multicellular life (600 mya) and animals (560 mya).

3. How does life seem to affect Earth's systems?

Life changed the atmosphere and the rocks. Photosynthetic microbes released oxygen, which rusted iron into banded iron formations (2.5 bya) and weathered land into red beds (2 bya). Plants and microbes on land created soil (407 mya). Plants raised oxygen to its peak around 300 mya.

4. How easy is it to tell what the fossils show without captions?

Very hard. Many are microscopic (cyanobacteria, eukaryotic cells) and look like dots or smudges. Stromatolites and banded iron just look like striped rock. Scientists need microscopes, chemical tests and context such as the rock layer and its age to identify them.

5. What evidence supports your conclusions? Correlative or causative? What else would you need?

The evidence is dated fossils and rocks placed in order. Most of it is correlative: it shows events happened one after another, not that one caused the other. Some links have a known mechanism that makes them more causal, e.g. iron only turns to iron oxide when oxygen is present, as in the nail demo. To strengthen the conclusions we'd need more samples from many locations, more precise dates, chemical signatures of life, and experiments showing the mechanisms.

6. Why is it hard to make a complete timeline of geosphere and biosphere changes?

The rock cycle keeps destroying and changing old rock. It gets eroded, melted, or metamorphosed by heat and pressure. Plate tectonics subducts crust back into the mantle. So very old rock (like the 4-billion-year-old Acasta Gneiss) is rare, and fossils inside it get damaged or erased. Soft-bodied and microscopic organisms rarely fossilize at all, which leaves big gaps.

2a. About how much U-235 remains in a 2.5-billion-year-old sample?

2.5 billion is between 3 half-lives (2.139 billion, 12.5%) and 4 (2.852 billion, 6.25%). That leaves roughly 9% (exact ≈ 8.8%). Accept any answer between 6.25% and 12.5% with reasoning.

2b. Why is it harder to determine how much Rb-87 remains?

Rb-87's half-life (48.8 billion years) is far longer than 2.5 billion years. Not even one half-life has passed, so about 96–97% of the Rb-87 is still there. The change is too small to read from the table and hard to measure precisely.

2c. Advantage of a shorter half-life?

More of the isotope decays during the sample's lifetime, so the change is bigger and easier to measure. That gives a more precise age, as long as some parent isotope is still left.

3. Which isotope dates early human fossils (~200,000 years)?

Potassium-40. Its useful dating range is 100,000 to 4.6 billion years, which includes 200,000. Carbon-14 only works from 100 to 70,000 years, so 200,000 years is out of its range. K-40 dates volcanic rock (tuff or lava) in the layers above and below the fossils.

Expect "Carbon-14." Many students know C-14 is used for "old bones." Ask them to find 200,000 in the range column. If a student picks C-14, grade the Q4 table on correct half-life math (5,730 · 11,460 · 17,190 · 22,920 years) and the Q3 reasoning separately.

Answer key

Part 3 · Oxygen graph Q1–7pp. 8–9
1. When did oxygen start to increase from nearly 0%?

About 2.4–2.5 billion years ago, at the start of stage 2 (the Great Oxidation Event).

2. When did oxygen start to increase sharply?

About 0.85 billion years ago (850 mya), in stage 4, continuing to ~0.54 bya.

3. When did oxygen reach its highest levels?

About 0.3 billion years ago (~300 mya), at roughly 30–35% of the atmosphere, higher than today's 21%.

4. How does this graph compare with your timeline?

They match. Photosynthesis (2.7 bya) comes just before oxygen first rises (~2.4 bya), and banded iron (2.5) and red beds (2.0) show up as oxygen rises. Eukaryotes (2.1) appear after the first rise. Oxygen stays low and flat for a long time while life stays mostly single-celled. The sharp rise ~850–540 mya lines up with abundant algae, the Rodinia breakup, recurring BIFs, then multicellular life (600), soft animals (560) and the Cambrian (518). The peak ~300 mya matches swamp plants and the giant dragonfly.

5. Why did oxygen increase so sharply 2.1–2.4 bya?

Photosynthetic microbes (stromatolites 2.7 bya, cyanobacteria) had been releasing oxygen for hundreds of millions of years. At first it reacted with iron dissolved in the oceans, forming banded iron formations (2.5 bya), so it didn't build up. Once that iron was used up and the microbes kept multiplying, the extra O₂ escaped into the atmosphere. The red beds (2 bya) are evidence of that.

6. How did the rise in oxygen affect the evolution of complex organisms?

Oxygen made aerobic respiration possible, which releases far more energy from food. More energy allowed bigger, more complex cells (eukaryotes, 2.1 bya) and later multicellular organisms (600 mya) and animals (560 mya). An ozone layer formed from the oxygen and blocked UV, which let life move onto land (~450 mya). High O₂ around 300 mya even allowed giant insects.

7. How did the rise in complex organisms affect the planet?

Complex organisms, especially plants, changed Earth's systems. They added more O₂ to the air (the peak at ~300 mya), created soil (407 mya), sped up weathering of rock, built reefs, and formed swamps whose buried plants removed CO₂. Life became a force that reshapes the planet.

Part 2 · Exemplar argumentp. 13 · grade with the five-element checklist

Claim: The evolution of complex organisms relied on the coevolution of the biosphere and Earth's systems.

When Earth formed 4.6 bya, its atmosphere was mostly hydrogen and helium, and by 3.8 bya it was methane, ammonia, water vapor and nitrogen, with almost no oxygen ①. Today it is 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen. Life made that difference. Stromatolite fossils with evidence of photosynthesis date to 2.7 bya, and cyanobacteria microfossils to 2.1 bya ②. Photosynthesis releases oxygen. That oxygen changed Earth's systems: banded iron formations dated to 2.5 bya show oxygen reacting with iron in the oceans, and red beds from 2 bya show oxygen in the air weathering rock on land ③. The oxygen then changed life. Eukaryotic cells, which use oxygen for efficient aerobic respiration, appear by 2.1 bya, and after oxygen rose sharply around 800 mya, multicellular organisms (600 mya) and animals (560 mya) appear ④. This is feedback: microbes changed the oceans and atmosphere, those changes allowed more complex life, and that life, especially plants, added even more oxygen and built soil, which supported more life on land ⑤. Complex life could not evolve until life had first changed the planet.

Reflection questionsp. 15
How does the variation or uncertainty in the data affect its usefulness?

Uncertainty makes the evidence less precise but still useful when several lines agree. The coral reef card spans 470–548 mya, a range of almost 80 million years, so we can't tell exactly what came first near the Cambrian. The oxygen graph has a "?" around 3 bya, and the extinction graph shows a wide shaded uncertainty band. As the reading says, folded rocks can look like stromatolites, and very old rocks have been deformed and weathered. Single pieces of evidence can mislead. Patterns that show up across fossils, rocks and chemistry are much more trustworthy.

How helpful is the data for determining causal or correlational relationships?

The dates alone only show correlation, meaning events happened in order. For example, eukaryotes appear after oxygen rises, but timing alone doesn't prove oxygen caused them. The data become more useful for causation when combined with known mechanisms: iron + oxygen → rust explains the banded iron formations, and aerobic respiration explains why complex cells need oxygen. Because there are gaps in the record, some links (like Rodinia's breakup → algae bloom) remain likely explanations rather than proven causes.

Summary Task Q1–3p. 16 · use it to see each student's thinking
1. How does the stability of Earth's systems, or changes to them, impact life?

When oxygen levels stayed low and stable for long periods (~2–0.8 bya), life stayed mostly simple and changed slowly. When oxygen increased (~2.4 bya and ~0.8–0.5 bya), new, more complex life appeared quickly. When oxygen dropped suddenly, during mass extinctions, many species died. Changes in Earth's systems drive changes in life.

2. Much of science deals with explaining how things change and how they remain stable. Support with examples.

In this investigation we explained why oxygen stayed near 0% for a billion years after life began (iron absorbed it), then rose, then stayed steady. We explained why life stayed single-celled for ~2 billion years and then diversified quickly. In earlier units we explained how the solar system formed and became stable, and how Earth's surface keeps changing through plate tectonics and the rock cycle.

3. Why discuss factors affecting data quality? Give one example.

Because a conclusion is only as strong as its evidence. If the evidence is incomplete or uncertain, the argument should say so, or readers may trust it more than they should. Examples: the rock cycle has destroyed most very old rocks; microfossils are tiny and hard to identify; the coral reef date spans 80 million years; using the wrong isotope (C-14 for old rock) gives no useful date.

  • The five major mass extinctions (Ordovician 444, Devonian 360, Permian 252, Triassic 201, K–Pg 66 mya) wiped out 75–90% of species.
  • Each involved big changes to Earth's systems (volcanism, glaciation, warming, ocean acidification, an asteroid) and coincided with a drop in atmospheric oxygen.
  • Extinctions were negative (lineages lost) and positive (open niches → new groups, such as dinosaurs after the Permian and mammals after K–Pg).
  • Recovery depends on plants. Regrowing photosynthesizers bring oxygen back up, which supports the return of animal life.

Answer key

Extinction events and oxygen questionp. 19
What impact could organism recovery after an extinction event have on oxygen levels?

After an extinction, surviving plants, algae and phytoplankton spread into the empty space. More photosynthesizers make more oxygen, so atmospheric O₂ rises again. That rising oxygen then supports the recovery and diversification of animals, a feedback in which life helps restore the Earth system that supports life.

Arguing from Evidence · exemplarp. 20 · check for all 5 bullets

Claim: Changes in Earth's systems have had both negative and positive impacts on the evolution of life.

Negative: In the Permian extinction (252 mya), massive volcanic eruptions released methane and CO₂, causing rapid global warming and ocean acidification. About 90% of all species died, including most marine invertebrates and many land animals and plants. The oxygen graph shows a sharp drop in O₂ at that time.

Positive: Extinctions opened niches. In the millions of years after the Permian, new plants and animals evolved, including dinosaurs. After the K–Pg asteroid impact (66 mya) ended the dinosaurs, mammals took over their niches, and flowering plants increased. Without that, humans, who are mammals, might never have evolved.

Recovery and oxygen: Recovery begins with plants and phytoplankton. As they regrow and photosynthesize, oxygen rises again (the graph shows levels climbing after each extinction), which supports the return of complex animal life.

Reliability: The dates come from radiometric dating with isotopes whose ranges fit these ages, such as K-40 and U-238. But the oxygen graph has a wide uncertainty band, and the table notes that scientists are still working out the exact causes. The fossil record is incomplete because the rock cycle destroys rock and soft organisms rarely fossilize. So the timing is well supported, while some causes are best described as strong correlations.

Look and listen for: major extinctions include large changes in atmospheric composition · large percentages of organisms die off · afterward, different organisms increase and take over · recovery includes the regrowth of plant species and the resulting rise in oxygen.

Day 8 · Evaluate

Why Did It Take So Long?

What set of conditions allowed for the evolution of humans on Earth, and why did it take so long?

Revisiting the Performance Task handoutIndividual → table groupsIdea CarouselDriving Question Board

By the end of today students should know

  • It took ~3.5 billion years to go from simple cells to humans because life first had to change Earth's systems, mainly by adding oxygen, before complex life was possible.
  • When organisms change oxygen levels, it shows up in the geologic record: the first photosynthesizers come before banded iron, and cyanobacteria come before red beds.
  • When oxygen increases, more complex life follows: eukaryotes, then algae, then animals, then plants on land, then animals on land.
  • A planet that supports intelligent life would need a very unusual set of features and a long time.

Answer key

Performance task · exemplar explanationBased on the sample student work in the teacher materials

Claim: Earth's systems and life coevolved over 3.5 billion years, with oxygen playing the central role, and that is why intelligent life took so long.

When the first life appeared ~3.5 bya, the atmosphere was methane, ammonia, water vapor and nitrogen, and life was very simple cells. About 2.7 bya, stromatolites show microbes began to photosynthesize, turning CO₂ and water into glucose and oxygen (organism → Earth system #1). Oxygen began to rise; red bands of oxidized iron appear from 2.5 to 2 bya. Oxygen example 1: once O₂ rose, eukaryotic cells that use aerobic respiration appear (~2.1 bya).

About 1 bya, multicellular algae spread in the oceans and kept photosynthesizing (organism → Earth system #2). Banded iron formations reappear ~750 mya. Oxygen example 2: the jump in oxygen correlates with the first multicellular organisms (~600 mya) and soft animals (~560 mya), which need efficient aerobic respiration.

Oxygen example 3: plants moved onto land ~455 mya, built soil and raised O₂ toward 20–30%. Animals moved onto land (~425 mya), and in the oxygen-rich swamps of ~300 mya, insects grew huge.

Each jump in complexity came only after oxygen increased, and oxygen could only increase as photosynthetic life grew. Those parallel changes in Earth and living things took 3.5 billion years.

Look and listen for: ~3.5 billion years from single cells to humans · when organisms change oxygen, it shows up in the geologic record (photosynthesizers before banded iron, cyanobacteria before red beds) · when plants increase oxygen, more complex life appears (red beds → eukaryotes → multicellular; algae → animals; plants on land → animals on land; swamps → large animals; high O₂ in the last billion years → rapid evolution → humans) · any other planet that supports life would need a very unusual set of features.
