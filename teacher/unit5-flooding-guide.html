The phenomenon in one paragraph

Storms flood coasts, but the same storm can do very different damage. Students watch coastal flooding from Winter Storm Jonas (Jan 2016), king tides in Marin County, California, and Hurricane Sandy (Oct 2012), and the class floats the idea that the Moon and high tides matter. With a flashlight and a ball on a stick they model why we see phases (the Sun always lights half the Moon; from Earth we see different amounts of that lit half as the Moon orbits) and when eclipses happen (the same new/full geometry, but in a straight line). Then they graph real NOAA tide predictions for The Battery at three scales: two highs a day, spring and neap tides twice a month, and the biggest spring tides when a new or full Moon falls near perigee. A tidal-forces simulation gives the mechanism (gravity bulges; Sun and Moon pulling in line vs. at right angles; closer Moon pulls harder). Sandy's ~9.3 ft surge arrived near high tide on a full-Moon spring tide and pushed the water at The Battery to a record 14.06 ft above MLLW. Students finish by linking tides to sea-level rise and designing a flood-damage solution for the Performance Task.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain moon phases with the Sun-Earth-Moon model: the Sun always lights half the Moon; the Moon's position in its orbit sets how much of that lit half faces Earth. Phases are not Earth's shadow.
  • Name the phases in order and the cycle length (29.5 days, new to new).
  • Explain that solar eclipses happen at new moon and lunar eclipses at full moon, but only when the three bodies line up, because the Moon's orbit is tilted ~5°.
  • Describe tide patterns at three scales: ~2 highs and 2 lows a day; spring tides at new and full moon, neap tides at quarter moons; biggest spring tides when the Moon is near perigee.
  • Explain spring and neap tides with gravity: Sun and Moon pulling along the same line vs. at right angles.
  • Explain why a storm surge on a spring high tide floods more: storm tide = tide + surge (Sandy at The Battery).
  • Connect sea-level rise to more frequent high-tide flooding and design a solution with criteria, constraints, costs and benefits.

Corrections to the packet, teacher materials and unit plan

Teacher Materials, Explain 1 Look & Listen and Key Points: "Moon phases repeat every 28 days" and new/full moons "occur once every 28 days because that is how long it takes the moon to orbit the Earth." The phase cycle is 29.5 days. The Moon orbits once in 27.3 days (relative to the stars), but Earth moves along its own orbit meanwhile, so the Moon needs ~2 more days to line up with the Sun again. The student text in Explain 2 (Monthly Tidal Fluctuations) also says the Moon orbits in "a month (four weeks)."
Teacher Materials, Explain 1 Key Points: "Everyone on Earth experiences the new moon on the same day, just during local nighttime." A new moon is on the Sun's side of Earth, so it is up during the daytime (rising and setting with the Sun) and can't be seen at all. Full moons are the ones up all night.
Teacher Materials, Explore 1 Implementation Tip: "the height of the moon compared to the moon does change" is a typo. Read it as: the Moon's height compared to the Sun-Earth line (Earth's orbital plane) changes through the orbit, which is why phases usually aren't eclipses.
Teacher Materials, Explaining Eclipses step 2 ("sometimes it does become flat and in line with the sun") and the student Eclipses text ("sometimes its path becomes aligned with the path of the Earth around the sun"): The tilt stays about 5°. What changes is where the Moon is when it crosses Earth's orbital plane. Eclipses happen only when a new or full moon falls near one of those two crossing points, which happens in two "eclipse seasons" about six months apart.
Teacher Materials, Explain 1 Key Points: "Everyone on Earth can observe the lunar eclipse." Only the night side of Earth (where the Moon is up) can see it. Everyone who can see the Moon at that time sees the same eclipse.
Student Materials, Explore 1 Q6: "Look at the moon calendar again." The SY 26-27 packet has no moon calendar (it was the old Engage). Project one (see prep list).

Misconceptions to listen for

"Phases happen because Earth's shadow covers part of the Moon." That is a lunar eclipse, and only at full moon. Phases come from viewing angle. Ask: "At a crescent, where is Earth's shadow pointing?"
"The Moon makes its own light" / "the dark part isn't there." The Moon only reflects sunlight. The dark part is the night side.
"We get a solar eclipse every new moon." The ~5° tilt usually puts the Moon's shadow above or below Earth.
"Spring tides happen in spring." "Spring" means the water springs up. They happen at every new and full moon, all year.
"Only the full moon makes big tides." New moons do too; Sun and Moon pull along the same line either way.
"High tide happens only on the side facing the Moon." There are two bulges, so two highs a day.
"The full moon caused Hurricane Sandy." The Moon set the starting water level. The storm made the surge.
"The Sun's gravity is too weak to matter." The Sun's tidal effect is a little under half the Moon's; it is what makes spring vs. neap.
"Sea-level rise is too slow to matter." A few inches turns many ordinary high tides into flooding tides (high-tide flooding up 300–900%+).

Key vocabulary

Phase
How much of the Moon's sunlit half we can see from Earth.
Waxing / waning
Lit part growing (after new moon) / shrinking (after full moon). Seen from New York, waxing = lit on the right.
Crescent / gibbous
Less than half lit / more than half lit.
Solar / lunar eclipse
Moon blocks the Sun (new moon, in line) / Moon passes through Earth's shadow (full moon, in line).
Tide · tidal range
The regular rise and fall of sea level / height difference between high and low tide.
Tidal bulge
The two "humps" of ocean, toward and away from the Moon.
Spring / neap tide
Biggest range (new and full moon) / smallest range (quarter moons).
Perigee / apogee
Moon's closest / farthest point in its oval orbit.
Perigean spring tide (king tide)
Spring tide when the Moon is near perigee: the most extreme tides.
Storm surge · storm tide
Water pushed ashore by a storm / surge + tide, the total water level.
MLLW
Mean lower low water, the zero on tide charts.
Criteria · constraints
What a solution must do / limits it must work within (cost, space, time).

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, graph, cartoon or design sketch. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has: their group's model positions, their own tide graphs, the class consensus poster, a classmate's Rumors idea.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Eclipses and Eclipse ModelsExplain 1: Explaining Lunar Patterns · Eclipses (PDF pp. 101–102)

How does the path of the moon around the Earth change? (p. 101)

The Moon's orbit is tilted ~5° compared to Earth's path around the Sun, so the Moon is usually above or below the Sun-Earth line. The tilted path slowly turns, so the two points where it crosses Earth's orbital plane line up with the Sun about twice a year. Only then can a new or full moon make an eclipse. (Packet says the path "becomes aligned"; accept that idea but push for "crossing point lines up," not "goes flat.")

Annotate the Eclipse Models (p. 102)

Solar eclipse: Sun → Moon → Earth in a line (new moon). The Sun is ~400× wider than the Moon but ~400× farther, so the Moon can just cover it. The Moon's dark shadow (umbra) hits a narrow path: total there, partial in a wider area, nothing elsewhere. Lunar eclipse: Sun → Earth → Moon in a line (full moon). The whole Moon passes into Earth's shadow and often turns red-orange; everyone on the night side sees it.

Think-Talk-Open ExchangeExplain 1: Explaining Lunar Patterns · Think-Talk-Open Exchange · Think Q1–2 + Reflection (PDF p. 103)

Q1. New moons vs. solar eclipses (monthly vs. longer cycle; observer)

Similar: both happen when the Moon is between Earth and the Sun. Different: a new moon happens every 29.5 days because the Moon is only roughly between; a solar eclipse needs an exact line, which only happens in eclipse seasons (a few times a year at most). Observer: everyone sees the new moon on the same date; only people in the shadow path see the eclipse (total in a narrow strip).

Q2. Full moons vs. lunar eclipses

Similar: Earth is between the Sun and Moon. Different: full moon every 29.5 days; lunar eclipse only when exactly aligned, so rarer. Observer: anyone on Earth's night side sees the full moon and the eclipse at the same moment (different clock times).

Open response; look for an idea borrowed from a peer.

Take time for these key points (teacher materials, corrected): new moon: Moon on the Sun's side, Earth-facing side dark · new and full moons every 29.5 days (not 28) · everyone has the same phase on the same day · solar eclipse = new moon in exact line; only observers in line see it · full moon = Moon opposite the Sun · lunar eclipse = full moon in exact line; seen from the whole night side · other phases = partly lit views as the Moon orbits.

Construct explanations Q1–3Explain 2: Explaining Flooding · Construct explanations (2 questions) + Think (PDF pp. 115–116)

Construct explanations 1. How do moon phases affect high and low tides each month? (spring tide, cause; neap tide, cause)

A spring tide is the time of month with the highest high tides and lowest low tides (biggest range). It happens at new and full moon, when the Sun, Earth and Moon are in a line, so the Sun's and Moon's gravity stretch the oceans along the same line and the bulges add. A neap tide has the smallest range (lower highs, higher lows). It happens at first and third quarter, when the Sun's pull is at a right angle to the Moon's and partly cancels it. Spring tides come about every 14–15 days; in January 2025 at The Battery, AM highs were ~5.2–5.3 ft at spring tides vs. ~3.9 ft at neap.

Construct explanations 2. How does the Moon's distance affect tides over the year? (apogee, perigee)

The Moon's orbit is an oval. Perigee is its closest point; apogee its farthest. Closer means a stronger pull, so tides at perigee are more extreme; at apogee, weaker. The Moon reaches perigee about every 27.5 days, but whether that lines up with a new or full moon changes through the year. When perigee lines up with a spring tide, tides are the year's most extreme (2025: 6.21 ft new-moon high in April at ~357,000 km vs. 4.88 ft in December at ~403,000 km).

Think (brainstorm): combined effects of alignment + distance → Sandy (spring tide) and Marin (perigean spring tide)

Alignment + close Moon = biggest tides. Sandy hit on a full-moon spring tide, so the water was already higher than normal when the surge arrived. Marin flooded during a perigean spring tide, the most extreme tide, so even a modest storm push (or none) put water over roads. Storm surge adds on top of whatever the tide is.

Q1. Explain what caused the flooding in Hurricane Sandy or Marin County

Sandy: On Oct 29, 2012 the Moon was full, so the Sun, Earth and Moon were nearly in line and their pulls added: a spring tide. Sandy's winds and low pressure pushed a storm surge of about 9.3 ft toward New York Harbor, arriving near the evening high tide. Storm tide = tide + surge, so the water at The Battery hit a record 14.06 ft above MLLW, about 9 ft above a normal high tide. Marin: a perigean spring tide (full or new moon with the Moon near perigee) made extreme high tides, and storm water on top pushed the bay over roads and into homes. Sea-level rise means every tide now starts higher than in the past.

Q2. Why is this a problem we need to address?

Coastal floods damage homes, subways and power; they harm people, especially those who can't evacuate or afford repairs. Flooding is predictable (tides) and getting more frequent with sea-level rise, so the damage will grow unless we plan.

Q3. Design a solution (sketch/describe)

Sample: a raised waterfront park with a berm plus deployable gates at subway entrances, closed using NOAA tide and surge forecasts. Design elements: berm, gates, raised electrical equipment, a wetland edge.

Q4. Costs and benefits

Costs: high construction cost, years to build, gates must be staffed and maintained, may move water toward unprotected neighbors, temporary loss of park access. Benefits: blocks storm tides like Sandy's, protects transit and homes, park for daily use, wetlands absorb wave energy.

Q5. Constraints or limitations

Limited space, budget, must allow drainage and shipping, community approval, sea level will keep rising so the height must allow for the future; a surge bigger than the design height still floods.

Q6. Design element → how it addresses the mechanism
