Water 5E

How does precipitation form? How can we design solutions that protect communities from water damage?

Tap a day to open it. Each day has a minute-by-minute plan, Do Nows, conferring questions, checks for understanding, the answer key, exit slips and drawn homework. Student chapter: Unit 5: Water, which follows this 5E.

The phenomenon in one paragraph

In the Wind 5E students explained storm wind. Now they take on the second sub-problem of storm damage: water. Hurricane Helene (Sept 2024) flooded western North Carolina and set off landslides; Winter Storm Jonas (Jan 2016) buried the Mid-Atlantic and New York City in snow. Students ask what they'd need to know to design solutions, then figure out how precipitation forms. A fronts simulator shows that cold, dry air stays low while warm, moist air rises at the boundary, and precipitation forms there. Cup models show that warmer water puts more vapor into the air, and cooling that vapor turns it back to liquid faster. They scale up: air masses get their temperature and moisture from where they form (land/ocean, high/low latitude), the same rise-cool-condense pattern explains wet and dry latitudes, and it explains Jonas (Gulf/Atlantic moisture meeting Canadian cold air) and Helene (tropical moisture forced up the Blue Ridge Escarpment after earlier rain from a low-pressure system). Warmer oceans mean more evaporation, so more water for storms to drop. Finally they evaluate solar geoengineering as a way to cool the oceans, focusing on a new engineering idea: constraints and limitations.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain at the molecular level why warmer water evaporates more and why cooling vapor makes it condense (cup evidence).
  • Describe what happens at a front: denser cold, dry air stays low; less dense warm, moist air rises, cools, condenses into clouds and precipitation.
  • Name the four air masses (cT, mP, cP, mT) and predict their properties from where they form: ocean = moist, land = dry; low latitude = warm, high latitude = cold.
  • Use the same pattern at three scales (cups, local storms, global wet/dry latitudes) to explain precipitation (CCC Patterns).
  • Explain why Jonas dropped so much snow and why Helene dropped so much rain on the southern Appalachians.
  • Predict that warmer oceans → more evaporation → more water vapor → heavier precipitation in coastal storms (rain and snow).
  • Identify the criteria, constraints and limitations of a proposed solution and weigh its costs, benefits and unintended consequences (HS-ETS1-1).

Misconceptions to listen for

"Clouds are water vapor." Vapor is invisible gas. Clouds (and the fog in the cups) are tiny liquid drops or ice crystals that already condensed.
"Cold air holds the moisture, so cold air makes the snow." Cold air holds very little vapor. The moisture comes from the warm, moist air mass; the cold air forces it up and cools it.
"Warm air rises because heat rises." Warm air rises because it is less dense; denser cold air sinks under it and pushes it up.
"The drops on the top cup leaked through the plastic." The water evaporated from the bottom cup and condensed on the cooler top.
"Snow storms mean global warming isn't real." Warmer oceans put more water vapor into storms. If the air is still below freezing, that extra water falls as heavier snow.
"Hurricanes lose their rain when they hit land." They weaken, but they still carry huge amounts of moisture. Mountains can squeeze it out far inland (Helene).
"More rain = only a flooding problem." Rain also causes landslides, power outages and road washouts; snow causes roof collapse and travel shutdowns.
"Constraints and criteria are the same thing." Criteria = what the solution must do. Constraints = limits it must work within (money, time, materials, physics, laws).

Key vocabulary

Precipitation
Water falling from clouds: rain, snow, sleet, hail.
Evaporation
Liquid water turning into invisible water vapor (gas). Faster when the water is warmer.
Condensation
Water vapor turning back into liquid drops when it cools. How clouds form.
Air mass
A huge body of air with about the same temperature and moisture throughout; it takes on the traits of where it formed.
cT · mP · cP · mT
Continental = dry (land); maritime = moist (ocean); tropical = warm; polar = cold.
Front
The boundary where two air masses meet: cold, warm, occluded, stationary.
Orographic lift
Air forced up a mountain slope; it cools and drops its moisture (Helene and the Blue Ridge Escarpment).
Ocean heat content
The total heat energy stored in the ocean; rising over recent decades.
Geoengineering
Changing Earth's climate system on purpose, at large scale (e.g., reflecting sunlight).
Constraint / limitation
A limit a design must work within / something the design can't do.

Connection to the Performance Task

The unit task asks students to break "storm damage" into sub-problems and design a solution with at least five design elements. The Wind 5E taught criteria and tradeoffs; this 5E adds constraints and limitations; the Flooding 5E adds design elements. Day 7's PTO page, Revisiting the Performance Task: Water, is the evidence for SEP 1 (Asking Questions and Defining Problems), ETS1.A and the CCC Influence of Science, Engineering, and Technology on Society and the Natural World. Remind students: a strong final design names the mechanism it acts on (here: warm, moist air rising and condensing), not just the damage.

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Why 7 days? The teacher materials say 5–7. At 50 minutes the cup lab, the Idea Carousel and the Class Consensus Discussion each need real time, and Day 7 includes a full PTO page.
  • Five-day version (the low end of the teacher materials' range): Day 1 Engage + Model 1; Day 2 Model 2 + Making Sense (rubric as homework); Day 3 Explain Part 1 (wet/dry latitude questions as homework) + Part 2 individual responses; Day 4 short Idea Carousel (one rotation) + Class Consensus, Summary Task as homework; Day 5 Elaborate Q1–2 + Think-Talk (20 min) and Evaluate (geoengineering text + PTO), with the Domino Discover tally and DQB at the start of the Flooding 5E.
  • Six-day version: merge Days 4 and 5 by assigning the Part 2 individual responses as Day 3 homework.
  • Do Now / Exit Slip boxes. The L2 Google Doc has none. Use a half-sheet or notebook page.
  • Period length. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • Device-light option. The fronts simulator and all three videos can run from one projector while groups record.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, storyboard, cross-section or comic. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class has: their cup observations, their Rumors post-its, their group's poster, the class tally.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Precipitation Damage and Fronts

How does storm precipitation cause damage? How does precipitation form?

Handout: Precipitation DamageHandout: Precipitation Formation Investigation, Model 1Independent → pairsRumorsVideo: How Hurricane Helene Spawned Record Landslides in NCWeather Fronts Simulator

By the end of today students should know

  • Storm water (rain and snow) is its own damage sub-problem: floods, landslides, power outages, collapsed roofs.

Cup Lab and Making Sense

How does water behave in the air, and how does temperature change that?

Handout: Precipitation Formation Investigation, Model 2Handout: Making Sense of the Precipitation Formation InvestigationHandout: Precipitation Formation Investigation RubricTable groupsDomino DiscoverCups · hot/warm/cold water · ice · magnifier

By the end of today students should know

  • Warm water evaporates into invisible vapor; it condenses back into drops on a cooler surface.
  • Hotter water → more evaporation → more vapor in the air.
  • Cooling vapor (ice on top) makes it condense faster. Warm air holds more water than cold air.
  • This is the same thing happening at a front: warm, moist air rises into cold air and condenses.

  4. 16–24

    Hot, warm, cold side by side. Start this while recording Experiment 1. Confer.

  5. 24–32

    Experiment 3: air temperature

    Two hot cups, ice on one top cup, wait 2–3 min, dry the spot, compare. Diagram both.

  6. 32–42

    Individually on Making Sense. Push the molecular "Think" rows.

Q1. What does this tell you about the relationship between water in the air and the temperature of the water beneath?

The warmer the water below, the more water vapor enters the air above it. Evidence: the hot-water cup produced the most fog and drops, the cold cup the least.

Q2. Does cooling water vapor increase the rate at which it becomes a liquid?

Yes. With the same hot water below, the top cup with ice collected more drops in the same 2–3 minutes than the one without ice.

Q3. Does air hold water more effectively at high or low temperatures?

High. Warm air carried vapor up from the hot water; where the air was cooled (under the ice) the vapor came out as liquid. (The amount of vapor air can hold rises steeply with temperature: ~12 hPa at 10 °C vs. ~42 hPa at 30 °C.)

Look and listen for (teacher materials): when the warm air reaches the cooler top, water droplets form · moisture condenses when cooled · warmer water → more evaporation, "maybe warmer waters cause more rain" · when air is cooler, vapor turns to liquid.

Day 3 · Explain

Air Masses, Local and Global

How do different air masses meet to cause precipitation?

Handout: Explaining Precipitation Formation, Part 1PairsAir-mass map · global circulation diagram

By the end of today students should know

  • An air mass takes on the temperature and moisture of where it forms: maritime (ocean) = moist, continental (land) = dry, tropical = warm, polar = cold.
  • When a cold, dry air mass meets a warm, moist one, the warm air is forced up, cools, condenses into clouds and precipitation.
  • The same pattern works globally: rising air at ~0° and ~60° makes wet belts; sinking air at ~30° and the poles makes dry belts.

Part 1 · Patterns on Local Scales · Q1 Storyboard of Storm Formationcold dry meets warm moist
Q1. Cause-and-effect storyboard: how clouds form and why precipitation happens when a cold dry air mass meets a warm moist air mass

(1) A warm, moist air mass (lots of fast, spread-out vapor molecules) meets a cold, dry air mass at a front. (2) The cold air is denser, so it stays low and wedges under; the less dense warm air is forced up. (3) As it rises it cools; vapor molecules lose energy and slow down. (4) Slowed molecules stick together as tiny drops or ice crystals: a cloud (condensation). (5) Drops/crystals grow until they're too heavy and fall as precipitation; as snow if the air is at or below 32 °F. Annotations should link each box to the cups (bottom cup = warm moist source, ice = cold air).

Part 1 · Patterns on Global Scales · Q2–Q3wet and dry latitudes
Q2. Why do the 'Wet' latitudes tend to experience a great deal of precipitation?

At ~0° (equator) and ~60° air converges and rises (low pressure). At the equator strong heating makes warm, moist ocean air rise; near 60° warm air from the south meets cold polar air and is forced up (the polar front, where mid-latitude storms like Jonas form). Rising air cools and its vapor condenses, the same thing as in the cups and the fronts simulator.

Q3. Why do the 'Dry' latitudes tend to experience very little precipitation?

At ~30° and at the poles air sinks (high pressure). Sinking air warms, so its vapor doesn't condense and clouds don't form. Near the poles the air is so cold it holds very little vapor to begin with. Deserts line up near 30°.

Integrating three dimensions (CCC Patterns): warm, moist air meeting cold, dry air → condensation → precipitation, at every scale: cups, local fronts, global belts.

Explaining Precipitation Formation · Part 2 · Jonas prompt1st prompt · words + model
Where did the warm moist and cold dry air masses that created Jonas come from, and why did their interactions produce massive snowfall?

The warm, moist air (maritime tropical) came from the Gulf of Mexico and the warm Atlantic off the Southeast coast; the storm's low formed in the Gulf and moved up the coast, pulling that moisture north. The cold, dry air (continental polar) came from Canada and was sitting over the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast. The denser cold air stayed at the surface, so the warm, moist air was forced up over it for a long time, cooled and condensed into heavy clouds. Because the air below was at or below 32 °F, the precipitation fell as snow, and the large supply of ocean moisture made it a lot of snow (27.5 in at Central Park, 30.5 in at JFK, NWS). Model: cross-section with the cold wedge, warm air sliding up, cloud and snow.

Explaining Precipitation Formation · Part 2 · Helene prompt2nd prompt · words + model
How did air rising around the Blue Ridge Escarpment and the low pressure system in the region combine with Hurricane Helene to produce massive amounts of rain?

Before Helene arrived, a low-pressure system over the region was already lifting moist air and producing heavy rain, so soils and rivers were full. Then Helene's huge supply of warm, moist tropical air was pushed northward into western North Carolina. When that air hit the steep Blue Ridge Escarpment, it was forced up the slope (orographic lift), cooled and condensed, wringing out rain over the same mountains again and again. Low pressure + mountains lifting + a very moist air mass = extreme rain (peak 30.78 in at Busick, NC, NHC). Soaked, steep ground → flash floods and landslides. Model: a slope with arrows of moist air climbing it, a cloud and rain on the windward side, and labels for "already wet ground."

Key point: no cold, dry air mass is needed here. Lifting is done by the low-pressure system and by the mountains. The common thread is still warm, moist air rising and cooling.

Summary Task · content Q1–2"Explain what you know…" · words + model
Content Q1. Explain how the overall patterns of heating on Earth's surface result in predictable patterns of seasonal weather and precipitation.

The equator is heated most and the poles least. Uneven heating makes air rise where it's warm (equator, low pressure, wet) and sink where it's cooler (~30°, high pressure, dry), and sets up global winds that carry air masses. Warm, moist air masses form over tropical oceans; cold, dry ones over high-latitude land. Where they meet (mid-latitudes, especially in winter when the north is cold) we get fronts, storms and precipitation; in winter it is cold enough to fall as snow. As seasons shift, the strongest heating and the storm belts shift too, so the same places get rainy or snowy seasons each year. (Packet wording is missing "how"; see overview.)

Content Q2. Describe how precipitation formation at the local and global scales follows similar patterns.

At every scale, warm, moist air rises (or is lifted), cools, and its vapor condenses into clouds and precipitation. Small scale: hot water → vapor → drops on the cold top cup. Local: at a front, cold, dry air lifts warm, moist air (Jonas); mountains lift moist air (Helene). Global: rising air at ~0° and ~60° gives wet belts; sinking air at ~30° and the poles gives dry belts.

Take Time for These Key Points (teacher materials): precipitation can occur when warm, moist air meets cold, dry air · warm, moist air masses usually form around tropical oceans · cold, dry air often forms over continents farther from the equator · global winds move air masses · the pattern holds at small, local and global scales · hurricanes can make extreme precipitation without meeting cold, dry air.

Design cycle: identifies the problem and its mechanism · describes a solution aimed at that mechanism · analyzes limitations and constraints. Proficient = all; Developing = some. Most common gap: naming damage without the mechanism, or listing costs without any constraints.

Exit slip options

Option A

Yes, No or Maybe on geoengineering? Give your strongest piece of evidence.

Option B

Name one constraint and one limitation of geoengineering, and say which is which.

Constraint: hardware doesn't exist / scale / no rules. Limitation: doesn't remove CO₂ / only ~15% hurricane weakening.

Solution Flowchart

  • Scissors placed at the right step
