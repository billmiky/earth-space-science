The phenomenon in one paragraph

In August 1883 Krakatoa (Indonesia) erupted; about two-thirds of the island collapsed into a caldera roughly 300 m deep, and the tsunamis killed about 36,000 people. Temporary ash islands came and went. In 1927 a new island, Anak Krakatau, broke the surface at 9 m and grew to 338 m by 2018. Where did that new material come from? Nobody can dig there (the deepest hole ever drilled reached 12.3 km of Earth's 6,371 km radius), so students "listen" to earthquakes. Seismic-wave arrival patterns show a P-wave shadow zone (about 104°–140°) and no S waves beyond 104°, so Earth has a liquid outer core and an inner core. A density column shows materials stack by density, and gravity pulls toward Earth's center. Rock samples and magnetism show the layers' composition: silica-rich rock in the crust, Mg/Fe-rich rock in the mantle, and iron–nickel in the core, with a moving, flipping magnetic field that points to flowing liquid metal. Krakatoa's new islands are andesite, dacite and basalt, which match the crust and upper mantle, not the core. In the Elaborate, students explain how heat, density and water make magma that rises through weak crust and crystallizes into igneous rock. The Evaluate asks who is most at risk from volcanic hazards.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Describe seismic waves as energy from an earthquake moving matter: P waves push–pull in the direction of travel; S waves shake side to side.
  • Explain why S waves only travel through solids (bonded particles drag their neighbors sideways) while P waves travel through solids and liquids.
  • Use the patterns in arrival times (the steady trend, the 104°–140° P shadow zone, the late "jump," no S waves past 104°, two P-wave reflections) as evidence for a solid crust and mantle, a liquid outer core, and a separate inner core.
  • Explain that layers stack by density because denser materials have more mass in the same space, so gravity (pulling toward Earth's center) has a greater effect on them.
  • Use samples, the Common Materials table and magnetism to name each layer's composition, and explain why a moving, reversing magnetic field means moving liquid metal (iron–nickel outer core).
  • Argue from composition that Krakatoa's new islands came from melted crust and upper-mantle rock, not the core.
  • Explain how heat (lower density), water (lower melting point) and weak crust let magma rise and erupt, and how cooling forms igneous rock in the order of Bowen's reaction series.
  • Use eruption frequency, VEI and deaths to start explaining who is most at risk from volcanic hazards (HS-ESS3-1).

Corrections to the packet and teacher guide

Packet p. 9, density column steps 1–2: students pour "the alcohol, water, and oil," in that order. Alcohol and water mix when they touch, so they get one blended layer plus oil, not three layers. Have groups pour water, then oil, then alcohol (alcohol gently down the side). For step 4 ("a different order"), only swap water and oil. Food coloring is water-based, so it colors the water and alcohol, not the oil.
Packet p. 12, Density and Gravity text: says "the heavier something is, the stronger this pull is… these heavier more dense materials sink." Students hear "heavy things sink." Restate it: same volume, more mass → gravity pulls more on that space, so it sinks. A heavy log floats; a light pebble sinks.
Packet p. 23, Common Materials table, "Magnetism" column: labels liquid iron–nickel "magnetic" and solid iron–nickel "not magnetic." Neither core layer is a permanent magnet. Both are far above iron's Curie point (~770 °C). Earth's field is generated by flowing, electrically conducting liquid iron in the outer core (the geodynamo). Tell students: "The moving liquid metal makes the field," not "liquid iron is magnetic."
Packet p. 26: "Materials like silica, iron, and magnesium are molten hot in the mantle, in a liquid called magma" makes the whole mantle sound liquid. The mantle is solid (S waves cross it). It flows only very slowly, and magma forms only in places where rock partly melts. Reword it as "in parts of the upper mantle." Iron and magnesium travel inside silicate magma; they are not separate molten metals.
Packet p. 4, Changing Islands See-Think-Wonder rows: the prompts ("Where do the largest earthquakes occur? Where do the most explosive volcanoes erupt?") are left over from the Unit Opening maps. The Krakatoa images can't answer them. Have students use the rows for what changed on the islands, and save the earthquake/volcano questions for the DQB.
Teacher guide, Explore 1 Part 1 (both years): refers to a Seismic Waves Inside the Earth handout. Students use packet pp. 6–7 (What's Inside the Earth?, "Looking at a seismic model").
Teacher guide, Explain 2 key point (both years): "New islands form from materials… in what we call the 'mantle' layer." Say crust and upper mantle. The Common Materials table puts andesite and basalt in the crust, and peridotite where the mantle meets the crust.
Unit plan "semi-solid mantle": keep "solid mantle" in the class model, as the DCI and the packet's rubric do.
Packet p. 32, deaths table: the Andes show only 84 volcanic deaths since 1900. The 1985 eruption of Nevado del Ruiz in the Colombian Andes killed about 23,000 people, so the region as drawn must leave out Colombia. Use it as a data-quality discussion, and don't let students conclude the Andes are "low risk."
Region naming: Krakatoa is in Indonesia (maritime Southeast Asia), not "Mainland Southeast Asia," the Key 5 region with 0 deaths and almost no eruptions. Students often conflate the two.
Small numbering quirks: the Explain 1 Summary Task is numbered 5–6 (p. 14) and the Explain 2 Summary Task is numbered 5 (p. 24). There are no questions 1–4 missing. The packet has Do Now/Exit Slip boxes for only Classes 1–6, but this 5E runs 9 days. Use the back of the packet for Classes 7–9.

Misconceptions to listen for

"Lava comes from the liquid outer core." The outer core is liquid metal about 2,900 km down and far too dense to rise. Magma is melted rock from the crust and upper mantle. The teacher guide flags this as the key misconception for Explain 2.
"The mantle is a sea of magma." It's solid (S waves cross it). Melting happens only where it's hot enough, where water lowers the melting point, or where pressure drops.
"Gravity pulls everything down." Gravity pulls toward Earth's center. "Down" in Australia points the opposite way from "down" in New York.
"Heavier things sink." A big piece of wood floats; a tiny pebble sinks. It's density (mass in a given space), not total weight.
"S waves stop because they run out of energy." They stop because liquid particles aren't bonded, so a side-to-side shake can't pass along.
"The inner core is liquid because it's the hottest." Pressure keeps it solid even though it's hottest.
"Earth is a giant bar magnet with a chunk of magnetized iron inside." It behaves like one at the surface, but the field comes from moving liquid metal. That's why it drifts and flips.
"Seismic waves are the ground cracking." They are energy moving matter: particles push or shake their neighbors and pass the energy along.
"Scientists drilled to the mantle/core to know this." Deepest hole: 12.3 km. The model comes from indirect evidence: waves, density, meteorites, magnetism and lab experiments.

Key vocabulary

Seismic wave
Energy released by an earthquake moving through Earth's matter.
P wave (primary)
Push–pull (compressional) wave; fastest; travels through solids and liquids.
S wave (secondary)
Side-to-side (shear) wave; slower; travels through solids only.
Seismometer / seismogram
The instrument that records ground motion / the recording it makes.
Shadow zone
Region on the far side of Earth where direct waves don't arrive. For P waves, ~104°–140°.
Density
Mass per unit volume (g/cm³). Denser materials sink below less dense ones.
Crust · mantle · outer core · inner core
Earth's layers from the outside in: solid, solid, liquid, solid.
Magnetic field / polarity
The region where a magnet's force acts / which way north and south point. Earth's polarity has reversed many times.
Magma / lava
Melted rock underground / melted rock that has reached the surface.
Igneous rock
Rock formed when magma or lava cools and crystallizes. Intrusive (slow, big crystals) or extrusive (fast, tiny crystals).
Felsic · mafic · ultramafic
Rich in silica, K and Na (light, less dense) → rich in Fe and Mg (dark, denser).
Bowen's reaction series
The order minerals crystallize from cooling magma: olivine first (hottest), quartz last.
VEI
Volcanic Explosivity Index, 0–8. Each step is about 10× more erupted material.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, cross-section, comic or map. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (or colored pencils) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task uses something only your class has: their own lab measurements, the earthquake they picked in the viewer, their group's Domino Discover wondering, or the class consensus chart.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one part of their drawing out loud.

Reading Earthquake Waves

How do seismic waves reach different locations on Earth at different times?

Packet pp. 5–7 (Q1–2)PairsGlobal Seismogram ViewerSeismic Waves modelDomino DiscoverDevices

By the end of today students should know

  • Seismic waves arrive at nearby stations first and far stations later, in an almost straight trend, until about 104°.
  • Past ~104° the pattern breaks: there's a gap with no direct P waves (to ~140°), then P waves arrive later than the trend predicts.
  • In the Seismic Waves model, P waves travel through the whole Earth but bend and slow in the middle; S waves stop at the middle.
  • The same patterns appear for every earthquake, so they're telling us about Earth, not about one earthquake.

Answer key

Global Seismogram Viewer See-Think-Wonderp. 5 · "Near East Coast of Honshu, Japan, Mag 9.1"
See

The earthquake isn't felt at the same time everywhere. Arrival time increases steadily with distance, in an almost straight trend, until about 104°. Then there's a gap, and beyond ~140° the waves arrive later than the trend predicts: the line "jumps." The same pattern appears for every earthquake.

Collecting Observational Data, Q1–2p. 7 · Seismic Waves model · shadow zone view
1. Sketch the motion of the P and S waves over time.

Sketch should show both waves spreading out from the quake through the mantle. P (faster) is ahead of S. When P reaches the middle it bends and continues to the far side, leaving a gap (shadow zone) on the surface. S stops at the edge of the middle and never reaches the far side.

2. Describe differences and similarities in how the P and S waves move.

Similar: both start at the earthquake and spread outward in all directions through the outer part of Earth. Both curve slightly as they go deeper. Different: P waves are faster (the gap between them keeps growing). P waves pass through the center but are bent, slowed and broken up. S waves don't pass through the center at all.

How Do Materials Form Layers?

What could make waves behave differently inside Earth? How do different materials form layers?

Packet p. 7 Q3, pp. 8–9Pairs → table groupsSeismic Waves model (standard waves)Density column labGoggles

By the end of today students should know

  • In the standard-waves view, part of the P wave reflects twice in the middle of Earth, so there are two boundaries there.
  • Mass of a liquid alone = (cylinder + liquid) − (empty cylinder); density = mass ÷ volume (g/mL = g/cm³).
  • Liquids settle in the same order every time: least dense on top, most dense on the bottom.
  • Earth might be layered by density the same way.

Minute by minute 50 min

  1. 0–4

    Do Now

    Check 3–4 mini seismogram drawings.

  2. 4–13

    p. 7 Q3: standard waves (pairs)

    Switch from shadow zone to standard waves. Watch only the leading edge of the P wave. Count how many times part of it bounces back. Tell students not to get lost in all the reflected lines. The goal is "two bounces = two layers in the middle."

P Waves, S Waves and Gravity

What do differences in seismic waves detected around the world tell us about Earth's interior?

Packet pp. 10–13Table groupsDomino DiscoverHuman earthquake modelVideo: Demonstrating P and S waves (backup)

By the end of today students should know

  • Seismic waves are energy from an earthquake moving matter (CCC 5).
  • P waves push particles forward and back in the direction of travel, so they pass through solids and liquids: unbonded particles still bump the next one.
  • S waves shake particles side to side, which only passes along when particles are bonded (solids). So S waves stopping at ~2,900 km means the layer below is liquid.

What's Different about P and S Waves? See-Think-Wonder + Q2–3pp. 11–12
P waves: motion · materials · what about their structure explains this

Particles push and pull straight ahead, in the direction the wave travels. P waves move through solids and liquids (and gases). Even unbonded particles push on the next one, so the push still passes along.

S waves: motion · materials · what about their structure explains this

Particles move side to side, across the direction of travel. S waves move only through solids. Particles must be bonded so each one drags its neighbor sideways; in a liquid they slide past each other.

3. How does the human earthquake model help make sense of the computational model?

In the computer model, S waves stopped at the middle of Earth but P waves got through. The human model shows S waves can't pass through unbonded (liquid) particles while P waves can. So the layer where S waves stop must be liquid, and the outer part of Earth, where both waves travel, is solid.

Answer key

Part 1: Magnetism and Earth Q1–2p. 22
1. What does the reversal of polarity make you think could be happening inside Earth?

The material that makes Earth's magnetism is moving and changing. Earth's magnetic field has flipped direction many times, which a solid, fixed magnet wouldn't do.

2. What properties would the layer that produces these effects have?

It would be metal (iron) to make a magnetic field, and liquid so it can flow and change: the layer S waves can't cross, the outer core.

Where did the new islands in Krakatoa come from?p. 24

The islands are andesite, dacite and basalt, which are high in silica, oxygen, iron and magnesium. Those match the crust (silicic rocks, andesite, basalt) and the upper mantle where it meets the crust (Mg/Fe-rich). They do not match the iron–nickel core. So the material came from melted rock in the crust and upper mantle that rose and erupted.

Key misconception (teacher guide): don't let anyone leave thinking lava comes from the liquid outer core. Magma is melted rock from the upper layers. How the rock melted is Day 8.
Summary Task Q5p. 24
5. How did the additional empirical evidence and patterns help strengthen your model, including relationships between components?

Seismic waves showed where the layers are and which are liquid. The new evidence showed what they're made of. Rock samples show silica-rich rock at the surface and Mg/Fe rock deeper. Day 8 · Elaborate

How Do Materials Move to Create New Islands?

The crust and mantle are solid rock. How did that rock get out of the ground and build Anak Krakatau?

Packet pp. 25–29 (Q1–10)Table groupsESSRT pp. 11, 13, 14Consensus-Building ShareRed pens

By the end of today students should know

  • Temperature and density both increase with depth.
  • When rock heats up it expands (same mass, more space), so its density decreases and it rises.
  • Magma forms in parts of the upper mantle. It erupts where water lowers the melting point and where crust is weak or thin.
  • Magma that cools and crystallizes forms igneous rock. Minerals crystallize in order (Bowen's reaction series): Fe/Mg-rich, dense minerals first at high temperature; silica-rich, less dense minerals last. That sorts materials between and within layers.

Q10 · Bowen's and layeringp. 29

As magma cools, minerals crystallize in order. High-temperature minerals (olivine, pyroxene) form first; they're rich in iron and magnesium and denser, so they sink and stay deeper. Low-temperature minerals (feldspar, quartz) form last; they're silica-rich and less dense, so they end up higher. Between layers: the mantle is Mg/Fe-rich peridotite, and the crust is silica-rich granite and basalt. Within a layer: the crust itself varies, with denser basalt making ocean crust and lighter granite making continental crust, and magma bodies sort as they cool.

Look and listen for (teacher guide): heat from Earth's interior melts solid mantle and crust rock into magma · hot magma is less dense and rises, pushing on the crust · as it rises it cools, and rocks solidify at different temperatures · that differential solidification makes different rock types at different levels of the crust.
