Energy and Matter

Where is all the energy coming from to move plates apart? What processes inside Earth cause it to reach the surface? And why do people keep living in these hazardous areas?

PEs HS-ESS2-3, HS-ESS2-1 Length 6 days × 50 min (curriculum says 5–6) Routines Rumors · Domino Discover · Class Consensus · Read-Generate-Sort-Solve
1Engage 2Explore 3Explain 4Explain 5Elaborate 6Evaluate

Tap a day to open it. Each day has a minute-by-minute plan, Do Nows, conferring questions, checks for understanding, the answer key, exit slips and drawn homework. Student-facing version: Unit 3: Energy and Matter chapter (good for absent students and as a virtual stand-in for the Explore lab).

The phenomenon in one paragraph

Africa's Great Rift Valley is where the African continent is slowly pulling apart. It has frequent earthquakes and active volcanoes, and it is also the cradle of humanity: people have lived there for more than 200,000 years. In the last two 5Es students built a layered model of Earth and connected plate boundaries to hazards, but they still have no mechanism: what moves the plates, and why is Earth's inside so hot? Three heating-and-cooling models (oil on a hot plate, hot and cold colored water, oil over candles with floating plastic "plates") show that heated matter becomes less dense and rises, cooled matter becomes denser and sinks, and the loop can drag plates apart and push them together. Students map Model 3 onto Earth, then use a short reading and an evidence table to find the heat source: residual heat from Earth's formation plus radioactive decay in rock (not fusion, not burning). Finally they ask why people stay: the same energy moves matter to the surface as fertile volcanic soil, metal and gem deposits, hot springs, fossil fuels and coastlines. Students add this to their performance-task model of who is most at risk.

By the end of the 5E, every student should be able to…

  • Explain that heating makes matter expand and become less dense, so it is pushed up; cooling makes it denser, so gravity pulls it down. A loop of rising and sinking matter is a convection current.
  • Map Model 3 onto Earth: candles = Earth's internal heat, oil = mantle, plastic pieces = lithospheric plates, gap = ridge/rift (divergent), pile-up = trench (convergent).
  • Use evidence to rule out chemical reactions (no free O₂) and nuclear fusion (needs ≥ 4 million °C and gas/liquid) and identify radioactive decay plus residual heat as Earth's heat sources.
  • Describe two convection systems: mantle convection moves plates; outer-core convection in liquid iron generates the magnetic field.
  • Explain the Great Rift Valley: rising hot mantle stretches and cracks the African Plate, making rift valleys, volcanoes and earthquakes.
  • Use the conservation idea: energy is not created or used up. It moves from the interior to the surface and space, and it drives the cycling of matter (CCC 5).
  • Argue that plate boundaries carry risks and resources (soil, minerals, energy, water, trade), and use that to explain why populations live there.

Corrections to the packet and teacher guide

"Where does the heat come from?" (p. 13) says 44 trillion joules per second is "about six times as much energy as the United States consumes per year." It mixes a rate (J/s) with a yearly total. Over a year, Earth releases ~1.4 × 10²¹ J. The U.S. uses ~1 × 10²⁰ J a year (EIA, 2023). So it is about 14 times U.S. use. The student chapter says "many times faster."
Fusion row (p. 13): "A suitable ion density is 4.9833887e-13 g/ml." This number has false precision and isn't a real fusion threshold (the Sun's core is ~150 g/mL). Tell students to rely on the two rows that matter: fusion needs ≥ 4 million °C and gas or liquid.
Table 1 directions (p. 5) say "draw the beaker of water." Model 1 is oil. Say so before students draw.
Reading (p. 21): "One type of volcanic ash, called andisol." Andisol is a soil that forms from volcanic ash. It isn't the ash itself.
Part 2 (p. 14): "The energy dissipated by the solid inner core drives convection in the liquid outer core." This is oversimplified. Outer-core convection is driven mainly by heat leaving the core into the mantle, plus heat and light elements released as the inner core slowly freezes. Accept packet-based answers, but don't teach the inner core as the heat source.
Teacher guide, Explain: "metal becomes magnetized when it is in motion." Better: moving liquid iron is an electrical conductor, and its motion makes electric currents that generate the magnetic field (the geodynamo). Iron hotter than ~770 °C can't stay permanently magnetized, so the core can't be a giant bar magnet.
Packet Explain diagram temperatures (mantle 2,800–5,000 °C) are too high for the upper mantle. The mantle runs from ~1,300 °C at the base of the plates to ~3,500–4,000 °C at the core. Keep the packet's numbers on the diagram, but don't quiz them.
Reading (p. 13): residual heat is "radiating primarily from the core." Heat leaves the core mainly by conduction and convection. Fine to say "flowing outward."
Energy and Matter Explanation Rubric (p. 26): the Stability & Change "Developing" cell repeats the Proficient text. Use: "Argument is missing rates, or rates are inaccurate or not connected to risk."
Teacher guide, Elaborate: "up until about 1500, most societies were living along tectonically active areas." That's overstated. Many large early populations lived on river floodplains (Nile, Indus, Yellow and Yangtze Rivers). Say "many" and let the data speak.
Teacher guide typos (still in SY 26-27): "CCC #2 Energy and Matter" (Class Consensus sidebar) → CCC #5. The Explore launch sentence "as you go deeper into the Earth, the temperature" should end with increases. The Engage says people lived here "100,000 years," but the reading says 200,000. Use more than 200,000. The Explain look-for says residual heat is "radiating out of the crust"; it flows from the core through the mantle and out through the crust. (SY 26-27 already fixed "heard" → heated and the Summary tip that mentioned natural selection.)

Misconceptions to listen for

"The mantle is a sea of liquid magma, and the plates float on it." The mantle is almost all solid rock that flows very slowly, like extremely thick putty, over millions of years. Magma forms only in small zones such as under rifts and above sinking slabs. The oil in the models is a stand-in. Name that limitation.
"Heat rises." Heat itself doesn't rise; hot, less dense matter is pushed up by denser matter that gravity pulls down. Push for density and gravity in every explanation.
"Earth is hot because of fusion, like the Sun" or "rocks are burning down there." The evidence table rules both out.
"All the heat comes from the core." Radioactive decay happens throughout the crust and mantle, and it's not uniform, because rocks aren't evenly distributed.
"The energy gets used up." Energy isn't created or destroyed. It moves from the interior to the surface and out to space, and it moves matter along the way.
"Convection only pushes; plates just ride along." At trenches, cold, dense slabs sink and pull the plate (slab pull). That is the sinking half of the same loop.
"People live there because they don't know it's dangerous." People weigh real benefits (soil, minerals, water, trade) against risk, and many have no other choice. Connect this to vulnerability from the PTO.
"The Rift isn't near an ocean, so it's not a plate boundary." It's a continental divergent boundary at an early stage. The Red Sea and Gulf of Aden show a later stage.

Key vocabulary

Density
Mass per volume. Heating makes the same mass take up more space, so density goes down.
Convection current
A loop where heated, less dense matter rises, then cools, becomes denser and sinks.
Lithosphere
The rigid crust plus top of the mantle, broken into plates.
Mantle
Hot, solid rock layer that flows very slowly; its convection moves the plates.
Outer core
Liquid iron and nickel; its convection generates Earth's magnetic field.
Residual heat
Heat left over from Earth's formation (collisions, sinking iron).
Radioactive decay
Unstable isotopes (U, Th, K) break down on their own and release energy.
Divergent boundary / rift
Plates move apart; crust stretches, cracks and drops into valleys.
Slab pull
A cold, dense plate edge sinks at a trench and drags the plate behind it.
Hydrothermal fluid
Hot water circulating in rock; it dissolves and concentrates metals into deposits.
Natural resource
Something from Earth that people use: soil, water, minerals, energy.
Vulnerability
How likely a population is to be harmed by a hazard (from earlier in the unit).

Pacing and logistics notes

  • Period length. Every plan assumes 50 minutes. For 45-minute periods, cut the Do Now to 3 minutes and move the item marked flex to homework.
  • Five-day version. Combine Days 1 and 2: show the video and run a short Rumors (15 min), skip separate predictions, and do predictions at each station just before lighting or heating. Then move See-Think-Wonder to the start of Day 3.
  • Lab stations. With one set of each model, run three stations and rotate groups about every 7 minutes. With materials for every group, have each group run all three in order (Model 2 first: it needs no heat-up time).
  • No lab possible? Use the Model 1–3 videos from the teacher bundle, or the animated models in the student chapter. Label them as models for students.
  • Summary Task (end of Explain) can be an exit ticket or homework. Read it before Day 5. It's the curriculum's main individual check on the mechanism.

How the homework stays AI-proof

Each night's homework is a hand-drawn model: a diagram, cross-section, comic or map. Four rules make it hard to outsource:

  • Paper and pencil (colored pencils welcome) only. No printed or digitally made images.
  • Every task references something only your class saw: their own Model 3 observation, the Rumors idea they heard, their group's starred idea.
  • Every drawing has one personal "I chose…" annotation explaining a decision the student made.
  • 30-second check the next day. During the Do Now, pick 3–4 students to explain one arrow on their drawing out loud.

Modeling Earth's Interior Processes

How do differences in temperature affect matter inside Earth, and how could that lead to the movement of matter in the Tonga eruption?

Packet pp. 4–10: Tables 1–3 Observations + See-Think-Wonder (p. 10)Investigation Rubric (SY 26-27)Table groupsDomino DiscoverLab: hot plate, candles, goggles

By the end of today students should know

  • Matter next to an energy source rises; matter that moves away and cools, or starts cold, sinks.
  • The rising and sinking make a loop (a convection current) that carries energy from the bottom toward the top.
  • In Model 3, the loop moved the plastic "plates" apart above the heat and pushed them into others at the edges.

Minute by minute 50 min

  1. 0–4

    Do Now

    Call on 3–4 students to explain one arrow on their Rift drawing.

  2. 4–8

    Launch + safety

    "Last time we asked whether the energy is coming from the core or the mantle, and how it rises so far. Today you'll model layers of material with heat added and look for patterns in how matter moves."

    Goggles on. Roles: timer, heat manager, recorder, materials. Hot plate low–medium. Nobody leaves a flame.

  3. 8–30

    Models 1–3

    Model 2 first: water must be completely still. Add 3–4 blue ice cubes on one side, then gently lay the bottle of hot red water on its side at the other end. Model 1: oregano in oil, beaker on the hot plate, observe after ~30–45 s. Model 3: 4 plastic pieces touching in the middle of the oil, 3–4 lit candles in a row under the center, observe after ~30 s. Blow out the candles before moving on. Students fill in the Observations columns. Confer.

  4. 30–38

    See-Think-Wonder (individual)

    Three rows: matter rising, matter sinking, anything else, plus how this could explain the plate motion behind the Tonga volcano.

  5. 38–45

    Domino Discover

    Each group picks one important idea from its See-Think-Wonder. Groups share in turn, each building on the last. Chart the ideas. Answer key

Tables 1–3: Observationspp. 5, 7, 9 · what students should see if the setup works
Model 1 (oil + oregano on hot plate)

Energy is greatest at the bottom. Oregano near the hot plate rises up the middle, spreads across the top, then sinks down the cooler sides and gets carried back to the bottom: a loop.

Model 2 (hot red, cold blue water)

Energy is greatest in the hot red water. The red spreads out across the top. The blue meltwater sinks to the bottom and spreads along the floor. Over time the colors mix and the motion slows as temperatures even out.

Model 3 (candles + plastic plates)

Energy is greatest in the middle of the bottom, over the candles. Oil and oregano rise over the candles and spread outward at the surface. The two middle plastic pieces move apart, opening a gap. Outer pieces get pushed toward the edges and into each other or the dish wall.

If a model "fails": Model 2 mixes instantly if the table is bumped. Model 3 needs enough oil (≥ 1 cm) and a central row of candles. Use the teacher-bundle video or the chapter animation to show the expected pattern, and label it as a model.

Day 3 · Explain (Part 1)

Models, Density, and Earth's Heat

How does plate motion occur, and where does the heat come from?

Packet pp. 11–14: Part 1, The Role of Density Q1–3, Where does the heat come from? + Q4Table groupsRed/blue pencils

By the end of today students should know

  • Candles = Earth's internal heat; oil = mantle; plastic = plates; gap = ridge or rift; pile-up = trench.
  • Heating spreads particles apart: same mass, more volume, lower density. That is why hot matter rises.
  • Earth releases ~44 trillion J every second. About 20–50% is residual heat from formation, and most of the rest is radioactive decay. Fusion and chemical burning are ruled out by evidence.

Minute by minute 50 min

  1. 0–5

    Do Now

  2. 5–9

    Launch

    Review the Domino Discover chart. "Today we'll bring those ideas together to explain what inside Earth is moving the plates."

  3. 9–17

    Part 1: match the models (p. 11)

    Table groups annotate the side-by-side images: find Earth's matches for the tea candles, oil and floating plastic, plus the gap and the edges.

  4. 17–20

    Pause: review the matches

    Cold call 3 groups. Name the limitation: "The real mantle is solid rock that flows very slowly, not a liquid."

  5. 20–28

    The Role of Density, Q1–2 (pp. 11–12)

    Recall the density column. Students draw particles after heating: the same number of particles, spread farther apart. Then add red rising and blue sinking arrows to a preliminary convection sketch.

  6. 28–33

    Density Q3: annotate the Earth model (p. 12)

    Students annotate how energy and matter move in the mantle to drive plate motion. Then ask: "Why do you think the center of Earth is so hot?" Take 2–3 answers without judging.

  7. 33–45

    Where does the heat come from? (pp. 12–14: reading, table, Q4)

    Read the text in groups. Then test each process in the table against what they know about Earth's interior and answer Q4 with evidence. Confer (prompts below). Tell students to ignore the "ion density" number (see corrections).

  8. 45–50

    Review key information + exit slip

    Draw out two points: some heat is residual from Earth's formation flowing out from the core, and the rest is from radioactive decay. Ask the fist-to-five confidence question again.

Option A · Model 3 recall

Draw Model 3 from the side. Add arrows showing how the oil moved.

  • Does less dense matter rise or sink? What pushes it?
  • Did we see any evidence of oxygen gas inside Earth?Rules out chemical reactions.
  • Do we have evidence that Earth is hot enough for nuclear fusion? What's the hottest temperature on the diagram?
  • Which type of reaction could be making heat in rock?

The Role of Density, Q1–2pp. 11–12
1. How do layers of different substances form?

Substances settle by density. Denser materials sink to the bottom and less dense ones float on top. That is why Earth has a dense iron core and lighter rock above.

2. How does a change in temperature affect density? (draw particles after heating)

When heated, particles move faster and spread farther apart, so the same mass takes up more volume and density decreases. Drawing: the same number of particles, spaced farther apart (often with motion lines). Watch for students who draw more or bigger particles.

Density Q3: annotate the Earth modelp. 12 · what a complete annotation includes
  • Heat flowing from the core into the lower mantle (red arrows outward)
  • Heated mantle rock expands → less dense → rises beneath the ridge or rift
  • Rock spreads sideways under the plates, dragging them apart at the ridge
  • Rock cools → denser → sinks, especially as cold slabs at trenches (slab pull)
  • A complete loop, with "gravity" labeled on the sinking side
Q4: Which process is most likely responsible for heat in the mantle?p. 14 · claim + evidence

Radioactive decay. Chemical reactions (like burning) need free oxygen gas, but inside Earth oxygen is bonded into minerals. Nuclear fusion needs at least 4 million °C and light elements as gas or liquid, but Earth's interior is at most ~6,000 °C and mostly solid. Radioactive decay happens spontaneously in rocks, and the crust and mantle are rock. Together with 20–50% residual heat from Earth's formation, decay supplies the rest of the heat.

Real-data extension: in 2011 the KamLAND detector in Japan measured geoneutrinos (particles released by uranium and thorium decay) coming from inside Earth. They account for ~20 TW of Earth's ~44 TW of heat flow.

Explaining the Great Rift Valley

How does energy inside Earth drive the motion of the plates at the surface?

By the end of today students should know

Summary Taskpp. 16–17 · reflection is personal; Q1–5 sample answers
Reflection (went well / improve / who helped / my idea)

No key. Read for honesty and specifics. Use it to plan the next consensus discussion.

1. What causes the motion of matter within Earth's interior?

Temperature differences cause density differences. Heated mantle rock expands, becomes less dense and is forced up. Cooler, denser rock is pulled down by gravity. The result is convection currents.

2. How does energy within Earth's interior drive the cycling of matter?

Energy from residual heat and radioactive decay heats mantle rock, which rises and carries the energy toward the surface. Near the top it gives off heat, cools, becomes denser and sinks, then is reheated. The same matter keeps cycling as long as energy flows outward.

3. How was energy transferred from Earth's interior to the surface in the Tonga eruption?

Mantle convection moves the plates. At the Tonga Trench the cold, dense Pacific Plate sinks under the plate to its west. As the slab goes down, it releases water that helps melt the hot mantle above it, making magma. The magma is less dense, so it rises, carrying thermal energy up to the volcano. In 2022 that energy was released in an explosive eruption (made more violent where magma met seawater). It sent energy outward as a pressure wave in the atmosphere and as tsunami waves in the ocean.

4. How does energy drive the movement of matter within and between systems?

Energy flows from hot to cold. Where it heats matter, the matter becomes less dense and moves (rises); where matter loses energy, it becomes denser and sinks. Energy moving from the core to the mantle, the plates, the surface and the atmosphere/ocean moves matter in each system: mantle rock, plates, magma, ash and water. Energy isn't created or destroyed; it is transferred.

5. How did you use multiple lines of evidence for the Great Rift Valley?

Strong answers name at least three: the maps (earthquakes and volcanoes along the rift = divergent boundary); the physical models (heat → rising → plates moved apart); the density reasoning; the reading and process table (radioactive decay + residual heat); and seismic and interior evidence from Earth's Interior.

Generate: ideas students should pull from the readingpp. 19, 21–22 · chart paper
  • Every living person traces back to a population in the region ~200,000 years ago.
  • Hot springs and hydrothermal vents may have let early human ancestors cook food ~1.8 million years ago, before fire, giving more calories for bigger brains (a hypothesis).

Solve: How has resource availability guided the development of human populations and societies?p. 20 · sample group answer

Energy from Earth's interior drives plate motion, and plate motion moves matter to the surface. That creates resources: fertile volcanic soil for farming, concentrated metals and gems for tools and trade, hot springs and fossil fuels for energy, and varied landscapes and coastlines for food, water and trade. People settled, grew and built societies where these resources were. Resource wealth also drew outside powers, leading to colonial exploitation in the Great Rift Valley and continuing demand for cobalt today. People live in these hazardous areas because the benefits are large, though the risk is real.

Look and listen for: plate boundaries have beneficial resources · hot fluids concentrate trace elements into precious metals and gems · tectonic areas have diverse landscapes · volcanic soil is fertile · tectonic areas are often near coasts or water.
